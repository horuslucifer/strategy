# 13. Julian Stafford Corbett — Some Principles of Maritime Strategy

## 1. Identity

A book of theory written by a barrister for admirals, published in London and New York by Longmans, Green and Co. in 1911, four years before the Dardanelles and three before the war that would test every claim in it.

Julian Stafford Corbett (12 November 1854 – 21 September 1922) was not a naval officer and had not been a soldier. Marlborough, then Trinity College, Cambridge, first-class honours in law, called to the bar at the Middle Temple in 1877, practising until 1882. Then a decade as a novelist of Elizabethan historical fiction and an occasional war correspondent for the *Pall Mall Gazette*. He came to naval history at about forty, through John Knox Laughton and the Navy Records Society, editing documents on the Spanish war of 1585–87. In 1902 he began lecturing full-time at the Royal Naval College, Greenwich, on the new War Course; when Fisher moved the course to Portsmouth it became the Royal Naval War College, and Corbett's lectures went with it. He was not a bystander there. From 1905 he functioned as the Admiralty's unofficial strategic adviser, he wrote the theoretical introduction ("Some Principles of Naval Warfare") to the Admiralty's War Plans of 1907, he was secretary of the Historical Section of the Committee of Imperial Defence, he took the Chesney Gold Medal in 1914 and a knighthood in 1917, and he was chosen to write the official history, *Naval Operations*. He died in 1922 on completing its third volume.

**The text.** An Introduction on the use and limits of theory, then three parts. Part I, Theory of War, six chapters: the theory of war; natures of wars, offensive and defensive; natures of wars, limited and unlimited; limited war and maritime empires; wars of intervention; conditions of strength in limited war. Part II, Theory of Naval War, three chapters: theory of the object (command of the sea); theory of the means (the constitution of fleets); theory of the method (concentration and dispersal of force). Part III, Conduct of Naval War, four chapters covering the inherent differences between war on land and at sea, the typical forms of naval operations, methods of securing command (decision and blockade), methods of disputing command (the fleet in being, minor counter-attacks), and methods of exercising command (defence against invasion, attack and defence of trade, attack and support of military expeditions). Then an appendix and an index.

**Edition used.** Project Gutenberg eBook 15076, prepared by Suzanne Shell, Keith Edkins and the PG Online Distributed Proofreading Team, cached at `sources/corbett_maritime.txt`. English original; no translation question arises. **Correcting the blog.**

*The blog says* Corbett was a "civilian historian and lecturer at the Royal Naval War College (not a naval officer)." *The evidence says* the first half is right and the second half understates his position, *because* "lecturer" describes a man who talks to a class, and Corbett was inside the machine: the Admiralty's unofficial strategic adviser from 1905, author of the theoretical introduction to the Admiralty's own War Plans of 1907, secretary of the CID's Historical Section, and later the officially appointed writer of the Royal Navy's war history. The distinction matters for how the book reads. It is not an outsider's commentary on strategy; it is the theory a government was using, written up for publication by the man who had been supplying it, which is also why serving officers resented it.

*The blog says* Corbett gave us "the army as a projectile fired by the navy." *The evidence says* that phrase is not in the book. The word "projectile" does not occur anywhere in the cached text — a `grep -F projectile` over the whole file returns nothing, appendix included. The line "the British Army should be a projectile to be fired by the British Navy" is attributed to Sir Edward Grey and is quoted from Fisher's *Memories* (1919). It is a Fisherite slogan, and attaching it to Corbett inverts him. Corbett's formulation is that a maritime state possesses "a larger strategy which regards the fleet and army as one weapon," and that the fleet's value is partly "the fear of what the fleet makes it possible for your army to do." He also devotes the last pages of Part III to insisting that combined expeditions be commanded jointly by two commanders-in-chief precisely so that neither service can subordinate the other. A projectile does not negotiate with the gun.

*The blog says* the book "contrasts with Mahan on decisive battle." *The evidence says* the contrast is real but cruder than the book. Corbett denies that the enemy's battle fleet is always the primary objective, and he treats "seek out the enemy's fleet and destroy it" as a maxim that has to be kept on a leash. But he cites Mahan approvingly as "a weighty critical authority" on what concentration means (*War of 1812*, i, 316) and quotes Mahan's *Lessons of the War with Spain* to convict the Americans of their 1898 error, and he says flatly that all natures of war alike "demand the use of battles." He is against the universality of the decisive-battle rule, not against battle. The contemporary attack on him, by Spenser Wilkinson, lumped Mahan with Philip Colomb, Cyprian Bridge and Reginald Custance as the settled orthodoxy Corbett was accused of throwing away — which is a better guide to what the quarrel actually was.

*The blog says* Corbett "builds on Clausewitz." *The evidence says* yes, and he names his text: "The references are to Colonel Graham's translation of the third German edition, but his wording is not always followed exactly." So Corbett is working from J. J. Graham's 1873 English *On War* and silently improving it, which is worth knowing before treating his Clausewitz quotations as Clausewitz's words.

## 2. The world the text assumes

An island state with the largest navy, the largest merchant marine, the largest financial market and a small army, whose wealth arrives by sea and whose enemies are continental powers with large conscript armies it cannot hope to beat on land. That is the whole frame. Almost every asymmetry Corbett exploits comes from it.

He assumes a world in which the sea is a barrier as well as a road, so that possessions across it can be cut off from the power that owns them. He assumes fleets fast and self-directing enough to be dispersed and still cohere — he writes ten years into wireless telegraphy and speculates about what it will do to the value of a supporting ship in a cruiser line. He assumes coal, not oil, and therefore fuel as the one real limit on a steamer's freedom of movement. He assumes the right of private capture at sea still exists in law and argues at length that it must be kept, because without it a maritime power has no means of pressure at all; the Declaration of Paris of 1856 had already abolished privateering, and the Declaration of London of 1909 was live politics as he wrote. He assumes finance is a theatre of war: "When other things are equal, it is the longer purse that wins."

Technologically he is writing at the moment when the flotilla acquired battle power. The locomotive torpedo means a cheap boat can kill a capital ship, and Corbett says so: it is "a feature of naval warfare that is entirely new," it has torn the old principles of cruiser design "to shreds," and it has turned the hardest naval problem from how to attack with a battle fleet into how to defend one. The submarine he treats as unproved and says so — "the unproved value of submarines only deepens the mist which overhangs the next naval war." That is an honest 1911 sentence and it dates the book precisely.

Politically he assumes a cabinet, a staff and a diplomatic service that must agree with each other, and he treats the friction between them as structural rather than pathological: "the deflection of strategy by politics... It is usually regarded as a disease. It is really a vital factor in every strategical problem." He assumes limited wars are still possible between great powers, that a belligerent may rationally quit when the cost exceeds the value of the object, and that the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05 is the modern proof. Three years later the assumption that Britain could fight its war the British way, limited and maritime, collapsed on the Western Front. That collapse is the single largest fact standing between this book and anyone reading it now.

Psychologically he assumes professional officers trained on maxims, an admiralty culture that had "deified the battle idea," and a public that will demand its trade be protected whatever the staff college says. He treats all three as data.

## 3. Core doctrine

**1. Theory cannot make a commander; it makes minds interoperable.**
*Locus:* Introduction.
*His reasoning:* theory "does not pretend to give the power of conduct in the field; it claims no more than to increase the effective power of conduct." Its real work is upstream of action: it lets a chief's order "awake in every brain the same process of thought," and it gives officers the "verbal apparatus" to argue with ministers at a council table instead of dumbly acquiescing in bad plans.
*Limits he attaches:* it is "a question of education and deliberation, and not of execution at all." Theory determines the normal; the greatest successes have been departures from the normal, made with open eyes.

**2. The first and largest question is what kind of war this is.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 1.
*His reasoning:* a staff asked for a war plan must answer with a question — what will the war be about, and how much does the object mean to each side? War is a continuation of policy, so the method must follow the political object, not the fashion set by the last victorious general.
*Limits:* he quotes Clausewitz that this is "the first and the most far-reaching of all strategical questions," and warns that at close range accidental factors always distort the view.

**3. Naval strategy is a subordinate branch of maritime strategy, which is itself a branch of the theory of war.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 1; Green Pamphlet, opening.
*His reasoning:* "it is almost impossible that a war can be decided by naval action alone." Unaided sea pressure works only by exhaustion, slowly, and irritates neutrals and one's own merchants into accepting inconclusive peace. Men live on land. So the paramount question of maritime strategy is the relation of army to navy in the plan, and only when that is settled can naval strategy begin.
*Limits:* "except in the rarest cases." He allows purely maritime wars — the Dutch wars approximately were.

**4. Offence and defence are complementary, not alternatives; and defence is a position the enemy must break, plus a counter-stroke.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 2; Green Pamphlet, "General Characteristics of the Defensive" (cached lines 8219, 8224).
*His reasoning:* no offensive can be pushed to the full without defence on all but the main line, and no defence survives without counter-attack, so the honest distinction is between a positive object (taking something) and a negative one (denying something) rather than between attack and defence. Within that, "counter-attack is the soul of defence" — the Green Pamphlet puts it as "True Defensive means waiting for a chance to strike" and "The strength and the essence of the defensive is the counter-stroke." A defensive position that can be turned or bypassed has no strength at all: "its elements of strength entirely disappear" unless the enemy must break it down to reach his object. Where defence is well designed, surprise passes to the defender, who is on familiar ground and can lay traps.
*Limits:* he keeps the old classification anyway, because it forces the analysis of the relative advantages of attack and defence, "the corner stone of strategical study." And prolonged defence rots the offensive spirit. "Anything that is not activity is not war." Submitting to blockade merely to occupy a superior enemy is "almost wholly evil" if kept up.

**5. The operative classification is limited versus unlimited war, and it turns on what the object is worth, not what it is.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 3.
*His reasoning:* Clausewitz's late discovery, which he never finished writing up. The value each side sets on the object determines the force each will spend and the point at which each will quit. Jomini reached the same place by a different road.
*Limits:* Corbett says the distinction is nearly useless for continental warfare between contiguous states, where it collapses into a difference of degree.

**6. A genuinely limited object must be both politically minor and physically isolable; a war can therefore be limited physically as well as morally.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 4, including its closing paragraph.
*His reasoning:* "Firstly, it must be not merely limited in area, but of really limited political importance; and secondly, it must be so situated as to be strategically isolated or to be capable of being reduced to practical isolation by strategical operations." Fail the first and the enemy will spend without limit to keep it; fail the second and he can ignore your object entirely and strike at your heart. This is Corbett's extension beyond Clausewitz: the sea can be made "an insuperable physical obstacle to the whole national force being brought to bear," so a war may be limited "by the strategical isolation of the object, as well as morally by its comparative unimportance."
*Limits:* this is why Clausewitz's own examples — Silesia, Saxony, Belgium, Alsace-Lorraine — are not real examples. They are organic parts of the enemy's country, with no obstacle between. And the physical limitation only exists where geography and the naval balance permit; otherwise the distinction has no purchase at all.

**7. Limited war is therefore permanently available only across water, and only to the side that commands it.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 4.
*His reasoning:* if the belligerents share a frontier, the stronger can escalate at will; a weak neutral in between will simply be violated. So "limited war is only permanently possible to island Powers or between Powers which are separated by sea, and then only when the Power desiring limited war is able to command the sea" both to isolate the object and to make invasion of the homeland impossible. This, he says, is the explanation of the expansion of England, and Clausewitz died without seeing that he had found it.
*Limits:* isolation "can never be established until we have entirely overthrown the enemy's naval forces" — the precondition is expensive and may not be met.

**8. Limited war runs in three phases: seize, hold, press.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 6, on the Russo-Japanese War.
*His reasoning:* first an offensive movement to occupy the territorial object before the enemy can gather; then a defensive phase forcing an attenuated, exhausting offensive on him; then renewed pressure until he prefers the new situation to the cost of breaking it. Jomini's formula: push the offensive "according to circumstances and your relative force in order to obtain the cession desired."
*Limits:* the phases overlap and never appear cleanly. Japan, he argues, lost its grip on the scheme in phase two — starved the reduction of Port Arthur in its eagerness to hit the Russian field army, and paid in time, lives and a lost opportunity.

**9. The limited form gives you strategic offence with minor-strategic defence — the strongest combination available.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 6.
*His reasoning:* Moltke held the strongest form of war to be strategic offensive with tactical defensive. Limited war produces it: "our major strategy is offensive and our minor strategy is defensive." You take the object first, which gives you the initiative; the enemy must then attack you on ground you chose, at the end of a long and exhausting line.
*Limits:* it presupposes you can get there first, by "superior readiness or mobility or by being more conveniently situated."

**10. Isolation lets you choose how much force you will have to fight.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 6.
*His reasoning:* the force you must beat is not the enemy's whole force but only what he can and will bring. That depends on how much the object is worth to him, how preoccupied he is elsewhere, and how bad his communications are — the last of which you can make worse. "In favourable circumstances therefore (and here lies the great value of the limited form) we are able to control the amount of force we shall have to encounter."
*Limits:* only where naval action can do the isolating.

**11. "War limited by contingent" is a method, not a form of war; and the value of a small intervention is the threat, not the performance.**
*Locus:* Part I, ch. 5.
*His reasoning:* Clausewitz could not place the practice of sending an allied contingent and left it as an embarrassment to his theory. Corbett's answer: give the contingent either no reservation at all, or else a definite territorial object, an independent organisation and an independent function. British success came when the theatre was independent and touching the sea — Portugal, the Crimea. Failure came when the contingent was merely auxiliary to somebody else's army. Wellington's own account is the mechanism: "it is our maritime superiority gives me the power of maintaining my army while the enemy are unable to do so." Where the force is too small for positive results, it still earns its keep by threat: the positive results of British coastal expeditions were usually negligible and historians despise them, but every great continental soldier feared them, because they threatened results unless strongly met. "Its value lay in its power of containing force greater than its own." After Walcheren in 1809 Napoleon ordered a scheme for 300,000 National Guards to defend the French coasts, writing that 30,000 British in transports at the Downs could paralyse 300,000 of his army.
*Limits:* where no truly limited theatre exists, the choice narrows to placing a contingent frankly under the ally's command or confining yourself to coastal diversion. And a threat that is never executed decays — "in war to ignore a threat is too often to create an opportunity," but the opportunity must be created or waited for, and the British were often "not ready or not bold enough" to take the first one. Once Britain declared its line of operations by concentrating in the Peninsula, "the threat ceased."

**12. The object of naval war is to secure command or to deny it; the normal state is an uncommanded sea, and command exists only in degrees.**
*Locus:* Part II, ch. 1; Green Pamphlet, "Various Conditions of Command" and "Command in Dispute."
*His reasoning:* the commonest error in naval speculation is assuming that if one side loses command the other gains it. "The most common situation in naval war is that neither side has the command; that the normal position is not a commanded sea, but an uncommanded sea." Disputed command is precisely the condition naval strategy exists to handle; when command is settled, naval strategy ends. And what is won is never absolute: command is general or local, permanent or temporary, and even permanent general command cannot stop sporadic raiding — a transport was taken between Cork and Portsmouth after Quiberon. "By general and permanent control we do not mean that the enemy can do nothing, but that he cannot interfere... so seriously as to affect the issue of the war."
*Limits:* the state of dispute favours the weaker only until the preponderant side forces a decision, which is why the preponderant side should seek one quickly. And he gibbets the maxim "the sea is all one" when it is stretched into a doctrine that no battalion may move until the enemy's fleet is destroyed. The Americans in 1898 nearly lost their campaign to that caution; the Japanese in 1904 sailed anyway, content to deny effective control rather than hold it.

**13. Command of the sea is control of communications, not conquest of territory.**
*Locus:* Part II, ch. 1; Green Pamphlet, "Naval Strategy Considered as a Question of Passage and Communication."
*His reasoning:* you cannot own the sea, cannot exclude neutrals from it, cannot feed an army off it. "The only right we or our enemy can have on the sea is the right of passage." So the analogy with conquest is false, and "command of the sea, therefore, means nothing but the control of maritime communications." Maritime communications are not an army's supply line but the arteries of national life, which is why interfering with them is economic pressure and why, unlike on land, it starts from the first day rather than after victory.
*Limits:* he insists the whole structure depends on the surviving legal right of private capture at sea; abolish it and "the object for which we fight battles almost ceases to exist."

**14. Sea communications are largely common to both sides; therefore offence and defence merge.**
*Locus:* Part II, ch. 1; Green Pamphlet, "The Peculiarity of Maritime Communications."
*His reasoning:* on land the two sides' lines run in opposite directions, so striking his exposes yours. At sea the great lines run parallel or are identical, so "we cannot defend our own without striking at the enemy's." The dispositions that protect your trade are the same dispositions that threaten his, and the places where trade converges are where fleets meet.
*Limits:* it is the controlling peculiarity of maritime war and, he says, the source of most of the errors made by importing land maxims wholesale.

**15. Separate the exercise of control from the security of control, and resource each.**
*Locus:* Part II, ch. 2.
*His reasoning:* "On cruisers depends our exercise of control; on the battle-fleet depends the security of control." Numerous cheap units do the actual work of controlling communications; the expensive concentrated force exists to stop them being interfered with. Battleships cannot exercise control — too few, too specialised; cruisers can, if unopposed.
*Limits:* the dilemma is permanent. Every cruiser attached to the battle fleet as eyes is one withdrawn from control, and the correct ratio can only be settled case by case. Nelson resolved it by starving his fleet of scouts, and it cost him contact once.

**16. Concentration is coherent dispersal about a strategical centre, not massing.**
*Locus:* Part II, ch. 3.
*His reasoning:* mass ends concealment and ends flexibility, so "the further from the formation of the ultimate mass we can stop the process of concentration the better designed it will be." Concentration is "a continual conflict between cohesion and reach," and the adjustment of those two tensions is most of practical strategy. Combinations require division; "if we risk nothing, we shall seldom perform anything." Worked correctly, the deployment conceals your intention, covers every objective the enemy might choose, and turns each detachment into bait: "the ideal concentration, in short, is an appearance of weakness that covers a reality of strength."
*Limits:* "Division is bad only when it is pushed beyond the limits of well-knit deployment" — no part should be placed where it cannot fall back on the centre. And massing is bred in peace: "the idea of massing, as a virtue in itself, is bred in peace and not in war."

**17. Three conditions make sea war unlike land war, and the chief casualty is the maxim "seek out the enemy's fleet."**
*Locus:* Part III, ch. 1 and ch. 2, §1; Green Pamphlet, "Final Note" (cached lines 8808–10) and the 1909 revision (cached line 9665).
*His reasoning:* first, the enemy can withdraw his fleet into a defended port and "remove his fleet from the board altogether," so the maxim about the enemy's main force being the primary objective may have nothing to attack. Second, there are no roads and no obstacles at sea, so you cannot predict his line of operation and are much likelier to miss him. Third, fleets must protect trade, so effort cannot be concentrated purely on the enemy's force; purists who deny this are ignoring the facts of war. The maxim's real content, therefore, is: get contact in the best position for a decision, as soon as the rest of the plan allows. Taken nakedly it means conforming to the enemy's movements and being led wherever he likes — the American error of 1898, when Sampson was sent to Puerto Rico and uncovered his own army's line of passage. Togo did the opposite and waited for the Baltic fleet to come to him. Corbett's replacement wording: "Seek contact with the enemy's fleet in the most certain and favourable manner that is open to you."
*Limits:* the maxims are not wrong, only conditional; he keeps all three and puts them under judgment. And the Final Note's two cautions stand — if you seek him out with superior force you will probably find him somewhere you cannot destroy him, and since the defensive is the stronger form "it is *prima facie* better strategy to make the enemy come to you than to go to him and seek a decision on his own ground."

**18. A fleet in being is a fleet kept actively alive, not one kept safe.**
*Locus:* Part III, ch. 3, §1.
*His reasoning:* naval defence means "keeping the fleet actively in being — not merely in existence, but in active and vigorous life." Its instrument is mobility, not entrenchment, because a fleet sitting in port leaves the ulterior object — the communications — wholly open, whereas an army in position at least covers territory and exhausts its attacker. Torrington's 1690 design is the model: refuse the decision, keep contact, retire towards reinforcement, and keep a port of retreat. Kempenfelt's rule for inferiority is the sharpest statement of the positive side: "if a squadron cannot be formed sufficient to face the enemy's at home, it would be more advantageous to let your inferiority be still greater in order by it to gain the superiority elsewhere."
*Limits:* it cannot by itself produce a positive result, and the moral risk is severe. Prolonged defensive policy, Corbett says of France, "cursed her when she was weak, and cursed her when she was strong," breeding an incapacity to strike when the strength finally came.

**19. Minor counter-attack promises more than it delivers.**
*Locus:* Part III, ch. 3, §2.
*His reasoning:* the weaker side is always drawn to the cheap disabling blow, and the torpedo made it newly plausible, but the only modern case — the Japanese strike at Port Arthur — was abnormal in every particular and still achieved very little materially. He traces the same arc for fireships: decisive once in 1588, then steadily discredited as the defence learned. Locating the target precisely is the hard part, and even a feeble alert defence disrupts the attack.
*Limits:* he says plainly that the submarine is unproved and that he cannot calculate its effect, and he grants that the moral effect of such attacks at the start of a war will be considerable.

**20. In exercising command the objective changes: against trade, defend where attack is easiest; against invasion, attack the transports and not the escort.**
*Locus:* Part III, ch. 4, §§1–3.
*His reasoning:* the maxim that the fleet's objective is always the enemy's fleet is true for securing command and false for exercising it. Commerce is vulnerable only where it converges — at terminals and focal points — and those areas are few and narrow and can be held in strength; out on the open routes attack is possible but so diffuse as to be ineffective, and convoy handles it. Hence "facility of attack means facility of defence," and whoever makes commerce destruction his primary object has thereby confessed inferiority. Against an expedition the object is the army in the water. Admirals' orders said so in plain technical language — "the transports of the enemy are to be your principal object" — and Keith's orders against Napoleon's flotilla told him to go for the loaded vessels rather than their protectors and to lose sight "entirely of the possibility of idle censure for avoiding contact with an armed force."
*Limits:* raiding squadrons could and did intrude on the trade system; it contained them rather than excluding them, and could only be broken by a superior battle fleet. And the escort must still be turned or contained, and treated as a primary objective only if neither is practicable.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

This is the text on the list that was written for the weaker party, and it says so without inference being needed.

Directly. Chapter 6 of Part I is titled "Conditions of Strength in Limited War" and opens by saying that as correct defence lets an inferior force win against a superior, so "the correct use of the limited form of war has enabled a weak military Power to attain success against a much stronger one, and these instances are too numerous to permit us to regard the results as accidental." He says the conquest of Canada is "the leading case of a weak military Power succeeding by the use of the limited form of war in forcing its will upon a strong one." He says of Japan in 1904 that she had no such preponderance as Clausewitz required for the higher form and had no business attempting it. He says that if the doctrine of overthrow were universally true, "it would never be possible for a weaker Power to make successful war against a stronger one in any cause whatever — a conclusion abundantly refuted by historical experience." The Green Pamphlet states it as a bare rule: the offensive "should be adopted by the stronger Power," the defensive "is adopted by the weaker Power"; and "if you are not relatively strong enough to assume the offensive, assume the defensive till you become so."

His mechanisms for the weak are five, and all of them are in the text.

*Reduce the size of the fight.* Do not try to beat the whole of him; beat only what he can and will bring to the object. You raise that difficulty deliberately by your choice of object and by your conduct of the opening moves.

*Choose an object he will abandon.* Not just a small one — a cheap one. The measure is what it is worth to him relative to the cost of taking it back.

*Take it first, then make him attack you.* Seize the object before he can gather; then you hold the initiative while fighting defensively, and he exhausts himself at the end of a long line.

*Refuse the decision.* Kempenfelt's rule and Torrington's design: stay in contact, stay mobile, deny him the general command he needs, keep a line of retreat, and hit only when he exposes himself. Nelson's own version, which Corbett quotes: an inferior fleet handled with skill can expect that "some one time in twenty-four hours you must be able to attack a part of a large fleet."

*Be worth containing.* The intervention that cannot win can still lock up a force larger than itself, so long as the threat is credible and undeclared. The moment you declare where you are going, the threat stops working.

And two warnings for the weak, which he states as sharply as the mechanisms.

The defensive corrodes. Used long, it destroys the will to strike, and the weak party who lives in it ends up unable to take the offensive even when the balance has turned. That is his verdict on the French navy across a century.

And the whole structure has a precondition. Corbett's weak actor is weak in one dimension and strong in another. Britain had the small army and the largest navy and the longest purse. Japan in 1904 had a competent state, an army, a geographic advantage and the Anglo-Japanese Treaty isolating the quarrel. Limited war is not a method for those who are weak everywhere. It is a method for converting strength in one medium into leverage over strength in another, and it fails outright when the barrier is absent. That reading is mine, but it is a reading of what he says rather than an extension of it: he states the condition twice, in Part I chapters 4 and 6, and says that where the condition is absent the distinction between limited and unlimited war has no practical content at all.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Corbett's preparation is conceptual, not material: the purpose of peacetime study is to determine "the normal," so that departures from it are made deliberately, and to give a staff a shared vocabulary so that a plan survives contact with a council table. He is scathing about ideas bred in peacetime — massing "as a virtue in itself" is one — and about administrations that fail to spend the winter getting the fleet ready to be first in the field in spring.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** He treats intelligence as structurally unreliable at sea. The sea gives no roads and no tracks, so locating the enemy is the recurring problem and the risk of missing him is the standing check on offensive movement. Monk and Rupert divided their fleet in 1666 on a false report of a French squadron that did not exist. The Japanese at Port Arthur sent two of five destroyer divisions to Dalny on a report that was wrong. Of all intelligence, he says, "the most difficult to obtain in war is the distribution of an enemy's fleet from day to day."

**Timing.** Twice explicit. Drake's case for striking Spain in 1588 was "a question of time as well as place," and the value of the blow was in catching the Armada before its mobilisation was complete; by the time permission came, the moment had passed. And the Green Pamphlet makes it a rule: where the weaker side successfully takes the offensive, "it is due to his doing so before the enemy's mobilization or concentration is complete."

**Terrain and position.** Corbett's terrain is the pattern of communications and the geography that shapes it: terminals, focal points, naval bases, and the narrows and capes where routes converge — Finisterre, Gibraltar, Suez, the Cape, Singapore. Position is preponderance: relative strength is modified by "the inter-relation of naval positions and the comparative convenience of their situation in regard to the object of the war." Rooke took Gibraltar in 1704 and the Toulon fleet had to come to him; a position controlling communications vital to the enemy's plan is, nine times in ten, the most effective way of "seeking out" his fleet.

**Deception and information.** Concentration is itself a deception: the point of not massing is "to deny the enemy knowledge of our actual distribution or its intention at any given moment," and the ideal presents "an appearance of weakness that covers a reality of strength." Every detached squadron should be "a trap to lure the enemy to destruction." Feints get a full treatment: Drake at San Domingo in 1585, Saunders holding Montcalm away from Wolfe's landing, Togo detaching a cruiser squadron into the Gulf of Pe-chi-li so that Stakelberg dared not concentrate. And the protective converse: in the 1909 pamphlet, when observing a port to draw the enemy out, use every means to prevent his knowing he is being observed.

**Alliances.** Named as the first of the three historic functions of the fleet in the Green Pamphlet: "the prevention or securing of alliances (*i.e.*, deterring or persuading neutrals as to participating in the war)." Rooke's operations of 1702–04 to bring Savoy and Portugal into the Grand Alliance mattered more at their crisis than attacking Toulon. He also notes the alliance as an isolating device — the Anglo-Japanese Treaty "had isolated the struggle" of 1904–05 — and the alliance as a trap, since even a neutral buffer is no protection if the neutral is weak enough to violate or strong enough to be bought.

**Logistics and economy.** Economy is a theatre. Economic pressure at sea "starts from the first," unlike on land where it follows victory, and crippling the enemy's finance "is a direct step to his overthrow." "When other things are equal, it is the longer purse that wins. It has even many times redressed an unfavourable balance of armed force." Logistically, the defining constraint of the steam age for him is fuel; the defining constraint of the whole limited-war method is that the contingent must keep "touch with the sea," which is to say an uncuttable line back to its own base. And he notes the reverse: the offensive "grows weaker as it advances, by prolonging its communications."

**Morale and discipline.** He is candid that the offensive's largest advantage is moral — "the strength and energy which are born of the moral stimulation of attack" — and that the defensive's largest cost is moral, being "so insidious in its working as to tempt us never to utter the word." Drake's stated reasons for wanting to strike Spain in 1588 were "purely moral." And he attributes the one clear case of a successful surprise torpedo attack partly to a defending squadron whose discipline had fallen so low that standing orders were obeyed "in a very perfunctory manner."

**The decisive point.** He resists the phrase in its usual sense. Decision by battle remains the only way to permanent general command, and "sooner or later it must be done, and the sooner the better." But the decisive point is not automatically the enemy's main force; in limited war it may be the object itself, and in exercising command it is the transports. The 1909 pamphlet reframes the whole question as one of disposition: dispose so as to concentrate at the decisive point once it is identified, "and also so as to conceal from the enemy what it is intended to make the decisive point."

**The culminating point and when to stop.** He does not use Clausewitz's term but the idea runs through the limited-war chapters. The structural weakness of limited war is "the arrest of your offensive action" once the object is taken, and an arrested offensive is "inherently vicious." Japan escaped the arrest only because her object obliged her to keep going. Stopping is a calculation about the other side, not about you: the third phase is to "demonstrate to Russia that the acceptance of the situation that had been set up was more to her advantage than the further attempt to break it down." Japan ended the war "very far short of having overthrown her enemy," with her own offensive power "almost if not quite exhausted." And the whole definition of a limited object is that there is a point "at which, long before your force was exhausted or even fully developed, it would be wiser to abandon your object rather than to spend more upon it."

**When not to fight.** Directly: "If we are strong, we press to the issue of battle when we can. If we are weak, we do not accept the issue unless we must." And upstream of that, he says that if you have no reasonable hope of crushing the enemy's war power and the object requires it, "you ought not to go to war."

**Defence versus offence.** Covered above at principles 4, 9 and 18. His one-line summary is the Green Pamphlet's: the offensive is "naturally the more effective form of war," the defensive "naturally the stronger form"; and — the sentence most easily lost — "the Offensive must not be confused with the Initiative," since a defensive position the enemy must dislodge you from leaves the initiative in your hands.

**The character required of the commander.** Deliberately thin. He says theory "should educate the mind of the man who is to lead in war... but it should not accompany him on the field of battle," and that execution depends on "the combination of intangible human qualities which we call executive ability." The one quality he does specify is a sense of proportion under risk: "The great leader is the man who can measure rightly to what breadth of deployment he can stretch his concentration." He also notes Clausewitz's condition for the unlimited form — great preponderance or "an innate propensity for extreme hazards" — and sides with Jomini, who thought that propensity was what destroyed Napoleon.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The contemporary attack: he is dismantling the battle faith.** Spenser Wilkinson, the leading military publicist of the day and from 1909 the first Chichele Professor of Military History at Oxford, went after Corbett twice: in the *Morning Post* of 3 August 1909, against the Green Pamphlet's redefinition of command of the sea and its limited-war doctrine, and again on 19 February 1912, reviewing the book. His charges were that Corbett had "completely misunderstood" Clausewitz on limited war, that redefining command of the sea "is to propose to substitute the effect for the cause — the end for the means," and that "the naval commander ought never to allow anything but the enemy's naval forces to occupy the first place in his mind." He treated Corbett as an amateur discarding the settled conclusions of Philip Colomb, Cyprian Bridge, Reginald Custance and Mahan, and wrote that if the book "were read by young naval officers it must have a disastrous offset upon the Navy."

**The Admiralty's public repudiation.** The quarrel outlived him. On the title page of the third volume of *Naval Operations* (1923), published after his death, the Board of Admiralty printed: "Their Lordships find that some of the principles advocated in this book, especially the tendency to minimize the importance of seeking battle and of forcing it to a conclusion, are directly in conflict with their views." The immediate cause was Corbett's handling of Jutland, which defended Jellicoe and was unsparing about Beatty, by then First Sea Lord. It is the most public institutional rejection of any book on this list, and it is a caution about what happens when a theory of restraint is written for a service whose self-image is Nelson's.

**The continental critique: it only works if you are Britain.** Admiral Raoul Castex, the foremost French naval theorist of the interwar years, borrowed heavily from *Some Principles* and attacked it anyway, holding Corbett to be a civilian without the experience to challenge Mahan and his theory to be parochial: "As a good Anglo-Saxon, he has a phobia about permanent armies on the continent... He rides his hobby-horse of limited war that he presents as original and above the understanding of continental strategists," and, Castex charged, he "breaks with the idea of the nation in arms, of the importance of organized force, of battle, of the offensive." Castex concluded that Corbett's maritime strategy rested on Britain's unique situation and had little relevance beyond it. He also conceded, in a passage worth more than the attack, that Corbett had given him "an intense, intellectual and almost moral crisis" and forced "a disagreeable but useful review of myself." (Quoted from Hervé Coutau-Bégarie, "Corbett and Richmond in France," in Goldrick and Hattendorf, eds., *Mahan Is Not Enough*, Naval War College Press, 1993, p. 282, as reproduced in Samuel E. Rogers's 2018 East Carolina University thesis.)

**The most consequential misreading: Liddell Hart.** Basil Liddell Hart took Corbett's limited-war argument, and particularly *England in the Seven Years War* (1907), and built *The British Way in Warfare* (1932) on it without much acknowledgement. Andrew Lambert's judgement is that in co-opting Corbett's principles Liddell Hart transformed them "from a pragmatic flexible response into dogmatic prescription." That is the exact inversion Corbett wrote the book to prevent: a contingent, conditional method turned into a national rule about never sending an army to the Continent. Brian Bond's assessment is that Liddell Hart "was original only in his rather extreme and one-sided statement of the maritime case." Anyone meeting Corbett through the "British way of war" slogan is meeting Liddell Hart.

**"Fleet in being" as a licence for doing nothing.** Corbett anticipates this one and spends pages on it. Nottingham in 1690 thought Torrington meant a fleet safe in port was "in being"; Corbett says he meant the opposite, and states it as flatly as he states anything: active and vigorous life, not mere existence. He adds that a fleet withdrawn into a fortified base has abandoned the ulterior object entirely, and that submitting to blockade to tie down a superior enemy is "almost wholly evil" if prolonged. The phrase has nonetheless been used ever since to dignify inaction, and it is the standing failure mode of the doctrine.

**"He is against decisive battle."** He is not. "All forms alike demand the use of battles," and limited war "differs only from that of unlimited war in that instead of having to destroy our enemy's whole power of resistance, we need only overthrow so much of his active force as he is able or willing to bring to bear." He explicitly rejects any return to "the old fallacy of attempting to decide wars by manoeuvres" and says limited war "has nothing to do" with parading for strategical positions. His argument is about the objective of the main strategic effort, not about whether to fight.

**Two attributions to check before repeating.** "The British Army should be a projectile to be fired by the British Navy" is not Corbett; it is attributed to Sir Edward Grey and quoted by Fisher in *Memories* (1919), and the word does not occur in Corbett's text. "He that commands the sea is at great liberty and may take as much or as little of the war as he will" *is* in Corbett — but it is Corbett quoting Francis Bacon, and it is routinely re-quoted as Corbett's own sentence.

**The absence that says most.** Unlike Sun Tzu, Musashi and Machiavelli, Corbett has essentially no self-help or business-strategy afterlife. A search across the popular management literature turns up analogies to "command of the sea" and Mahan, and almost nothing built on Corbett. The reasons are diagnostic. He produces no portable aphorisms — his best sentences are conditional clauses. He offers no moral uplift. And his central mechanism requires a physical barrier the stronger party cannot cross, which is exactly the element that does not survive translation into a market. The one place he is heavily used is in the professional literature on space and on maritime competition, where the geography argument still has something to hold on to. That silence is a warning to anyone adapting him: the part of Corbett that is quotable is not the part that does the work.

**The modern scholarly assessment.** Donald M. Schurman's *Julian S. Corbett, 1854–1922* (1981) is the standard biography; Eric Grove edited the Naval Institute Press "Classics of Sea Power" edition (1988); Jerker Widén's *Theorist of Maritime Strategy* (2012) is the systematic study; Andrew Lambert's *The British Way of War: Julian Corbett and the Battle for a National Strategy* (Yale, 2021) is the major recent treatment; Jon Sumida reads Corbett as a genuine and intelligent Clausewitzian. Kevin D. McCranie's 2025 *Texas National Security Review* essay names the standing limits candidly: nuclear weapons cut against the assumption of a protective barrier, mines and submarines and missiles have made command of the sea much harder to establish cleanly, isolation of an object is far harder against non-state actors, and states — the United States in particular — have repeatedly found limited objectives psychologically intolerable and escalated past them. McCranie opens with Corbett's own complaint, which is the fairest summary of his reception: "I seem to have written so entirely in vain & seldom see any effect except where someone has mistaken or misapplied the teachings & consequently gone wrong."

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Corbett is the most useful text on this list for a man with no position, and the one most likely to be misapplied, and both for the same reason: he is the only one who states the preconditions under which the weak actually beat the strong, and states them as *conditions* rather than encouragements.

**Start with his first question, not with method.** What is this contest about, what is the thing at issue worth to you, and what is it worth to him? A man with nothing usually cannot answer the third part, and that is the whole game. If the thing you want is something the incumbent will spend without limit to keep, you are in an unlimited war you cannot win, and Corbett's advice is unambiguous: you ought not to go to war. Pick again. The object must be small *to him*, not small to you.

**Then apply the second condition, which almost everyone skips.** A limited object must also be isolable — situated so that he cannot bring his whole weight against it even if he wants to. Corbett is explicit that Clausewitz's own examples fail this test and are therefore not examples. Most of what gets called "finding a niche" is Clausewitz's failure, not Corbett's success: a small thing adjacent to the incumbent's core, with no barrier between, which he can take back by simply deciding to. Before committing, name the barrier. If you cannot name it, you do not have a limited-war situation, and the correct conclusion is that the method does not apply. Corbett would not soften that and neither should you.

**Barriers, in the modern sense, are the transferable part.** The sea was Corbett's because it was a physical obstacle to the movement of mass. The modern analogues are the things that make an incumbent's mass fail to arrive: a customer set too small to justify his fixed costs, a channel his distribution does not reach, a regulatory or licensing position he cannot enter without unwinding something else, a relationship he cannot buy, a body of specific knowledge that takes years rather than money. These are weaker than an ocean — every one of them can be crossed eventually — so Corbett's method for you is slower and more precarious than it was for Britain. Treat the barrier as a depreciating asset and know roughly how fast it is depreciating.

**Take the thing before contesting his strength.** The three-phase shape is directly usable. Seize the object first, while he is not organised against you; that gives you the initiative even though you are weaker. Then defend it on ground you chose, forcing him to spend at the end of a long line while you spend at the end of a short one. Then press only when the balance has moved. Corbett's own summary of what this buys — strategic offence combined with minor-strategic defence — is the most efficient use of a small force anyone on this list identifies.

**Control communications, not territory.** The best single transfer in the book. You cannot own a market, exclude third parties from it, or live off it. What you can control is passage: the routes by which things reach people. Your object is not to possess a position but to sit on the lines. And Corbett's structural point about parallel lines transfers with it: at sea the two sides' communications are largely the same lines, so defending yours and attacking his are one action. Distribution works like this. The channel that carries your work to a customer is often the same channel that carries his. Occupying it is simultaneously defence and offence, which is why it is cheap.

**Stop waiting for command; nobody has it.** The commonest posture of a man with nothing is to wait until conditions are clear — until the product is finished, the credential is issued, the funding is secured. Corbett's answer is that the normal condition is an uncommanded sea. The Americans in 1898 held their army in port because an undefeated Spanish squadron was somewhere at large, and nearly lost the campaign. The Japanese in 1904 landed at Seoul with a hostile fleet inside the theatre, content that they could deny the enemy control even though they could not hold it. Denying control is much cheaper than holding it and is usually enough to move.

**Separate what exercises control from what secures it.** "On cruisers depends our exercise of control; on the battle-fleet depends the security of control." The many cheap units do the actual work; the one expensive concentrated thing exists so the cheap ones are not interfered with. A man with nothing has almost all cruisers and no battle fleet, which means he can exercise control anywhere nobody is contesting him, and nowhere anybody is. Know which of your activities are cruisers and which is the one thing you must not lose, and do not spend the second protecting the first out of nervousness — Corbett's reproach to commanders who withdrew cruisers from their real work to screen the fleet applies exactly.

**Disperse, but stay coherent.** Concentration is not putting everything in one place; it is coherent dispersal about a centre, retaining the ability to mass fast and the ability to conceal where you will mass. The check is his: no detachment placed where it cannot fall back on the centre. That is the honest line between a portfolio and a scatter. And the sentence to keep: "the ideal concentration, in short, is an appearance of weakness that covers a reality of strength." Announced strength is spent strength; the concentration that has become a mass has lost its concealment and its flexibility at the same moment.

**Keep touch with the sea.** The single most practical clause in the limited-war chapters. Every British contingent that worked had an independent theatre, an independent function, and a line back to its own base that the enemy could not cut. The equivalent is a source of income and support that does not depend on the party you are competing with, and that he cannot interrupt as a response to your success. If your line of retreat runs through him — his platform, his patronage, his payroll — you do not have a limited-war position; you have a hostage situation, and Corbett's failure cases are all of this kind.

**Refuse the decisive engagement, but stay in contact.** Kempenfelt's rule is the sharpest single instruction for the weak on this list: since you cannot face him where he is strong, accept being even weaker there in order to be locally superior somewhere he must act. And Torrington's: keep moving, keep observing, keep a retreat, decline the general action, take the counter-stroke when he exposes himself. What makes this work is that it is activity. "Anything that is not activity is not war." A fleet in port is not a fleet in being, and a man who describes waiting as strategy has lost the distinction Corbett spent a chapter making.

**Do not chase.** Cornwallis's order to Cotton — if the French put to sea and you have not seen them, do not follow unless you are certain of their course, because leaving your position uncovered is worse than missing them — is directly usable against the most common error of the ambitious with nothing, which is conforming to the strongest competitor's movements. Corbett's diagnosis of the maxim in its naked form is that it hands the enemy the power to lead you wherever he likes. "Seeking out" the market leader is not a plan; it is his plan.

**Make the threat, and understand its decay.** Small forces earn their keep by containing larger ones. But the threat works only while undeclared: once Britain concentrated in the Peninsula and its line of operations was known, "the threat ceased." And a threat never executed stops being believed — Napoleon ignored coastal expeditions for years and was right to, until one nearly worked. Both halves apply. Do not announce; and do not build a career entirely out of an option you never exercise.

**Two things about money.** Economic pressure at sea begins on day one rather than after victory, and finance is a theatre in its own right: "when other things are equal, it is the longer purse that wins." For a man with nothing, the practical reading is unsentimental — the longer purse is usually not yours, so the only version of this you can run is lowering your own burn until the ratio between what a month costs you and what a month costs him is the thing that is asymmetric. Corbett's whole exhaustion mechanism requires that you be able to outlast, and most people using the limited-war vocabulary have not checked whether they can.

**Now the things that do not transfer, and why.**

*Corbett's weak actor is not weak.* Britain had the smallest army among the great powers and the largest navy, the largest merchant marine and the deepest capital market in the world. Japan in 1904 had a modern state, a competent army, a favourable geography and a treaty isolating her quarrel. Every case in the book is a power strong in one medium converting that strength into leverage against strength in another. The book has nothing to say to someone weak in every dimension except this, which is worth having: find the one medium in which you are not weak, and make the contest happen there. If there is no such medium yet, the honest reading of Corbett is that you are not yet in a position to run his strategy, and the prior task is to acquire one.

*A fleet in being requires a fleet.* Torrington's defensive worked because Tourville had to account for fifty-six ships of the line. A man with nothing has no force in being and therefore exerts no containing effect on anyone. The deterrent half of the doctrine is unavailable until you have built something the other party must plan around, and pretending otherwise is the most attractive self-deception this book offers.

*Blockade and commerce prevention do not translate.* They require a sovereign's legal right to seize and destroy, an apparatus of cruisers, and a target with seaborne trade. There is no individual analogue to interdicting another party's supply, and attempts to construct one usually amount to harassment, which Corbett would classify with the plunder and ravaging that war abandoned because it demoralised your own force.

*The institutional machinery does not translate.* Joint staffs, two commanders-in-chief, war plans, the deflection of strategy by diplomacy — all of it presupposes an organisation. What survives at individual scale is the underlying observation, which is that strategy and your relationships are decided together and neither has a clean slate. Corbett's own phrasing is better than any paraphrase: this friction "is usually regarded as a disease. It is really a vital factor in every strategical problem."

*The timescale does not translate comfortably.* The Seven Years War took seven years and the conquest of Canada five campaigns of it, against a France deliberately declining battle. Britain ran this method for two centuries with a state's revenue behind it. A man with nothing has a much shorter clock and a barrier that erodes much faster, and the exhaustion mechanism at the heart of limited war rewards whoever can wait. If the shape of your plan is "hold the position until he gives up," you should be able to say how long he can afford to keep pushing, and if you cannot, you have a wish rather than a strategy.

*And the failure the book was written to prevent.* Corbett's method became Liddell Hart's rule, and a conditional argument about when limited war is correct turned into a doctrine that it always is. The same thing happens to individuals: "stay small, stay niche, never take him on directly" hardens into an identity, and then the defensive does what Corbett says it does to those who live in it too long — it "cursed her when she was weak, and cursed her when she was strong," breeding an incapacity to strike when the strength has finally arrived. The entire point of the limited form is that it is a way of *accumulating* relative strength until the more drastic form becomes available. If there is no third phase in your plan, you have not read him; you have read the slogan.
