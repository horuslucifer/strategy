# 9. Maurice, comte de Saxe — Reveries on the Art of War (Mes Rêveries)

## 1. Identity

*Mes Rêveries* is a working general's private notebook on how to build, feed, move, discipline and employ an army, written in 1732 by a thirty-six-year-old soldier who at that moment held no command, and published twenty-five years later by an editor working from the manuscript after the author's death. It is not a treatise offered to the public by its author. That single fact governs everything about the book's shape: its unfinished arguments, its sudden digressions, its willingness to say things a serving officer would not print, and its total absence of the deferential dedication-to-the-prince that frames Vegetius and Machiavelli.

**Structure.** The work is in two books. Book I is what de Saxe calls *the detail* — the material foundation: eight chapters, of which chapter I is subdivided into six articles (raising, clothing, subsisting, paying, exercising, and forming troops for action), chapter II is the Legion, chapter III is cavalry in seven articles, chapter IV is the grand manoeuvre and the column in two articles, and chapters V–VIII are small arms, colours, artillery, and military discipline. Book II is headed *Of the Sublime Branches of the Art of War* and runs to twelve chapters: fortification and the attack and defence of places; reflections upon war in general; mountain war; war in enclosed country; lines and retrenchments; situations for encampment and for engagements; the passage of rivers; the attack of retrenchments; redoubts; spies and guides; signs; and the qualifications necessary for the commander in chief of an army. To these the printed editions append letters (between de Saxe and Augustus II of Poland, and to the chevalier de Folard) and a short essay, *Reflections upon the Propagation of the Human Species*.

**Date of composition.** De Saxe finished the book at the end of December 1732 and said so himself in a closing note to the reader. The abbé Pérau, who edited the French first edition, quotes that note in his life of the author: *"J'ai composé cet ouvrage en treize nuits; j'étois malade: ainsi il pourroit bien se ressentir de la fièvre que j'avois. Cela doit m'excuser sur la régularité & l'arrangement, ainsi que sur l'élégance du style. J'ai écrit militairement & pour dissiper mes ennuis."* Pérau adds: *"Ce fut à la fin de décembre 1732 qu'il termina cet ouvrage."* The thirteen-nights-of-fever story is therefore not a legend attached to the book by later admirers; it is the author's own excuse for its disorder, printed at the end of the second French volume.

**Publication.** The French first edition is *Mes Rêveries. Ouvrage posthume de Maurice comte de Saxe*, two volumes, Amsterdam and Leipzig, chez Arkstée et Merkus, 1757, "augmenté d'une histoire abrégée de sa vie" by the abbé Pérau. Carlyle, citing the book, gives the same imprint and date. Modern reference works sometimes give 1756; the title page of the copy I opened reads M. DCC. LVII.

**Which translations I used.** Three English texts and one French.

1. The 1757 London octavo, *Reveries, or Memoirs upon the Art of War by Field-Marshal Count Saxe*, printed for J. Nourse, translator unnamed (the dedication to the General Officers is signed only "THE TRANSLATOR"). This is my base text. I worked from the archive.org OCR of the actual octavo, normalised the long *s* and rejoined hyphen-broken words, and grep every quotation against it.
2. The 1759 Edinburgh octavo (Sands, Donaldson, Murray and Cochran, for Alexander Donaldson), *Reveries, or, Memoirs concerning the Art of War*, with Pérau's life prefixed and the *Treatise concerning Legions* annexed.
3. The 1776 Edinburgh reprint of the same.
4. Volume I of the French 1757 first edition, used to check what the English translators did and did not render.

**A bibliographic finding that matters for quotation.** The Edinburgh text is usually catalogued as a second English translation. In every body passage I compared it is word-for-word the London 1757 text. Book II chapter XII, London 1757: "Although I have dwelt so much upon the subject of general engagements, yet I am far from approving of them in practice, especially at the commencement of a war; and I am persuaded that an able General might avoid them, and yet carry on the war, as long as he pleased." Edinburgh 1759, same chapter: "general engagements, yet I am far from approving of them in practice, especially at the commencement of a war; and I am perfuaded... that an able general might avoid them, and yet carry on the war as long as he pleased." Identical. The same holds for "the principal part of all discipline depends upon the legs, and not the arms," for "it is the goodness, and not the number of troops, on which victory depends," and for "it is the soul of all armies." The *preface*, however, is freshly and differently englished in the Edinburgh printings. My inference, labelled as inference: Alexander Donaldson — the Edinburgh reprinter who would later break the London booksellers' perpetual copyright in *Donaldson v. Becket* — reprinted the Nourse body, commissioned a new preface and a translation of Pérau's life, and advertised the result as translated from the French. I therefore treat the English editions as one translation-family, and cite the 1757 London text as the source, noting where the Edinburgh preface reads better.

**What the English translators dropped.** De Saxe's own first sentence is missing from every English edition I opened. The French opens: *"Cet ouvrage n'est point enfanté par le désir d'établir un nouveau système sur l'art de la guerre: je le compose pour m'amuser & pour m'instruire."* — This work is not brought forth by any desire to establish a new system on the art of war; I compose it to amuse and to instruct myself. The English editions begin at the next sentence, "War is a science covered with darkness." So the book that reached English readers, and through them Phillips's 1940 translation and the whole modern reception, is a book with its author's disclaimer removed. The English de Saxe sounds like a man proposing a system. The French de Saxe expressly denies he is doing anything of the kind. The closing note about the thirteen nights, which makes the same point, is also absent from the 1757 and 1759/1776 English editions; Phillips restored it in 1940 as "To the Readers."

**Corrections to the blog entry (entries xxxv–xxxvi, and xxxviii where it bears on de Saxe).**

*The blog says "Maurice de Saxe (1696 – 1770)." The evidence says 1750.* Pérau's life records that he was seized with fever on 21 November 1750 and died on the 30th, and that he was interred at St Thomas's in Strasbourg on 8 February following. The French Wikipedia infobox gives 30 November 1750 at Chambord; the English gives 20 November 1750; Phillips gives "November, 1750, in the fifty-fourth year of his age." Every source agrees on 1750. The 1770 in the blog is twenty years wrong and would make him outlive the publication of his own posthumous book by thirteen years.

*The blog says the Reveries is "a 32 chapter book." That is the number of headings, not the number of chapters.* As de Saxe left it the work is two books of eight and twelve chapters — twenty chapters. The number 32 appears when Book I's chapters-in-articles are counted as separate items: 6 articles in chapter I, 1 chapter II, 7 articles in chapter III, 2 articles in chapter IV, plus chapters V–VIII, giving 20 headed sections in Book I, plus Book II's 12, equals 32. That is exactly how Major Thomas R. Phillips laid out his 1940 contents page in *Roots of Strategy*, which lists 32 headed sections from "Raising Troops" to "The General Commanding." The blog is counting Phillips's table of contents and calling it the book's structure.

*The blog says Frederick "became King in 1740 after succeeding Marshal de Saxon as Commander in the War of Polish Succession." Every clause of that is wrong.* Frederick II became King of Prussia in 1740 on the death of his father, not by succeeding anyone in a command. He held no command in the War of the Polish Succession (1733–38). De Saxe did not vacate any post for him: he was alive and in French service until 1750, was made Marshal of France in 1744, won Fontenoy on 11 May 1745, and was made Marshal General in 1747 — the whole of that career postdating Frederick's accession. The two were contemporaries. Phillips records that de Saxe visited Frederick at Sans-Souci in the summer of 1749 and was entertained there. Carlyle records that twenty years later, at the Neisse interview with Joseph II in August 1769, Frederick gave the Kaiser a copy of the Reveries as a keepsake. The correct relation is admiration across a generation, not succession.

*The blog says he wrote the book "during a thirteen-night span while being bedridden with a fever." Correct, and the source is de Saxe himself*, in the closing note quoted above — though a reader working from the 1757 or 1759 English editions would never find it, because those editions omit the note.

*The blog says the rank of Marshal General was an "honor given to only five others in French history." Correct.* The title *maréchal général des camps et armées du roi* has been borne by six men; de Saxe, made Marshal General in 1747, was the fifth, and Soult in 1847 the sixth.

*The blog says "his fascination with military technology improved precision and manufacturing throughout the early half of the nineteenth century." Unsupportable and chronologically confused.* He died in 1750. His own inventions — the *amusette*, a long light gun he claims pierced eighteen-inch oaks at a thousand paces; the thirteen-foot half-pike; the Roman helmet and the lambskin wig — were not adopted by any army.

**Two dating errors inside the text itself.** De Saxe says he entered the camp of Belgrade in 1718 with 55,000 men and that "on the day of battle, which was the 18th of August, we could muster only 22000 under arms." The relief-army battle outside Belgrade was fought on 16 August 1717, and the city surrendered on 21 August; the campaign is 1717, not 1718, and the day is the 16th. And in the redoubt chapter he has Peter arriving before Charles XII "towards the evening of the 8th of July, 1709," with the battle the following day; Poltava was fought on 8 July 1709 by the modern calendar. These are the slips of a man writing at speed from memory in a fortnight, and they are a fair warning about the book's factual texture.

## 2. The world the text assumes

He assumes **dynastic cabinet war**: armies belonging to a sovereign, fought for provinces and fortresses, with limited aims and no national mobilisation. Nothing in the book requires a cause; the soldier serves a king, and the question is only how to make him serviceable.

He assumes **an army that melts**. "I have always remarked, that a single campaign reduces an army one third at least, and sometimes one half; and that the cavalry in particular, is in so wretched a condition by the beginning of October, as to be no longer able to keep the field." Disease and desertion, not battle, are the main destroyers. This is the premise behind half his doctrine: if the army wastes anyway, the general who wastes it least wins, and the man who wins battles at the cost of his army has lost.

He assumes **war supports itself from the enemy's country**, by a system of written summonses backed by the threat of burning. Contributions, not a treasury, pay for his campaigns. His model campaign against Poland is explicitly designed to cost the invader nothing.

He assumes **the smoothbore flintlock and the volley**, and holds both in contempt. He counts the dead: two battalions at Belgrade fired a general discharge into a mass of Turks at thirty paces and killed thirty-two men, after which the Turks came through the smoke and cut both battalions to pieces. His whole tactical argument runs off that arithmetic.

He assumes **a venal officer corps**: regiments given to boys of eighteen or twenty by court favour, captains who keep their companies full by force, purveyors who profit on half-baked bread, provosts who look the other way. He is not reforming an ideal army but naming abuses by name.

He assumes **soldiers are not cannon-fodder but a perishable asset.** The chapter on clothing is about wet feet, rotting stockings, wool against skin, the hat that falls off a sleeping man's head — because those things produce the hospital, and the hospital produces the battalion of a hundred men at the end of a campaign.

And he assumes, as the one constant beneath all the rest, **that the human heart is unstable under sudden events.** He says this is why he wrote the book at all. Not courage, not cowardice: instability. The same troops who stormed a wood at Friedlingen ran from two squadrons that might have been their own, because one man shouted that they were cut off.

## 3. Core doctrine

Loci are given as Book.chapter.article in the structure of the 1757 English text (Book I chapter I article 5 = I.i.5). Where Phillips's 1940 section titles differ I note them.

**1. War has no fixed principles; what passes for its foundation is custom and ignorance.**
*Locus:* Preface.
*Reasoning:* Every other science rests on stated principles; war alone does not. The great captains who wrote on it gave none, having preferred to be agreeable rather than instructive, because "the mechanical part of war is insipid and tedious in description." Histories are worse than useless, since historians write war as it appears to their imagination.
*Limits:* He attaches a footnote in the English text conceding that war does have rules "as relate to the detail, or inferior branches," and not to the sublime. And his own French opening — cut in English — disclaims any intent to build a system at all.

**2. The detail is the foundation; no man can execute what he cannot build.**
*Locus:* Preface.
*Reasoning:* A man with a taste for architecture can draw a palace correctly, but if he does not know how to shape materials and lay a foundation "the whole fabrick must presently fall to ruin." The Romans beat multitudes of barbarians with small armies for no reason except the composition of their troops.
*Limits:* He immediately concedes the opposite case: "a man of enterprise and genius will not be [un]able to make some figure, even at the head of an army of Tartars; as it is much easier to take men as they are, than to make them what they ought to be."

**3. Raise troops by a universal law of limited service, binding every rank.**
*Locus:* I.i.1.
*Reasoning:* Enlistment by capitulation is fair but broken in practice; enlistment by artifice (slipping money into a man's pocket and then claiming him) is scandalous; conscription by compulsion is worse, because money buys exemption. A five-year term for all men between twenty and thirty, "no rank or degree whatsoever excluded, and the nobles and rich rendered, in a principal manner, subservient to it," would give an inexhaustible fund of recruits and end desertion, because those who had served would despise those who complained.
*Limits:* He binds it absolutely to keeping the bargain: a term of service must be honoured, or the whole thing collapses into the abuses he is trying to cure. And he concedes the plan suits only populous states that can fill their own armies.

**4. Health is a weapon system; the small things decide the great.**
*Locus:* I.i.2.
*Reasoning:* Hair that never dries, a hat that falls off, three pairs of garters that debilitate the nerves, woollen stockings that rot on the foot — these fill the hospitals and reduce battalions to a hundred men. He wants the hair cut short, a lambskin wig, a Roman helmet, a Turkish hooded cloak of two and a half ells, thin shoes worn greased on the bare foot, leather gaiters and wooden-soled galoshes. "Thus we perceive how far the smallest things may influence and affect those of the greatest consequence."
*Limits:* Cost is a stated constraint throughout — the wig costs twenty pence and lasts a life, the cloak is light and cheap; every proposal is defended on economy as well as health.

**5. Feed centrally, prefer biscuit to bread, and teach men to do without.**
*Locus:* I.i.3.
*Reasoning:* Messing in small groups makes a man forage for wood after a march, maraud, spoil his clothes and break his health. One sutler with four ox-carts and one great pot per century feeds the whole and puts an officer at every meal. Biscuit keeps, is carried seven or eight days, and does not require the train of bakers, mills, waggons and guards that bread requires; and the erecting of ovens "discovers so much of your intentions to the enemy, that it is needless to say any more about it." He adds the Roman vinegar ration as the secret of their resistance to climate, reporting the fact and leaving the cause to physicians.
*Limits:* He recommends periodically denying men even biscuit and making them bake bruised corn on iron plates, on Turenne's practice and his own: he made eighteen-month campaigns with troops who never had bread and never complained, and other campaigns with troops so accustomed to it that one day's interruption made every rapid enterprise impracticable.

**6. Pay enough that hope is real; quality, not quantity, wins.**
*Locus:* I.i.4.
*Reasoning:* "A handful of men well subsisted and disciplined is superior to a multitude of such as are neglected in those important particulars; for it is the goodness, and not the number of troops, on which victory depends." Underpay the officers and you get either rich men who will not endure fatigue or poor men with no spirit. "Hope encourages men to endure and attempt every thing; in depriving them of that, or removing it to too great a distance from them, you divest them of their very soul."
*Limits:* Economy is commendable "while confined within certain limits"; beyond them it is parsimony. And he does not abolish birth — he allows men of quality to purchase regiments from broken or aged officers, but forbids them to sell on, so that the regiment reverts to merit.

**7. The manual exercise is the least part of discipline; discipline lives in the legs.**
*Locus:* I.i.5.
*Reasoning:* Drill with the firelock makes a man steady under arms and no more. "After this exception, the principal part of all discipline depends upon the legs, and not the arms: the personal abilities which are required in the performance of all manœuvres, and likewise in engagements, are totally confined to them." He quotes Folard's answer to whether war is a trade or a science: "it is a trade for the ignorant, and a science for men of genius."
*Limits:* He excepts, as positively dangerous, the parts of the exercise designed for parade — carrying the firelock over the left arm, and firing by platoons.

**8. Cadence is the whole mystery of tactics.**
*Locus:* I.i.6.
*Reasoning:* Troops march at whatever pace each man likes, so the rear runs to catch up and the column disorders itself; officers are posted at every turning to hurry the rear. The cure "is nothing more than to march in cadence, in which alone consists the whole mystery, and which answers to the military pace of the Romans." Drums and martial sounds were invented for it, and this, not the drawing-up of an army, is what the ancients meant by *tactick*. He grounds it in observation: people dance all night with music and cannot bear two hours without it; men fall into step involuntarily when the drum beats to arms.
*Limits:* Any air in common or triple time serves; there is no magic tune, only what suits drum and fife. And the real payoff is not neatness but control: "to be able to increase, or diminish the rapidity of a march during an engagement, is an advantage which may be of infinite consequence."

**9. Depth beats frontage; the man who reserves his fire and keeps walking wins.**
*Locus:* I.i.6, restated I.v.
*Reasoning:* A battalion four deep and wide cannot advance without crowding to eight in the centre and four on the flanks, then halting to fix itself in front of an enemy. His formation is eight deep on an equal frontage, able to widen by a pace or two, march twice as fast, and make way through the other's ranks "in an instant." Of two battalions, "the one that gives its fire in advancing; or the other, that reserves it," experience gives the victory to the second, because the first must halt or crawl while reloading and be struck before it is ready.
*Limits:* He admits the English drew up three deep and that his argument is aimed at French practice. He concedes fire is decisive where the two sides are separated by hedges, ditches, rivers and hollows — there it "cannot be too much encouraged."

**10. Where fire is right, make it aimed, unhurried and individually timed.**
*Locus:* I.v.
*Reasoning:* Word-of-command firing holds a man in a constrained position so he cannot level; in the hurry he forgets to ram, or loads the cartridge whole without biting the cap. Instead put an officer over every two files, advance the file-leader a pace, show him his target, and let the three men behind pass him their loaded pieces in turn while the officer stands beside him and stops him hurrying. Rotate the files. "It must be extremely unfortunate indeed, if the second or third shot does not take place."
*Limits:* It is explicitly a method for posts, sieges and obstacles — the very situations where he has just conceded fire belongs — not for the open field.

**11. Build an organisation that never changes shape: the legion.**
*Locus:* I.ii.
*Reasoning:* He forms the infantry into legions of four regiments, each regiment of four centuries, each century with a half-century of light foot and a half-century of horse, on three establishments (peace, preparatory, full war). Losses shrink the companies but never alter the number of divisions or officers, so "you will never be obliged to vary your manœuvre." He is blunt about why this matters: after a long peace he has seen troops of the same sovereign, assembled together, perform so differently that one would take them for different nations.
*Limits:* He is against raising new regiments at all unless grafted onto old ones and commanded by good officers, "for eight or ten campaigns generally destroys them"; and the cavalry he exempts from all reduction, because veteran men and horses are the only useful ones.

**12. Discipline is the soul of armies; few orders, no exemptions, moderate punishments.**
*Locus:* I.viii.
*Reasoning:* Without discipline established with prudence and supported with resolution, armies "are no better than so many contemptible heaps of rabble, which are more dangerous to the very state that maintains them, than even its declared enemies." Generals who answer abuse by enlarging their instructions are working on a false principle. "Few orders are best, but they are to be executed with attention, and offences to be punished without respect of either rank or extraction; all partiality and distinction must be utterly abolished."
*Limits:* Severity must be visibly without design, and punishments must be moderate enough that everyone will co-operate in enforcing them. His worked example is marauding: hang a man for it and nobody will report him; chain him to the provost on bread and water for a month or two and put him back in the ranks the night before an engagement, and the whole army will help catch him. He extends the same logic to the officers who let marauders past their posts.

**13. Labour is training, and rest is the enemy.**
*Locus:* II.ii, in the passage on constructing forts.
*Reasoning:* The Roman consuls prepared their legions by rendering them indefatigable, and employed them on works that were useless rather than let them be idle. "Continual exercise makes good soldiers, because it qualifies them for martial enterprises; and by being habituated to pain, they insensibly learn to despise danger. The transition from fatigue to rest enervates them."
*Limits:* He is precise about dosage: divide the workmen into four reliefs so each man labours three hours a day, and then he will work cheerfully and diligently — and the work is to be done to the drum, in cadence, as Lysander's Lacedaemonians pulled down the Piraeus to the flute.

**14. Make war pay for itself by system, not by plunder.**
*Locus:* II.ii.
*Reasoning:* Large detachments sent for contributions spread distress over a small area, are seen coming, are betrayed to the enemy, and their commanders compound with the inhabitants and come back with a fraction and a ruined detachment. His method is twenty parties of twenty-five or thirty men a month, marching only by night, carrying circular letters that threaten military execution, and — if refused — firing one detached house and marching away with a threat to return. Every party is examined at a rendezvous afterwards; any man guilty of the least rapine is hanged, any officer who took money is cashiered or shot. Payment is monthly and proportioned to ability, so the inhabitants can help one another meet it.
*Limits:* It is a system of terror kept deliberately cheap and legible: the threat does the work, the burning is a token, and the discipline against private plunder is what makes the threat credible. He is not describing a licence to loot; he is describing an extraction machine that would stop working if it became one.

**15. Decline the general engagement; it is possible to make war leaving nothing to chance.**
*Locus:* II.xii; anticipated at II.ii.
*Reasoning:* "Although I have dwelt so much upon the subject of general engagements, yet I am far from approving of them in practice, especially at the commencement of a war; and I am persuaded that an able General might avoid them, and yet carry on the war, as long as he pleased. Nothing reduces an enemy so much as that method of conduct... for by having frequent encounters with him, he will gradually decline, and at length be obliged to skulk, and avoid you." The general's whole province is to keep his army "in such a state, as never to be obliged to engage contrary to his inclination."
*Limits:* Two, stated in the same breath. First, an opportunity of a general action "in which you have all imaginable reason to expect the victory, ought [not] to be neglected." Second, the claim is about accident, not about certainty: the aim is "to make war, without trusting any thing to accident; which is the highest point of skill and perfection, within the province of a General." He also gives the practical criteria elsewhere (II.vi): attack only if your flanks are covered, if you can engage a small part of his army with a large part of yours, and if some river, marsh or obstacle checks him — "supported by circumstances of which nature, you can attack him with confidence, although considerably inferior in numbers, because you will risk nothing, and may obtain a great deal."

**16. Fortify the field: redoubts are the equaliser of the weaker army.**
*Locus:* II.ix, with the applications at II.vi.
*Reasoning:* The case is Poltava, told at length. Peter tells his council that the Swedes are better exercised, more adroit and more impetuous, and that the problem is therefore "to fall upon some scheme that may render this superiority of theirs useless to them." Seven palisaded redoubts thrown up in a night in front of his infantry did it: the Swedes could neither pass between them nor go round, broke themselves on them, and were destroyed by an army that had never beaten them in the open. De Saxe's conclusion is flat: "it was the disposition alone which conquered the Swedes in this action, without the Muscovite troops having contributed to the victory." A redoubt takes about 1,488 men five hours. One is often enough to stop a whole army in a confined situation, cover a wing, or hold more ground than your numbers allow.
*Limits:* Redoubts only work if you use the openings they make. He faults Peter for watching three of his own redoubts be taken without succouring them. And they are the *opposite* of continuous entrenchments, which he thinks defeat the troops posted behind them — at Malplaquet, he argues, Villars should have abandoned the entrenchment and used redoubts and attacking brigades instead.

**17. Intelligence is eyes; signs are readable; and the general must be unreadable.**
*Locus:* II.x, II.xi, II.xii.
*Reasoning:* "One cannot be too diligent in the procuring of Spies and Guides. M. de Montecuculli says, that they serve as eyes to the head... money therefore should never be wanting upon a proper occasion; for the acquisition of such as are good, is cheap at any price." Take them from the country of the war; disperse them among the enemy's general officers, his sutlers, and above all his purveyors, "because their stores, magazines, and other preparations, furnish the best intelligence concerning his real design." They are not to know one another; each reports through one man of the first order, who accounts daily to the general who pays them. Against this, the signs chapter teaches reading the enemy without agents at all: men gathering unemployed on high ground facing a town at dusk means an assault is prepared; much firing in camp means action tomorrow, because they are cleaning arms; the dust of foragers differs from the dust of marching columns; the flash of arms in sun tells the direction of march; general dust in camp means the sutlers and baggage are going and the army will follow. And the commander himself must have "the art of penetrating into other men, and of remaining impenetrable himself."
*Limits:* The agent-runner must be watched as hard as the agents: the general must guard "as much as possible, against his being corrupted."

**18. Design your visible weakness; then convert it.**
*Locus:* II.vi.
*Reasoning:* "There is more address required in making bad dispositions, than may at first be imagined, provided they be such as are intentional, and so formed as to admit of being instantaneously converted into good ones: nothing can confound an enemy more, who has perhaps been anticipating a victory, than a stratagem of this kind; for he perceives your weakness, and draws up his army in the order in which he expects to benefit the most from it; but the attack is no sooner begun, than he discovers the imposition." Once he has committed his formation to your apparent weakness, changing it in your presence destroys him as surely as not changing it.
*Limits:* The general rule under which it sits is stricter than the trick: "one cannot possibly be too careful in avoiding every step, that may correspond with any hopes, or expectations of his; this is a maxim in war, never to be departed from."

**19. When you do win, pursue until the army is destroyed.**
*Locus:* II.xii.
*Reasoning:* The maxim that a beaten army should be allowed to retreat "is very religiously observed; but is nevertheless founded upon a false principle." Pursue and the orderly retreat becomes a rout; "a detachment of ten thousand men, is sufficient to overthrow an army of a hundred thousand, in flight." He gives Ramillies: the allies followed at a walk, a single English squadron fired into two French battalions, the noise emptied the whole field in an instant. The pursuing detachment must not pride itself on the regularity of its march — "it is impossible that any manœuvres can fail, but those which take up time, and give respite to the enemy."
*Limits:* Not a general pursuit with the whole army; a detached corps, in good order, pushing as long as daylight lasts. And he names the real obstacle, which is not doctrine: "a great many Generals avoid making the most of these opportunities, from an unwillingness to put an end to the war too soon."

**20. The commander: courage first, then genius, then health — and above all a free mind on the day.**
*Locus:* II.xii.
*Reasoning:* Without courage the rest is useless. Genius must be "strong and fertile in expedients." His province is subsistence, conduct, posts, formations, and "seizing the advantage of that favourable minute which happens in all battles, and which is capable of determining their success." To see it he must be disengaged: his examination of the ground quick, his orders short and simple — "The first line shall attack, the second shall sustain!" — and if he takes on the sergeant's work and tries to be everywhere, he is the fly in the fable that thought it drove the coach. The defect is systemic: officers study drill because drill is what they know, and when they get armies "from their ignorance how to do what they ought, are naturally led to do what they know."
*Limits:* He denies this can be taught. "Unless a man is born with talents for war, and those talents moreover, are brought to perfection, it is impossible for him ever to be more than an indifferent General... application will refine the ideas, but can never give a soul, for that is the work of nature." He names Prince Eugene as the man he studied for it.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

The Reveries addresses the weaker side **directly and repeatedly**, more directly than any other text on the list except Aeneas Tacticus, and unlike Aeneas it addresses the weaker side that is *on the offensive*.

**Directly stated.** The redoubt doctrine is built on an explicit inferiority case: Peter's own words as de Saxe reports them are that his troops are not inferior in resolution but are inferior "in many other respects," and the problem is to make the enemy's superiority useless. De Saxe then generalises: if the Muscovites, "who were at this time undisciplined, and dispirited likewise by an uninterrupted series of misfortunes, owed the victory to their redouts, what success may not be expected from them under the defence, and direction of a nation experienced in war?" The engagement criteria at II.vi conclude that with covered flanks, local superiority and an obstacle, "you can attack him with confidence, although considerably inferior in numbers, because you will risk nothing, and may obtain a great deal." In enclosed country "a detachment of six hundred men may stop a whole army," because the enemy judges strength by frontage; and "a partisan of enterprise and spirit, with three or four hundred men, will find means to attack an army on its march." The contribution system rests on parties of twenty-five or thirty men — "with twenty five, or thirty men on foot, one may traverse a whole kingdom with security." The whole conquest of Poland is costed at forty-eight thousand foot and under four thousand horse, funded by the conquered country, with no battle fought.

**Directly stated, on the man with no birth.** He attacks the purchase and court-gift of regiments to boys of eighteen or twenty because it "destroys all manner of emulation amongst the officers of inferior birth, who thereby become, in a great measure, excluded from any chance of succeeding to the like preferments." His verdict on who is actually any good is unambiguous: "the gentry, who are, what we call soldiers of fortune, make the only good officers." He demands that advancement be accompanied by proportionate honours and income, and that every officer be able to believe the command of a regiment is reachable by service — because without that belief you have removed the soul.

**Inference, labelled as mine.** De Saxe was the illegitimate son of a king, a foreigner and a Protestant in Catholic France, and wrote the book in 1732, in a period of enforced idleness after losing Courland, with his mother and mistress and father dead or dying and the court having forgotten him. The book's entire architecture — that method beats position, that the cheap thing well done beats the expensive thing done by custom, that an unfashionable system honestly costed beats an admired one — reads as the systematisation of an outsider's only available advantage. He does not say this. I am saying it.

**What he does not offer the man with nothing.** Every one of his mechanisms presupposes a command and a treasury. He has no advice whatever for the man who has neither. The contribution system requires an army in the field; the legion requires a sovereign's authority to reorganise; the redoubt requires 1,488 men and five hours. The one thing transferable without resources is the analysis: he is consistently right about which cheap variables the rich and the customary neglect.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Total. The larger half of the book is preparation — recruiting law, clothing, diet, pay scales, drill, organisation, discipline — on the stated ground that the sublime part of war cannot be executed by a general ignorant of the rudiments. He treats preparation as the only part of war that is fully in your hands.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Two systems, run in parallel. A paid, compartmented agent network concentrated on the enemy's logistics (purveyors above all, "because their stores, magazines, and other preparations, furnish the best intelligence concerning his real design"), and a discipline of reading physical signs — dust, the flash of arms, firing in camp, men gathering on high ground — which costs nothing and cannot be cut off. He warns against the intelligence of locals in mountain country: they have "no other authority but tradition" for their own country, and must be checked by personal reconnaissance.

**Timing.** He is against the early campaign. Since one campaign consumes a third to a half of an army, he would stay in quarters longer than custom allows, let the enemy exhaust himself at a siege, and fall on him in the autumn with fresh troops, then subsist in the enemy's country over winter. Within a battle, timing collapses into one thing: the "favourable minute" of enemy disorder, which the general must be unoccupied enough to see and must exploit with whatever troops are nearest.

**Terrain and position.** Treated at length and always instrumentally: ponds, rivulets, defiles, causeways, woods and hills are things to be converted, and where nature has not provided he manufactures them, damming a rivulet into a chain of ponds or throwing up redoubts to divide a field. Mountains: never pass a defile before the heights are held; passes always exist if looked for. Enclosed country: actions there are never decisive, so keep your rear free.

**Deception and information.** Heavy. False attacks to draw troops off the real one; the intentionally bad disposition that converts; the march away from the enemy's ovens before turning back to burn them; the feint at a mountain pass to buy time to find another. The governing rule is negative — never do the thing the enemy hopes for.

**Alliances.** Nearly silent, and hostile where he speaks. In occupied country he says listen to no overtures from the inhabitants and above all refuse their troops: tempted by advantage they offer themselves in crowds, then plunder their own country and break your ranks in action. There is no diplomacy in this book.

**Logistics and economy.** Central and unusually concrete: ox-carts and cooking pots per century, biscuit against bread, ten thousand oxen for fifty thousand men over a two-hundred-day campaign, the cost of a wig, the saving of a million livres if captains pay for their own recruits, the timpfe on every tun of Polish beer that would support three hundred and fifty thousand men. He is the only writer on this list who argues tactics and household economy in the same voice.

**Morale and discipline.** The book's true subject. Discipline is "the soul of all armies," and subordination is not a debasement of courage. But morale is not a thing you install; it is the unstable substrate you design around. Redoubts work partly because troops behind them "know, their retreat is secure"; troops in a second line five or six hundred paces back give way untouched because a man who sees danger in front and no relief behind is discouraged; ten men on a parapet will empty it of whole battalions.

**The decisive point.** In the field, the redoubt-and-brigade arrangement that makes the enemy attack where you have arranged his ruin. In a campaign, the enemy's subsistence: his ovens, his magazines, his convoys — attacks on which are "equally as beneficial, as those which attend a complete victory," at little or no risk.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** He has no doctrine of overreach, and the omission is glaring. His error runs the other way: he thinks generals stop too soon, from prudence about retreats or from an unwillingness to end the war. The only stopping rule he gives is the seasonal one — take the field for a month in autumn, ruin the enemy's army, and go back into quarters in good order.

**When not to fight.** Constantly, and by preference. Not at the beginning of a war; not without covered flanks; not without local superiority or an obstacle; not when the situation corresponds to anything the enemy hopes for; not when the object can be got by contribution, posts, and attrition instead. The Polish scheme is offered as a conquest "made without even fighting a single battle."

**Defence versus offence.** He refuses the dichotomy. His characteristic construction is offensive war conducted from fortified posts: seize positions, entrench them, live off the country, and let the enemy exhaust himself attacking or marching. In battle he wants the tactical defensive with an offensive return — redoubts to break the assault, then fresh brigades charging through the intervals. He expressly rejects continuous lines and entrenchments, which he thinks defeat their own defenders.

**The character required of the commander.** Courage, then a strong and fertile genius, then health. Sudden resource; penetration into other men and impenetrability in himself; mildness of temper with punishment without mercy and without passion; the discipline to stay out of the detail on the day of action so that judgment remains free; and a born aptitude that no study supplies.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The oldest hostile verdict is Carlyle's.** Recording that Frederick gave Joseph II a copy at Neisse in August 1769, Carlyle calls the Reveries "a strange Military Farrago, dictated, I should think, under opium," and adds that the book lay on the Kaiser's night-table until his death twenty-one years later, "not a page of it read, the leaves all sticking together under their bright gilding." The sneer is not unfair to the book's surface: it really does move without transition from lambskin wigs to the conquest of Poland to the construction of casemates, and de Saxe himself blamed the fever for it.

**The book's own editor disowned part of it.** In the Avertissement to the 1757 French edition the abbé Pérau warns the reader about the *Réflexions sur la propagation de l'espèce humaine*, remarking that the marshal, elsewhere so profound in the military art, "ne possédoit pas de même la science des loix & du gouvernement." The 1757 English translator went further, appending a footnote to the same essay: "I believe all the world will concur with me in opinion, that the Marshal was a greater General, than he was a Civilian; and that these limited marriages which he recommends, instead of doing good, would on the contrary make a dreadful confusion amongst society." The essay proposes term-limited marriages to raise the birth rate, and reads today as a small eugenic fantasy. It matters for the whole book: it shows what de Saxe's habit of reasoning coldly from first principles produces when it leaves the field, where it was disciplined by experience, for a domain where it was not.

**Misreading one: "war without leaving anything to chance" as a promise of certainty.** This is the phrase that gets quoted, usually as evidence that de Saxe was an Enlightenment rationalist who thought war could be reduced to a system. His own preface says the opposite — war is a science covered with darkness in which no certain rules can be given — and his French first sentence, deleted in English, disclaims any intent to found a system. What he actually claims is narrower and defensible: that a competent general can decline battle indefinitely, and that declining the one event which "fortune has a great share in" is the highest skill. It is a claim about *avoiding the dice*, not about *knowing the outcome*. The contradiction people find in him is partly an artefact of the English text they read.

**Misreading two: taking the book as a description of eighteenth-century practice.** Almost none of its specific reforms were adopted. There was no French legion of his design, no amusette in service, no thirteen-foot half-pike, no helmets and lambskin wigs, no five-year universal conscription. What did pass into practice was the cadenced march, which the French army adopted in the decades after his death, and the use of field redoubts and light troops. A reader who takes the Reveries as evidence of how armies worked in 1732 or 1757 will be wrong about nearly every particular.

**Misreading three: separating the doctrine from what he actually did.** The reverse error is also common — treating him as a pure theorist. The doctrine and the practice connect at exactly one visible point, and it is the redoubt. At Fontenoy on 11 May 1745, judging his infantry inferior to the Allies', he placed them behind fortified villages and field works, with the *Redoubt d'Eu* and *Redoubt de Chambonas* covering the gap between the Bois de Barry and Fontenoy; Ingoldsby's brigade stalled in front of the Redoubt d'Eu for hours and the main Allied column went in unsupported on one flank. That is the Poltava analysis of Book II chapter IX executed by its author, thirteen years after he wrote it.

**Misreading four, the modern one: the self-help and business extraction.** Three passages are regularly lifted. "The human heart is the starting point in all matters pertaining to war" (Phillips's rendering) becomes a slogan about emotional intelligence; de Saxe's actual proposition is that men are unstable under surprise and that you should therefore build structures that do not depend on them. "I do not favour pitched battles" becomes advice to avoid confrontation; de Saxe's alternative to battle is a documented programme of burning villages to compel contributions and of garrisoned posts designed to reduce a kingdom to submission without an engagement. And the redoubt becomes a metaphor for "defensible positions" in a market, with the operational content — that its value lies in the counter-attack it launches, and that failing to reinforce it is what loses it — dropped. The book is not gentle. It is a technology of coercion written by a man who thought coercion was cheaper than battle, and it is honest about that.

**Textual and factual cautions.** The book is a first draft written in a fortnight and never revised: the Belgrade campaign is misdated by a year and the battle by two days; the Poltava sequence is a day out; the chapter on fortification is interrupted by the Polish scheme and resumed under a heading that no longer fits. The letters and the essay printed with it are not part of the 1732 work — the letters to Folard concern the 1741 Prague campaign and a proposal made to the French court in April 1740 — and readers who treat the appended material as contemporaneous with the treatise will misdate his views by a decade.

**Scholarship I did not open, named so the reader can find it, not quoted.** The standard modern treatments of de Saxe's place between Vegetius and Napoleon are Robert S. Quimby's *The Background of Napoleonic Warfare* (1957) and Azar Gat's *The Origins of Military Thought* (1989); the standard biography in English is Jon Manchip White's *Marshal of France* (1962); B. H. Liddell Hart devoted a chapter to him in *Great Captains Unveiled* (1927); the modern French edition is Jean-Pierre Bois and Jean-Paul Charnay's *Mes Rêveries* (Economica, 2002). I have not read these for this file and make no claim about what they argue.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

**What transfers.**

*The detail is the only thing you control, and it is where the advantage actually is.* De Saxe's most useful structural insight is that everyone competes on the visible, prestigious part of the work and nobody competes on the boring part. Officers study the manual exercise because it is what gets inspected; nobody studies feet, food, or the pace of the march, and so those are where a decisive margin lies, free. The modern analogue is exact: in almost any field, the glamorous layer is crowded and the mechanical layer beneath it is empty. The man with no money and no name cannot win the crowded layer. He can own the empty one, and owning it is what eventually makes the crowded one available to him.

*Health, sleep and feet are strategy, not hygiene.* The clothing chapter is the least-quoted and most transferable part of the book. A battalion is reduced to a hundred men not by battle but by wet stockings; a career is not usually ended by a competitor but by exhaustion, illness and the slow attrition of small avoidable costs. De Saxe costs the wig at twenty pence and the campaign loss at three quarters of the recruits. Do that arithmetic on your own life honestly and it changes what you spend on.

*Capability precedes confidence, and confidence is downstream of drill.* "The principal part of all discipline depends upon the legs, and not the arms." Whatever your equivalent of the legs is — the base capacity that every specific technique rides on — build that first, daily, in a way that does not depend on motivation. Cadence is the same idea one level up: a fixed, externally imposed rhythm removes the need for constant individual decisions about pace, which is precisely why it works when the situation is frightening. A man with nothing should not be relying on judgment for anything he can put on a drumbeat.

*Refuse the decisive engagement early.* This is the transferable core of the famous claim, and it is not a counsel of timidity. De Saxe's position is that a general engagement is the one event where the outcome is substantially outside your control, that its result is final, and that it is therefore the last thing to accept, not the first — while a series of small encounters that cost the other side more than they cost you produces the same result with no single point of failure. For a man starting with nothing this means: do not stake what you have on one meeting, one pitch, one launch, one confrontation that could end you. It means deliberately choosing contests that are survivable when lost and compounding when won, and refusing the impressive, all-or-nothing opportunity for as long as you can afford to refuse it. His own criterion at II.vi is the practical form: engage when your flanks are covered, when you can bring a large part of your force against a small part of theirs, and when something checks them — under those conditions you can attack a stronger party, "because you will risk nothing, and may obtain a great deal."

*Build redoubts before you need them.* The Poltava lesson is the sharpest thing in the book for the weaker party: you do not beat a better opponent by getting better fast enough; you change the ground so that his superiority is not usable. Peter's troops did not become Swedes overnight. Seven earthworks made Swedish superiority irrelevant. The modern translation is unromantic — a skill the strong party can't be bothered to acquire, a niche too small for them to enter, a relationship or a contract or a piece of owned infrastructure that forces them to come at you the long way. Two operational caveats come with it, and they are the ones people drop: a redoubt is worth having only because of the counter-attack it lets you launch, and a redoubt you fail to reinforce when it is attacked is worse than none, because losing it in front of everyone breaks the people behind it.

*Design what people see.* The intentional bad disposition is the most transferable single technique in the book. Being underestimated is an asset only if you have arranged for the underestimation and have something prepared behind it. De Saxe's point is that once the other party has committed his arrangements to your apparent weakness, he is trapped: changing them in front of you is as fatal as not changing them. This is not bluffing. Bluffing has nothing behind it. His version requires the prepared reversal to exist.

*Intelligence is cheap and nobody pays for it.* "Money should never be wanting... for the acquisition of such as are good, is cheap at any price." And the unpaid half — reading the signs — costs nothing at all: dust, the direction of the light on arms, firing in camp the night before. The modern equivalents are the suppliers, the job postings, the permit filings, the people who run logistics for your competitors. Watch what the purveyors are doing, not what the principals are saying: "their stores, magazines, and other preparations, furnish the best intelligence concerning his real design."

*Few rules, enforced without exception, beat many rules enforced by mood.* De Saxe's marauder analysis is a piece of real institutional design: make the penalty moderate enough that the people around the offender will actually enforce it, and then enforce it on everyone without regard to rank. Applied to yourself, it is the argument against elaborate systems and heroic resolutions: few commitments, no exemptions, sustainable penalties.

*Hope must be structurally real, or the soul goes out of the work.* He is describing a promotion system, but the mechanism is general. Effort collapses when the reward is invisible or too distant, and it collapses *whether or not the man is willing*. If you are starting with nothing, the design problem is to arrange visible intermediate gains — not for morale in the motivational sense, but because an arrangement that depends on sustained willpower against an unlit horizon is an arrangement that has already failed.

*Labour is the training.* "Continual exercise makes good soldiers... by being habituated to pain, they insensibly learn to despise danger. The transition from fatigue to rest enervates them." He also gives the dosage — three hours a day per man, in reliefs, to a beat — which is the part that makes it advice rather than exhortation. Sustainable daily load beats heroic intermittent load, and the mechanism is not that you get stronger but that fear shrinks.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

*Everything that presupposes command.* The contribution system, the legion, the twelve fortified posts on the Vistula, the five-year conscription law — all of it requires an army and a sovereign's authority. There is no move in this book for a man who has no one to command. His advice on organisation is advice on how to hold an organisation you already have.

*The coercion.* His method for making war pay is a disciplined machine for extracting money from civilians under threat of burning their houses, and he is proud of how cheaply it works. There is no business or personal analogue that keeps the mechanism and drops the violence; attempts to make one produce the manipulative self-help version of de Saxe, which is both dishonest about the source and legally dangerous. Take the accounting insight — that an operation which funds itself from its environment can be sustained indefinitely and one that draws on a fixed reserve cannot — and leave the method where he left it.

*The pursuit-to-destruction doctrine.* It is sound as military doctrine and close to worthless as life advice. Its premise is a terminal contest with an enemy you will not meet again. Almost nothing a man does in a career is terminal, and the people he defeats are the people he will need. De Saxe's own strongest argument here — that generals fail to finish because they do not want the war to end — is worth keeping as a diagnostic about mixed motives, and the tactics are worth discarding.

*The born-general fatalism.* "Application will refine the ideas, but can never give a soul, for that is the work of nature." Taken as life advice this is an invitation to stop, and it is also the one claim in the book he is least entitled to make: he never trained anyone, and the argument is an analogy to painting and music, not evidence. Note also that it is self-serving, from a man who had by his own account no system, wrote his book in thirteen nights, and attributed his art to nature.

*The technological specifics, and the man's judgment about his own inventions.* He was certain about the amusette, the half-pike, the legion, the helmet and the wig; none was adopted; and the thing that did survive him was the drumbeat, which was not his invention but his recovery of someone else's. That is the most useful unflattering fact in this file. A talented man's confidence is not correlated with which of his ideas will last. He was right about cadence and redoubts and human instability, and wrong about nearly every piece of hardware he was most excited by, and he could not tell the difference from the inside. Neither can you. Which argues for shipping many cheap things and letting the world select, rather than betting on the one you are most certain about.

*Finally, the register.* This is a brilliant man's unrevised notebook, written feverishly in a fortnight, published by other hands after his death, containing dating errors he never caught and an appended essay his own editor apologised for. It is worth reading precisely because it is that, and it should be quoted as that. Anyone who cites the Reveries as a finished system is citing a book that does not exist — and, if they are quoting the English, is citing a text from which the author's denial that he was building a system was deleted before it reached them.
