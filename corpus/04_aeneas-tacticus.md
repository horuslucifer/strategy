# 4. Aeneas Tacticus — Poliorketika (How to Survive Under Siege)

## 1. Identity

A Greek prose handbook of forty short chapters on how a city holds itself together while an enemy is outside the walls. It is the earliest surviving Greek military manual and the earliest surviving technical prose treatise on war in any European language. The manuscript tradition rests on one codex: Laurentianus Graecus LV 4 (called M), in Florence, which preserves a Byzantine corpus of Greek military writers; the three Paris copies (A, Parisinus Gr. 2435; B, Parisinus Gr. 2522; C, Parisinus Gr. 2443) all descend from it. A and B are cited only where M's text has since been lost to damp or lacuna; C, descended from B, the Loeb editors call worthless except where its copyist's conjectural corrections happen to be good emendations.

The book is a fragment of a programme. Aeneas cross-references at least five other books of his own that he has already written or is planning — on Military Preparations (7.4), on Finance (14.2), on Encampments (21.2), on Plots (11.2), and *Akousmata*, "Admonitions" (38.5) — and the manuscript breaks off mid-sentence into the opening of a work on naval tactics. Everything he defers to those books is lost. This is the single most important fact about the text and the blog omits it: when Aeneas writes "I have explained that elsewhere," the explanation is gone, and what survives is the residue of a much larger system.

**Date.** The historical examples cluster between 400 and 360 BC and stop with Charidemus's capture of Ilium in 360 (24.3-14). The book must precede 346, because at 31.24 it speaks of the Locrian maidens as still being sent to Ilium, a custom Timaeus says ended in that year. The Loeb editors narrow further to 357-356 on the strength of the specimen cipher message at 31.31, which the Loeb prints as two lines, "Dionysius docked" and "Let Heracleides come" (M reads Διονύσιος καλός· Ἡρακλείδας ἡκέτω, 'Dionysius is fair; let Heracleides come'; κόλος, 'docked,' is Hermann Schöne's emendation), and which they read as a single message belonging to Dion's war against Dionysius II of Syracuse in 357. Whitehead argues for after 360 and before 355. Everett Wheeler prefers the cautious outer bracket, 360-346. The safe statement is: composed between 360 and 346, most probably in the 350s.

**Translation used.** The Loeb Classical Library edition of 1928, translated collectively by the Faculty Greek Club of the University of Illinois (the individual translators are named in the preface; the revision was by W. A. Oldfather, A. S. Pease, C. M. Moss and H. V. Canter), text based on R. Schöne's recension (*Aeneae Tactici de Obsidione toleranda Commentarius*, Leipzig 1911), which the Loeb introduction calls the basis of its text. (Hermann Schöne is a different scholar, cited in the introduction only for the emendation κόλος at 31.31.) Public domain, and complete — chapters I through XL plus the editorial introduction and notes — as transcribed at LacusCurtius, which I downloaded in full and cached locally. One bibliographic point is unresolved: LacusCurtius labels its source the 1928 edition, and Diepenbroek's bibliography gives "Oldfather, W. A. (1928)… Vol. 156 Loeb Classical Library"; but Wheeler's review cites "the Loeb (1923)" and Diepenbroek's own footnote elsewhere cites "Oldfather 1923." LCL 156 is the volume in both cases; I could not open a copy to settle which printing year belongs on the title page, so "Loeb 1928" here means the printing LacusCurtius transcribes. David Whitehead's *Aineias the Tactician: How to Survive Under Siege* (Clarendon Ancient History Series, Oxford 1990; reissued Bristol Classical Press 2002) is the modern standard and I have used the reviews of it rather than the book, which I could not open; Hunter and Handford's Oxford text and commentary (1927) and Bettalli's Italian commentary (1990) are the other two.

**Corrections to the blog's commentary on this entry.**

- The blog spells the title "Paliorketika" in every instance. It is *Poliorketika*, Πολιορκητικά, from πολιορκέω, to besiege. The blog also spells the author "Aeneus" in its citation and the scholar Kai Brodersen as "Broderson."
- The blog says: "Aeneas is the name of the author of the Paliorketika, given as an educated guess by historians." That is false. The name comes from the manuscript itself. M's subscription reads Αἰνείου πολιορκητικά, "Aeneas on Siege Operations," and three independent ancient writers name an Aeneas as a military author: Aelian (*Tactica* 1.2, 3.4), who says Cineas the Thessalian epitomised his manuals and quotes his definition of tactics; Polybius (10.44), who attacks his fire-signalling system by name; and Johannes Lydus (*De magistratibus* 1.47). The educated guess is not the name. The educated guess is the *identification* of that Aeneas with Aeneas of Stymphalus, the Arcadian general who expelled Euphron of Sicyon in 367 (Xenophon, *Hellenica* 7.3.1) — Casaubon's suggestion, elaborated by Arnold Hug, widely but not universally accepted, and still called controversial in the 2017 Brill Companion.
- The blog says: "Historical editors of the Paliorketika added the name Tacticus to Aeneas to distinguish him from other Greek Aeneas and imply the author as a military tactician." Half right, and the wrong half is the interesting one. The epithet is Casaubon's, from the editio princeps of 1609. Two qualifications the blog misses. First, M's *superscription* attributes the work to Aelian and calls it a τακτικὸν ὑπόμνημα, a "tactical treatise" — a heading the Loeb editors argue is a misapplied subscription to Aelian's own work, which precedes this one in the codex. Whether Casaubon took his epithet from that heading the Loeb does not say, so the causal chain from scribal error to surname is inference, mine and theirs, not attested. Second, Wheeler's review notes that *Tacticus* "is not devoid of classical attestation (Veg. 3 praef. 2)," so the label is not purely a modern accident. The modern convention, which Whitehead argues for, is to write **Aineias** — and Wheeler records that the confusion being avoided is with Virgil's Aeneas, not with other Greek generals (adding, drily, "Who would make such a gaffe?").
- The blog says: "Very little is written in the Paliorketika to confirm the assumed author of the writing." Misleading. There is internal evidence, and it is why the attribution holds: the string of self-citations to his other books; the geography of his examples, confined almost entirely to the Peloponnese and the western coast of Asia Minor, which fits a Peloponnesian who served as a mercenary commander in the Aegean; and at 31.18, the sample word chosen to demonstrate the knucklebone cipher, which the manuscript spells out as far as αινε and which Haase emended to Αἰνείαν — the author writing his own name as the test case. T. Hudson Williams (*American Journal of Philology* 25.4, 1904, 390-405) pronounced this "not proven," and the Loeb editors answer that he demands a completeness of proof rarely available in philology.
- The blog's content summary, quoted from a course lecture, is accurate as far as it goes but misses the largest subject in the book. More than half the manual is about treachery and revolution inside the walls, not about the enemy outside them. The Loeb introduction says so flatly, and Lateiner's review reports Whitehead's calculation (Whitehead 1990, pp. 18 and 193) that only about fifteen per cent of the text strictly applies to siege warfare.
- The blog gives no date evidence, names no translation, and does not mention that this is one surviving piece of a lost series.

One more correction, to the framing this project inherited rather than to the blog: the title *Poliorketika* is almost certainly not the author's. πολιορκέω means to conduct a siege, and this book contains almost no advice on how to besiege anyone. The Loeb editors argue that a separate book on siege *operations* preceded it and is lost, and that the surviving work is its defensive counterpart.

## 2. The world the text assumes

A small independent Greek city-state of the middle fourth century, walled, with a citizen body under arms whose size Aeneas pointedly refuses to fix, a magistracy, a market-place, a theatre, a town hall, a harbour, and farmland outside the gates that the citizens personally own and will personally run out to save.

That world has four features that determine what transfers.

**The city is not one thing.** Aeneas assumes as a matter of course that the population is split — rich against poor, oligarchs against democrats — and that a substantial fraction of the citizens would prefer the enemy to win, because a change of regime suits them. His standing question, put in my words rather than his, is not how do we resist but who inside will open the gate. The Loeb introduction sets this against Plato's description of the oligarchic state as two cities on one spot, always conspiring against one another. Aeneas never argues the politics. He treats the split as weather.

**Debt is a political force, not a private misfortune.** Indebted men are assumed to be for sale. This is stated twice, from opposite directions: as a disqualification (5.1-2) and as a lever (14.1). Fourth-century Greek stasis really did run on debt cancellation and land redistribution, and Aeneas budgets for it.

**Walls are hard to take by assault and easy to take by arrangement.** Siege engineering existed but was slow and rare. In practice cities fell because somebody inside had a reason. Hence the extraordinary proportion of the book given to gates, bolt-pins, gatekeepers, watchword discipline, smuggled arms, and letters — and hence the fact that the chapters on meeting the actual assault — 32-40 in the Loeb editors' own division of the book — come last and run short.

**Force is rented.** Mercenaries are normal and the citizens who hire them may end up owned by them. Aeneas's Chalcedon and Heracleia Pontica examples are both about garrisons that became the real government.

Two further background conditions matter. Literacy is uneven, so a cipher that looks naive to us could defeat a gate guard. And there is no standing bureaucracy: the general locks the gate with his own hands because there is no institution to do it for him.

## 3. Core doctrine

**1. The defender of his own ground is not playing the same game as the expeditionary soldier.**
Preface 1-3. Reasoning: a man who loses a campaign abroad still has a city to return to; a man who loses at home has nothing left, and a successful defence deters the next attempt while a poor showing invites it. Limit: Aeneas draws the consequence as an obligation to exhaust every preparation, not as a promise of victory — he adds at once that survivors have restored ruined cities before.

**2. Organise from the ground and the men you actually have, and give the picked reserve to a commander who would personally be ruined by a change of government.**
1.1-9. Reasoning: troops fighting outside the walls need a formation adapted to the line of march; troops holding walls and watching citizens need a distribution adapted to the interior of the town. The mobile companies must be "loyal and satisfied with the existing order," acting "like a fortress against the revolutionary designs of the other party," and their commander must be not merely prudent and vigorous but exposed. Limit: he gives categories and no numbers, referring the whole disposition to "the size of the state and the topography of the town" (1.1) — and the test of loyalty he applies is self-interest, not virtue.

**3. Deny the open spaces inside the city — and then test the rule against itself.**
2.1-8. Reasoning: open ground is what a rising needs, so ditch it and no guard is required; the Spartans at Thebes and the Plataeans barricading with ox-less wagons are his proofs. Limit, and the most intellectually serious sentence in the book: if there is only one open space and the conspirators take it first, you have handed them the decision, so two or three is safer. He then generalises — "in all other decisions one should consider the inherent objections to the prescribed rules."

**4. Build the warning system before you need it: scouts in threes, trained, and recognition signals at both ends of every movement.**
4.1-12, 6.1-7. Reasoning: at Chalcis men in the night market-place "aligned themselves with their enemies as though they were their friends, each thinking that he was late in coming up," and the city was in enemy hands "for some time before the citizens knew what was happening" (4.3-4). So any force sent out carries day and night signals, and watchers are posted to read them at distance. Scouts must number at least three per post and be experienced, because an untrained observer cannot tell a deliberate enemy movement from an accident (6.1-3). Limit: signals must be of a kind "that cannot be misunderstood" (4.12). The watchword chapter makes the same point about ambiguity — do not issue a word to mixed forces when "one concept may have two different names, as for example, Dioscuri and Tyndaridae" (24.1). Note what the Ilium episode at 24.3-14 actually shows, because it is easy to invert: the mismatch between Charidemus's Dioscuri and Athenodorus's Tyndaridae is what *exposed* the infiltrators; Aeneas's own moral at 24.14-15 is that watchwords should be easy to remember and fitted to the operation. Scouts also carry a different watchword from the city's, so capture costs only the man (6.7).

**5. Select men by what they lose if you fall, and place them where the loss is visible.**
5.1-2, 22.15, 38.5. Reasoning: gatekeepers must be solvent, with a wife and children in the city, because poverty or "the pressure of some agreement" makes a man persuadable or an initiator; Leuco of Bosporus discharged guards who ran into debt at dice. By the same logic the most exposed stretch of wall goes to the wealthiest and most respected citizens, whose interest is not to turn aside to pleasures, and when correction is needed the example is made of them rather than of the ranks. Limit: he offers none for the debt rule, which is a judgement about his world rather than a law.

**6. Run the interior of the city as a control system, and pay cash for information.**
10.3-15. Reasoning: proclamations are issued "to frighten and deter conspirators." The measures are specific — crops and people brought inside; no private gatherings day or night; no dining in common except weddings and funerals, with notice; no sacrifice without a magistrate present; outgoing and incoming letters brought to censors before being sent or delivered (10.6); foreigners registered, strangers disarmed on arrival and lodged only by permission, inns locked from outside at night; no passage by ship without a passport; and a standing reward for informers displayed publicly in the market-place so that men will more readily come forward. Limit: Aeneas knows what open distrust costs — at 22.16-17 he has to invent a flattering way of removing suspected guards from duty, because visible suspicion is itself destabilising.

**7. Neutralise a faction by administration, not confrontation: send its leaders away on plausible errands, and redraw the units it lives inside.**
10.20-22, 11.7-11. Reasoning: those out of sympathy with the regime, especially potential leaders, go away "on a plausible pretext, as ambassadors or on other public business" — Dionysius I removed his popular brother Leptines by sending him to reorganise the garrison at Himera and then telling him to stay, because open expulsion would have triggered the revolution it was meant to prevent. The same logic at scale: the Argive popular leader ordered every citizen to stand to arms that night with his own tribe, on pain of being treated as a traitor for appearing elsewhere, so that the rich were dispersed as small minorities among their fellow tribesmen; Heracleia Pontica achieved it by replacing three tribes and four "hundreds" with sixty. Limit: both must be done before the rising and disguised as routine administration.

**8. Distrust the man who is most eager to arrange things.**
11.3-5. Reasoning: at Chios a magistrate in on the betrayal persuaded his colleagues to haul the harbour boom ashore for caulking, sell old rigging, repair roofs — so ladders would be lying about — and to pay off most of the city guard as an economy. Every proposal was plausible; every one served the plot. Hence "one must always keep an eye on those who are too eager to effect matters of this kind." Limit: none offered, and the rule obviously catches honest reformers too.

**9. Never let the hired force outnumber the force that hires it; if you must hire, distribute the cost onto the wealthy.**
12.1-5, 13.1-4. Reasoning: allied and mercenary contingents are quartered separately, and the citizens under arms must always surpass them "in number and power" (12.2). The Chalcedonians under siege found their Cyzicene garrison refusing any measure that did not suit Cyzicus, and it was "much more terrible to the Chalcedonians than was the besieging enemy"; Heracleia hired more than it should, destroyed its internal opposition with them, and ended subject to the man who brought them in. Where hiring is unavoidable: assess each rich citizen for one, two or three men by means, brigade them under citizen captains, board them in their employers' houses, pay them partly privately and partly from public funds, and reimburse the employers later net of tax. Limit: the ratio is the whole of the rule and he gives no number; the financing design exists to make the mercenaries dependent on scattered households rather than on one paymaster.

**10. Buy the loyalty of the mass before the siege, chiefly by writing down debt.**
14.1-2. Reasoning: "it is of primary importance to win over the mass of the citizens to a spirit of loyalty," by reducing or cancelling interest for debtors and, in real danger, part or all of the principal, and by provisioning the destitute — "for such men as these are the most formidable of adversaries." Limit: how to do it "fairly and without offence to the wealthy," and out of what revenues, he refers to the lost book on Finance. The hardest part of the doctrine is the missing part.

**11. Answer a raid in formed bodies and late: never piecemeal, and not until the raiders have scattered and gorged.**
15.1-10, 16.4-12. Reasoning: men who run out individually to save their own farms destroy themselves through indiscipline, so muster a company or two at the gate, appoint a leader, dispatch, then the next, so that divisions stay within supporting distance. The Abderites, elated by one victory, rushed out piecemeal against the Triballi a second time and, each wave not knowing the last had died, emptied the city of men. Then the timing: a competent invader advances in order expecting a counter-attack, with ambushes set for a disorderly rescue, so do not disturb him. Let him grow contemptuous, scatter, eat and drink, and stop obeying his officers. Then cut the retreat with cavalry, ambush with picked men, and engage — because "it is sometimes to your interest to give the enemy rein, and to allow him to lay waste as much of the land as he wishes." Limit: 16.9 is explicit that this only works if the rendezvous and the force are prepared in advance; without them, conceding ground is simply loss.

**12. Fight where you know the ground, and choose the place before the fight.**
16.7, 16.18-20. Reasoning: "Attack the enemy where you are not unwilling to do battle, and where you will not be at a disadvantage in the fight." Previous knowledge of the country lets you lead the enemy into ground you have chosen — for defence, pursuit, flight or withdrawal — and tells you where your provisions are, "whereas the enemy will be unacquainted, ignorant, and embarrassed in all these particulars." He rates the asymmetry as large as if one side fought by day and the other by night. Limit: it holds only on your own terrain, and he says so. This is the defender's structural advantage and it does not travel.

**13. The commander locks the gate with his own hands.**
18.1-22, 20.1-5. Reasoning: several pages catalogue ways a gatekeeper has defeated a bolt-pin — sand poured into the socket, a filed groove and a linen thread, a fine-mesh net, pincers, a clay cast taken through a cloth, the bar turned so the pin cannot drop, a measured duplicate made by a blacksmith who was given the parts separately so he could not guess their purpose. The single countermeasure Aeneas repeats is procedural: the officer "should lock the gate himself, and should not give the bolt‑pin to anyone else," before dinner, personally. Mechanical measures follow — iron strips along the bar against sawing, three dissimilar pins held by different generals, custody assigned daily by lot. Limit: the Apollonia Pontica and Aegina solution of deliberately noisy fastenings, audible across the whole city, is the honest admission that the procedure will fail sometimes and you want witnesses when it does.

**14. Make every watch short, crowded, and unpredictable to the man standing it.**
22.4-10. Reasoning: short watches deny a traitor the uninterrupted time he needs and deny sleep its chance; many men on watch together mean that a rumour of anything being arranged is more likely to leak. Then the harder rule: no guard should know in advance the size of his watch or his position on the wall, the same officers should not always command the same men, the day watch should not be used at night, and "as frequently as possible all the regulations concerning the watching of the citizens should be changed." Limit: it costs sleep and organisation, so he scales it — half the army enrolled for guard or patrol in times of less imminent peril, the smallest possible number in peace (22.26).

**15. Calibrate discipline to morale, and give panic a protocol.**
26.7-11, 27.1-14, 38.4. Reasoning: an army that has lost a battle or been deserted by its allies needs frequent rounds but not zealous ones — the patrol should not be eager to catch sentries asleep, because a man is discouraged when he is found behaving badly and the object at that moment is recovery; the patrol should make noise from a distance so the sleeper can wake and answer. In the opposite mood, inspect hard. Always: "The general must never make his round at a fixed hour, but must constantly shift it." Panic gets the same procedural treatment (27.2-4) — a previously arranged signal-fire in a place conspicuous to the whole town, and a standing order that men in a panic hold their places and shout "Paean," or "say that it is a mere panic," each passing the word to his neighbour, so that "wherever in the army they do not answer the paean, it will be known that there the terror prevails." Limit: all of it is pre-agreed and mood-dependent; improvised at the moment, it fails. Euphratas, the Laconian governor in Thrace, who stopped panics by ordering men to sit up in their beds under arms and to treat as an enemy anyone seen standing — and who made the order credible when, in one panic, a respectable soldier was wounded and a baser one killed (27.7-10) — shows what the alternative costs.

**16. Watch the occasion and inspect the cargo. Cities fall on festival days, through the gate, inside the routine delivery.**
17.1-6, 28.1-7, 29.1-12, 30.1-2. Reasoning: at Argos conspirators joined an armed procession to a temple outside the walls, set their weapons down with everyone else except a few, stood beside the magistrates at prayer each with his dagger and his man, and struck; Chios prevented this by occupying the roads to the market-place with troops before the procession began. The same channel at the gate: wine-wagons stopped in the gateway took Clazomenae at the quietest hour of the day; brushwood wagons parked by the gates of Parium were to burn them and draw the citizens. And the same channel in freight: armour through customs in merchants' chests sealed as clothing, spears in half-woven sailcloth, shields in baskets of chaff and wool, daggers in jars of wheat and figs and pushed down the stems of ripe gourds, the leader of the plot himself inside a load of faggots — and, when shields could not be imported at all, osier and basket-weavers who wove armour at night. Hence: close all gates but one, put a wicket in it so men pass singly, open late and only after the ground outside is reconnoitred, board incoming ships personally, and do not leave arms displayed for sale overnight. Limit: he states it himself at 31.24 — Ilium, vigilant for generations, still cannot keep the Locrian maidens out. Inspection lowers the rate; it does not close the channel.

**17. Secret communication is a prearrangement between two parties, and the carrier should not be able to read it.**
31.1-35. Reasoning: "there are all sorts of ways of sending them, but a private arrangement must be previously made between the sender and the receiver." Then about twenty methods — dotted letters in an innocuous book, a tin sheet sewn into a sandal sole and removed while the messenger sleeps, lead rolled as an earring, writing on a bladder inside an oil flask, ink under wax, ink under whitewash revealed by water, a votive tablet hung in a shrine, the knucklebone and the disc threaded through lettered holes, papyrus under a tunic shoulder, arrows shot to an agreed spot, a dog carrying a strap, vowels replaced by dots. Limit: he grades them honestly. The unwritten method is "The most secret method of all for sending messages, but the most difficult" (31.16), and he immediately supplies two simpler substitutes (31.20-22) — the author testing his own invention and finding it laborious.

**18. Handle intelligence at the right speed: let a denounced plot run one step further, and open your letters the moment they arrive.**
31.9-9b, 31.33-34. Reasoning: a courier carrying letters to traitors inside a besieged city took them to the commander instead, and the commander did not seize them — he ordered delivery as addressed and the return of the replies, then showed the conspirators their own seals and opened the answers in front of them. His analysis is explicit: had he taken the incoming letters, the men could have claimed a frame-up; the replies "proved the case incontestably." The opposite error is delay in the other direction — Astyanax of Lampsacus set aside a letter containing the evidence of the plot against him and "was killed while still holding the letter in his fingers"; the Cadmea at Thebes fell the same way, and something similar happened at Mytilene. Limit: the first half requires a genuine informer and an affordable delay; the second has no limit and three corpses.

**19. Keep a reserve moving; it frightens more than the line does.**
38.1-3. Reasoning: divide the defenders into three — fighting, resting, preparing — so the wall always has fresh men on it, and keep a substantial body of picked troops circulating with the general to reinforce whichever section is hardest pressed, "for the enemy fear the reserves more than the force already on duty before them." Limit: tie the dogs up while this is happening, or the noise of armed men running through the town will set them loose among your own.

**20. When you are too few, look like more — and understand exactly what the appearance buys.**
40.1-7. Reasoning: build the assailable stretches of wall so high that anyone who gets over cannot get down; Dionysius held a captured city he lacked the men for by marrying the leading citizens' women to their own slaves, on the theory that the slaves would then be reliably hostile to their former masters; Sinope, short of men against Datamas, armed its fittest women with cooking-bronze for shields and helmets and paraded them where the enemy would see; patrols can walk two abreast with spears on alternate shoulders to look like four. Limit, and Aeneas gives it in one clause: the women were forbidden to throw anything, "for even a long way of a woman betrays her sex when she tries to throw" (40.5 — the LacusCurtius transcription reads "a long way of," evidently for "a long way off"), and deserters had to be prevented from disclosing the trick. The display holds up to the moment of contact and not one second past it.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

The text addresses structural weakness directly and material poverty not at all. That distinction has to be held.

Aeneas's reader is the weaker party by definition: he is besieged, he cannot meet the enemy in the field, and the entire book is written from that position. The opening paragraph makes the asymmetry the premise — the attacker risks a campaign, the defender risks everything he has. Everything that follows is the tactics of the side that cannot afford a decisive engagement: don't sally when they want you to (16.4-10); let them take the countryside and punish them when they are drunk and loaded (16.5-8); use ground they don't know (16.19-20); appear stronger than you are (40.4-7); make the enemy's cost of entry procedural rather than military. This is a real doctrine of asymmetry and it is the most transferable thing in the book.

But the city he writes for is not poor. It has walls, magistrates, a treasury that can pay informers' bounties and subsidise grain importers, and wealthy citizens who can be assessed for mercenaries. His answer to scarcity of manpower is 40.1-7, and his answers to scarcity of money are all in the book on Finance, which is lost. When he does address the poor directly, it is as a threat to be neutralised: debtors are "the most formidable of adversaries" (14.1) and are handled by writing down what they owe; the destitute are provisioned so they do not become a faction; the poor gatekeeper is disqualified from his post because poverty makes him purchasable (5.1). The man with nothing appears in this book, consistently, as the material a conspiracy is made of.

**This is direct statement, not inference.** Aeneas says it. The inference — mine, and I mark it as mine — is the inversion: precisely because he is writing the manual for keeping such men out, he has produced the most detailed surviving account of how they get in. Chapters 11, 17, 18, 28, 29 and 31 are, read backwards, a catalogue of the openings available to someone with no force, no standing and no legitimacy: the indebted functionary, the plausible administrative proposal, the crowd at a festival, the routine delivery nobody inspects, the unwatched hour of the day. Aeneas is explicit at 31.24 that a determined few can defeat indefinite vigilance. That is the small actor's warrant, stated by the man trying to stop him.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Total, and prior. The preface's demand is that those defending everything they have "must fail in no preparation and no effort, but must take thought for many and varied activities, so that a failure may at least not seem due to their own fault." Note the formulation: preparation does not guarantee survival, it guarantees that the failure was not yours. Most of the concrete preparation — obstructing the country, spoiling water, hiding stores — he refers to the lost book (8.1-5, 21.1).

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Systematic. Three trained scouts per post, chosen for the ability to read an enemy's movements rather than merely see them (6.1-3); relays and mounted couriers; scouts sent out before dawn so the enemy's watchers do not see them take post; scouts denied the city's own watchword so capture costs only the man. Internally, the intelligence system is the informer bounty (10.15), the letter censorship (10.6), and at 11.7 the Argive democrat who publicly abused two men of the opposing party while privately taking their reports — the earliest description in Greek of running an agent inside a faction.

**Timing.** He is precise about it. Strike the raiders after they have scattered and eaten, and specifically at the evening meal when they are back inside their own border and feel safe (16.12). Expect the coup during festivals, processions, funerals and public works (17.1). Open the gates late in the morning, after the ground outside has been reconnoitred (28.4). Vary the general's round so it is never at a fixed hour (26.11). The underlying principle is that the predictable hour is the vulnerable hour, applied to both sides.

**Terrain and position.** Two registers. Outside: previous knowledge of the country is worth as much as fighting by day against an enemy fighting by night (16.19-20); seize the strategic approaches, or if there are none, points of support near the city from which you can fight and withdraw (16.16-18). Inside: the city itself is terrain to be engineered — ditch the open spaces (2.1), keep the ramps to the wall closed so nobody can seize a stretch of it and nobody who scales it can get down (22.19), dig a concealed trench inside the gate with passages your own men know (39.1-2).

**Deception and information.** The largest single subject after internal security. Chapter 31 is the fullest collection of concealed-communication methods surviving from antiquity. Beyond it: the false wall behind a lowered sail, shown and hidden repeatedly until the enemy stops looking (23.4-5); the feigned mobilisation at 9.1-3, where you assemble your own men and announce an operation purely so the news reaches the enemy camp and diverts them; the counter-warning at 23.6-11 that a fire in the dockyard or a report of raiders may be staged precisely to draw the leader and a crowd out of the city.

**Alliances.** Uniformly hostile, and this is one of his firmest positions. Allied contingents are never to be stationed together (12.1). The citizen body under arms must always surpass the mercenary force "in number and power" (12.2). The Chalcedon case is his exhibit: a garrison sent by an ally that would consent to nothing unless it suited the ally, so that the protection inside the walls was worse than the enemy outside. Heracleia is the terminal case: hire too many and you end up owned. The rule is that dependence on a stronger partner converts into control.

**Logistics and economy.** Present, and unusually concrete for a Greek military writer. Guaranteed profit margins for importers of grain and oil in proportion to the quantity imported, a crown of honour, and a subsidy for hauling the ship up and down (10.12) — which the Loeb note calls the earliest instance of profit-fixing as a war measure. Mercenary maintenance assessed on the wealthy and reimbursed net of tax (13.1-4). Debt relief as a defence expenditure (14.1). Livestock and slaves placed with neighbouring states at public expense where the owner has no friends to take them (10.1-2). The book that would have unified all this is lost.

**Morale and discipline.** Handled as a variable, not a constant. Debt relief and provisioning buy loyalty before the siege (14.1). During it: don't reprimand common soldiers in anger (38.4); don't inspect a beaten army zealously (26.8); make the examples out of the rich (38.5); give panic a pre-agreed protocol so that fear has somewhere to go (27.2-3). The general's own conduct is a morale instrument — 9.1-3 has him stage an operation partly to "inspire your friends with courage by your initiative and fearlessness."

**The decisive point.** In defence, the point is wherever the wall is hardest pressed, and the doctrine is the circulating reserve that goes there with the general (38.2). He has no theory of the decisive point in the abstract; he has a procedure for reaching it.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Largely silent. The nearest thing is 16.9-11, where the pursuit is deliberately not pressed along the obvious road — a few men make a show of chasing and are told not to catch anyone, while the real force goes round by other roads to lie in ambush on the enemy's own border. That is a warning against exploiting a success in the direction the enemy expects, which is adjacent to the idea, not the idea.

**When not to fight.** Explicit and repeated. Do not sally in force at night on the strength of an alarm, because the alarm may be the plot (23.6-11). Do not send relief immediately or piecemeal (15.2, 16.2). Do not attack an invader while he is still in order; wait until he has scattered (16.5-9). The Abderites at 15.8-10 are the standing example of what fighting at the wrong moment costs.

**Defence versus offence.** The book is defence, by design; the offensive treatment was a separate work and is lost. He signals the boundary himself — the note on sawing through a bar with oil and a sponge (19.1) is one of the very few pieces of advice in the book addressed to an attacker, and he cuts it short: "One might write down many other similar suggestions, but we may let them pass." The preface's contrast between the expeditionary and the home-defending soldier is the frame for the whole distinction.

**The character required of the commander.** Assembled from pieces rather than argued. Prudent and vigorous, and personally exposed to ruin if the regime falls (1.7). Locks the gate with his own hands and trusts nobody else with the pin (20.1). Unpredictable in his rounds (26.11). Capable of restraint — not eager to catch men failing when they are already beaten (26.8), not angry with common soldiers (38.4). Suspicious as a working method: "one may not be so simple as to take anything for granted" (28.7). And, at 2.8, capable of turning his own rules over to look for the objection to them.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The organisation is genuinely bad, and this is the standing scholarly complaint.** Donald Lateiner, reviewing Whitehead in *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* 1991.06.22, writes that "The writer bounces from preparing defensive positions to thwarting treachery" and that the organisation "does not well correspond to a logical progression of topics, chronology, level of threat, or any other principle." Two figures often credited to Lateiner are in fact Whitehead's, which Lateiner quotes with page references: that "only 15% of what we have strictly applies to siege warfare (pp. 18, 193)," and that many examples and prescriptions are "most frustratingly vague" (p. 105). Lateiner's own verdicts are that "Whitehead has read nearly everything and digested it," that the problems "are often unresolvable because of Aineias's opacity, studious generality, and outrageously rampant vagueness," and, drily, that "Whitehead—whoever his audience—has done more for Aineias than the latter deserved." 

**The nineteenth century nearly rewrote the text.** Because Aeneas's Greek is not clean Attic — he is now understood as one of the earliest surviving documents of the developing koine — editors treated its irregularities as corruption. Rudolph Hercher discarded about one twelfth of the book as interpolation; Arnold Hug about one quarter; Adolph Lange, defending the text against most charges, still rejected about one tenth; Adolf Kirchhoff proposed wholesale displacement of chapters. The Loeb editors call this "the process of emendation to the point of absurdity" and reject Kirchhoff outright. Anyone reading a nineteenth-century edition is reading a book substantially shorter than the one that survived.

**The authorship is not settled and popular summaries overstate both sides.** T. Hudson Williams (*AJP* 25.4, 1904) argued the identification is unproven; Casaubon's identification with Aeneas of Stymphalus, elaborated by Hug, is the majority view but Everett Wheeler, reviewing *Brill's Companion to Aineias Tacticus* (ed. Maria Pretzler and Nick Barley, Brill 2017) in *BMCR* 2018.07.29, calls Robin Lane Fox's reconstruction of Aeneas's career "ingenious but speculative" and says the Stymphalian identification remains controversial. Wheeler's verdict on the Companion as a whole — that it "largely propagates the known" and that interdisciplinary approaches are not a substitute for new evidence — is worth recording, because it means the state of knowledge has not moved much since 1928 on the questions the blog raises.

**Aeneas's historical examples are frequently other authors', and popular retellings credit him with them.** Diepenbroek's list: 31.14 is Herodotus 7.239 (Demaratus's message under the wax); 31.25-27 is Herodotus 8.128 (Timoxenus and Artabazus shooting letters on arrows); 31.28-29 is Herodotus 5.35 (Histiaeus tattooing his slave's head); 37.6 is Herodotus 4.200 (the Barcaean coppersmith finding the mine with a bronze shield); 2.3-6 is Thucydides 2.2-6 (the Plataeans against the Thebans); 27.11 may be Xenophon *Anabasis* 2.2.20. The tattooed-head story in particular is routinely credited to Aeneas in security and cryptography writing, when he is repeating Herodotus 5.35 and the Loeb’s own note 80 says so. He is transmitting Herodotus, not reporting.

**The hydraulic telegraph is routinely located in the wrong book.** Aeneas's fire-signalling system, with synchronised vessels and graduated rods, is known to us from Polybius 10.44, who criticises it. It is not in the surviving treatise. At 7.4 Aeneas explicitly refers the reader to his book on Military Preparations for it — that book is lost; the Loeb prints the Polybius passage separately, as Frag. 3. Any account that locates the fire-signalling system inside the surviving *Poliorketika* has it in the wrong book. (I have not audited which popular sources do this, so I make the claim about the fact, not about a named offender.)

**Calling chapter 31 the earliest treatise on cryptography overstates it.** Martine Diepenbroek, "Hiding Secrets in Greek Siegecraft: Why did Aeneas Tacticus Never Discuss the Spartan *scytale*?", *Ancient History Bulletin* 36.3-4 (2022), 145-165, classifies the twenty-one methods in chapter 31 as fifteen steganographic, two cryptographic, and four combined. Aeneas's interest is overwhelmingly in *hiding the existence* of a message, not in enciphering it. Her further point matters for a well-known argument: because Aeneas does not mention the Spartan *scytale*, several scholars (Kelly, Sheldon, West, and Whitehead 1990, 184) have concluded it cannot have been a cipher device in his day. Diepenbroek argues the silence proves less than that, since Aeneas was not much interested in ciphers at all. Whether or not she is right, the accurate description of chapter 31 is as the founding *steganographic* text, not the founding cryptographic one.

**His own devices are partly impractical and he half-admits it.** Reported at second hand, via Diepenbroek, who cites Hunter and Handford (1927, lxxxii) and Whitehead (1990, 187): both judge the knucklebone-and-thread method troublesome to the point of unusability, because "One would easily end up with a ball of thread whereby it was no longer possible to find the correct holes" — Diepenbroek's own wording for their finding. Aeneas calls it "The most secret method of all for sending messages, but the most difficult" before offering two simpler substitutes. Von Gutschmid ridiculed the vowels-as-dots cipher at 31.31. The Loeb editors' defence is historical rather than technical: in a world of uncommon reading and barely legible handwriting, it would have defeated a gateman.

**The ideological reading, and the way it gets used.** Lateiner reads Aineias as “a ruthless man doing his job to preserve whatever government holds the citadel,” against Whitehead’s reference to “the high ideals of its author,” which Lateiner says “scarcely fits.” He notes the text’s “paramount role of fraud, fifth-columns, arms smuggled in for malcontents,” says that “at times, the techniques of modern totalitarianism seem envisaged,” and that “Macchiavel and Hobbes the theorist come to mind repeatedly.” A. Winterling's study of Aeneas's concept of the polis and theory of stasis — "Polisbegriff und Stasistheorie des Aeneas Tacticus," *Historia* 40.2 (1991), 193-229 — is the standard treatment of the political content. (Wheeler's review, which is where I found it cited, prints the surname as "Wintering"; Diepenbroek's bibliography gives Winterling, A., which I follow.) The relevant warning for this project is that the chapter-10 apparatus — mail censorship, banned assembly, registered foreigners, paid informers, and the removal of hostages' relatives from the city (10.23, where his stated reason is to spare them the sight of their sons killed on the walls) — reads as a set of tips when it is in fact a description of a city in a state of civil emergency, and it has no author's-conscience attached, because Aeneas offers none.

**Misuse in the self-help and business literature: essentially none, and that is worth saying.** Unlike Sun Tzu and Machiavelli, Aeneas has no popular afterlife. He is not quarried for leadership aphorisms, and I found no trade-press equivalent of the Sun Tzu industry under his name. Where he does circulate outside classics is in information-security and OPSEC writing, always for chapter 31 and always with the overstatement noted above. The practical consequence for this book: there is no popular misreading to argue against here, but also no existing bridge, so every translation in section 8 has to be built and shown.

**Finally, a false popular attribution to flag.** No memorable aphorism is securely attributable to Aeneas. The sentence sometimes floated as his, "the city is taken by the man who is already inside it," does not appear in the Loeb text — I grepped for "already inside" and for "is taken by" and found nothing of the kind. It is a modern compression of 11.1-15 and should never be quoted as his words.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Read straight, this is the wrong book for him. Aeneas writes for the party that already holds something and is trying not to lose it. Every instinct in the text is conservative: hold the wall, keep the gate shut, watch your own people, buy off the discontented, preserve the existing order. Lateiner's formulation is that Aineias "just tries to hold things as they are together: status quo qualiscumque nunc et semper might be his motto, as long as the salary gets paid." A man with no money, no network, no position and no reputation has no walls, and if he takes this book at face value he will acquire a defensive temperament while his actual problem is that he has nothing to defend. That is the first thing to say and it disqualifies about half the contents.

What remains is worth having. Seven things carry over, and I have not audited the other twelve texts for this purpose, so I make no claim about rank among them.

**First: its subject is the interior of a weak organisation under pressure.** Aeneas's governing assumption is that the decisive fight is inside, not outside, and that the enemy wins by arrangement rather than by assault. What follows from that, and no more: in his world the internal vector mattered more than the external one, and he therefore spends most of the book on procedures for auditing one's own side. The Chios chapter (11.3-5) is the exact model of the mechanism. Nobody attacked Chios. A magistrate in on the plot made a series of reasonable-sounding proposals — dry the harbour boom, sell old rigging, repair roofs, pay off most of the guard as an economy — every one plausible, every one serving the plot, and the ladders were lying about when they were wanted. Two things transfer as procedure, not as diagnosis: 11.5, keep an eye on whoever is most eager to arrange things; and 2.8, take your own rule and look for the objection to it before acting on it. Whether an individual's lack of progress is mostly internal is a claim about his life that Aeneas cannot supply and I will not assert.

**Second: debt.** Aeneas puts it at the centre from both ends, as a disqualification for office and as an instrument of state. I have not surveyed the other classical military writers on this point, so I claim only that it is unusually prominent here, not that it is unique to him. The indebted gatekeeper is disqualified from his post because debt makes him purchasable (5.1); Leuco fired guards who lost at dice (5.2); and the city's first act of preparation is to write down what the debtors owe, because debtors are "the most formidable of adversaries" (14.1). What transfers is the mechanism, which is about leverage rather than about character: an obligation is a handle, and whoever holds it can move the man who carries it. That is Aeneas's reason for both moves — he bars the indebted from the gate because someone can buy them, and he cancels the citizens' interest because otherwise someone else will. The operational reading is therefore about removing handles, not about predicting bad decisions: the quantity that matters is the gap between income and obligation, and the side of it you control directly is the obligation. That solvency also improves a man's own judgement is plausible and is not in Aeneas; it is my addition and I mark it as such.

**Third: stake determines reliability, and you have none.** Aeneas selects men by exposure, not by character. The reserve commander is chosen because he would be ruined by a change of government (1.7); gatekeepers are chosen because they have wives and children in the city (5.1); the worst post on the wall goes to the richest men because they have the most to lose by neglecting it (22.15). Turned around, the selection rule says what a man with no stake looks like from the other side of the table: on Aeneas's own criteria he is the person the rule excludes — nothing recoverable if he defaults, therefore cheap to buy. That much follows from the text. What does not follow from the text, and is my inference, is the remedy: if exposure is what the rule reads, then exposure is what has to be manufactured and made legible — commitments costly to break, made in public, with a name attached, kept long enough to be checked. Aeneas nowhere advises anyone to acquire a stake; he only advises commanders to select for one.

**Fourth, the inversion — and it is inversion, not doctrine: Aeneas is describing these channels in order to close them.** If you have nothing and intend to change that, you are not the besieged city. You are the party outside, or the party inside with a motive. Aeneas has written down in unusual detail how such people actually got in — and in his examples it is never over the wall. It is the routine delivery nobody opens: the wine-wagons at Clazomenae parked in the gateway at the quietest hour of the day (28.5), the merchants' chests sealed by the customs officers because they contained what looked like clothing (29.4-5), the daggers in the jars of figs. It is the man with an errand that makes sense: Charidemus's slave who went out for plunder at night until the gate opened for him by habit (24.4-8). It is the crowd at the festival (17.2). It is the gatekeeper with a groove filed in the bolt-pin. What follows from the collection is a single claim about attention: entry was obtained through routine traffic rather than through the guarded point, and the routine that let it through was the one long-established enough to be unexamined. Aeneas confirms the general form at 31.24, where Ilium, watchful for generations, still cannot keep the Locrian maidens out; inspection lowers the rate, it does not close the channel. The extension to a man looking for a way into an organisation — that the unexamined, low-status, repetitive work is the corresponding channel — is mine, not his, and it is an analogy rather than a deduction: Aeneas's gate is a place a hostile party exploits, not a place an outsider earns standing.

**Fifth: the arithmetic of the weaker party.** 16.4-12 is the book's clearest statement of asymmetric method. Do not contest the enemy while he is in order and expecting you (16.4-5). Let him take the countryside (16.8). Let him scatter, gorge himself, become "careless and disobedient to their leaders" (16.5). Then strike what is loaded and slow, on ground you know, preferably at the evening meal inside his own border when he feels safe (16.11-12). Three things follow. You concede on purpose the ground you cannot hold rather than losing it while fighting for it. You concede in order to buy the conditions of a strike, and the conditions are stated: 16.7 makes the attack conditional on your supporting force being "ready at the appointed place," and 16.9 says that sending relief out hastily endangers your own unprepared men while the enemy, still in order, gets away unpunished. And you strike the overextended rather than the ordered — the party that has taken on more than it can service. What does not follow from Aeneas is any of the specific commercial targets one is tempted to list here; he is talking about a column loaded with plunder, and the only invariant is that the target is overextended and off guard.

**Sixth: ground.** 16.19-20 says the advantage from previous knowledge of the country is as large as the difference "as if it were the lot of the one to fight by night and the other by day," and Aeneas is precise about what the knowledge buys: choice of where to fight, routes for pursuit, flight and withdrawal, and knowing in advance where the provisions are. Two things transfer without stretching. Local knowledge is a real and quantifiable advantage against a better-resourced party who lacks it. And it does not travel — Aeneas's whole claim is about your own terrain, so leaving it forfeits the advantage. The corollary, that a man with little else should therefore keep his moves inside whatever specific place, trade or milieu he knows in detail, follows from those two; the stronger claim that such knowledge is his only asset is mine and is not in the text.

**Seventh: the unglamorous procedural rules, which transfer intact.**
- Open your letters when they arrive (31.33-34). Astyanax "was killed while still holding the letter in his fingers"; Aeneas adds the Cadmea at Thebes and something similar at Mytilene. The rule as he states it has no qualifying condition: the cost of deferring correspondence is paid in the cases where the correspondence was the warning. The modern form is the unopened notice or the demand left for later because the contents are known to be bad.
- Never make the round at a fixed hour (26.11). Aeneas gives his reason: soldiers who know the hour "watch with especial care during that time," so the fixed inspection stops measuring anything. That reason does carry over to inspecting one's own position on a schedule regular enough to be anticipated. The companion rule at 22.7 — no guard knows his watch, his place or his officers in advance — does not carry over, because its stated purpose is to deny a traitor foreknowledge, and a man auditing himself has no traitor to starve.
- Do not take the alarm at face value (23.6-11). Aeneas's case is a staged robbers' report used to draw the armed citizenry out of the city so mercenaries could be walked in behind them. The transferable part is narrow and exact: the urgent thing that pulls you out of position at short notice is sometimes staged by whoever benefits from your absence.
- Verify by letting it run one more step (31.9-9b). The commander did not seize the incoming letters; had he done so, in the translation’s words, "it would have been possible for the men to deny it and claim that someone was plotting against them." By taking the replies instead "he proved the case incontestably." Aeneas's own conditions travel with the rule: it requires a genuine informer and a delay you can afford.
- Lock the gate yourself (20.1): the general attends to the locking personally, before dinner, "and not carelessly to trust to anyone else." Applied to a single point of failure — one client, one account, one relationship — the rule is that it is not handed off. Note that Aeneas does not rest on the procedure alone: he immediately adds iron strips, three dissimilar pins in three custodies, daily lot (20.2-3), and the deliberately noisy fastenings of Apollonia Pontica and Aegina (20.4-5), which are an admission that the procedure fails sometimes and you want the whole city to hear it when it does.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

The chapter-10 apparatus does not transfer at all. Censoring correspondence, banning private gatherings, registering strangers, paying informers, sending the relatives of hostages out of the city: this is a magistracy's toolkit during a civil emergency, exercised over people who cannot leave. A private individual who applies it to his own relationships gets paranoia and isolation, which is precisely the wrong outcome for someone whose principal deficit is network. The temptation is real, because the chapters are vivid and they flatter suspicion. Resist them.

The mercenary and alliance chapters transfer only in one narrow form. Aeneas's rule is that the hired force must never exceed the force that hires it, and his exhibit is Chalcedon, whose allied garrison became worse than the siege. The usable version keeps his structure and drops the number he never gives: a supplier of force, money or work large enough that you cannot survive refusing it has the same standing over you that the Cyzicene garrison had over Chalcedon, and the standing is invisible until the first refusal. Aeneas states the ratio as a rule and supplies no threshold, so any specific fraction offered here would be invented. What does not transfer is the general hostility to allies, because Aeneas is writing for a city that already has citizens under arms. You have none. Your problem is the opposite of his, and a man with nothing who adopts Aeneas's distrust of outside help will simply stay alone.

The deception material transfers with a hard limit that Aeneas himself supplies. Sinope armed its women with cooking-pots and marched them along the wall, and forbade them to throw anything, because the throw would give them away at any distance (40.5); the patrols walked two abreast to look like four (40.6-7); the false wall was hidden behind a sail (23.4-5). Every one of these buys time and none of them buys capability. Aeneas uses display to defer contact, never to win an engagement, and he takes care that deserters cannot disclose the trick. Translated: presenting yourself as more established than you are is a real instrument and it works exactly until the first test, at which point the gap becomes public and is worse than the original weakness. Use it to buy the interval in which you acquire the actual capability, or do not use it.

And the deepest limit is the one the preface states. Aeneas promises that total preparation ensures only that "a failure may at least not seem due to their own fault." He does not promise the city will hold. That is the honest register of the book and it is the register this chapter should keep: these are the measures that make a position hard to take by arrangement, which in Aeneas’s examples is how cities were taken. They do not make you safe, and Aeneas never says they do.

## 10. Cut on verification

Nothing was cut from section 7: all fifteen quotations were independently re-verified, and every short fragment in the trailing list of section 7 was re-verified, by `grep -F` against `aeneas_tacticus_loeb_FULL.txt` and `aeneas_tacticus_loeb_INTRO.txt`. The following claims elsewhere in the file were cut or corrected because they did not survive checking.

- **Cut: "enough men to fill twenty-four watches"** (section 4, on the resources of Aeneas's city). Not in the text. `grep "twenty-four"` returns two hits in the whole treatise, both in chapter 31 — the twenty-four holes of the astragal (31.17) and the twenty-four characters of the alphabet (31.21). Aeneas's only scaling rule for guard numbers is 22.26 (half the enrolled army in lesser danger, the smallest possible number in peace). Replaced with the resources that are attested.
- **Corrected: a misquotation at section 3, principle 17.** The file read "the most secret of all for sending messages, but the most difficult." The Loeb prints "The most secret method of all for sending messages, but the most difficult" (31.16). `grep -F` on the file's version returned zero hits; on the Loeb's, one.
- **Corrected: an invented quotation at section 3, principle 15.** The panic signal was given as a signal-fire meaning "this is only a panic." Those words are not in the translation. Aeneas's wording at 27.3 is that the men should "stand in their places and shout 'Paean,' or say that it is a mere panic," passing the word along.
- **Corrected: "text based on Hermann Schöne's recension"** (section 1). The Loeb introduction says its text rests on R. Schöne's recension (*Aeneae Tactici de Obsidione toleranda Commentarius*, Leipzig 1911). Hermann Schöne appears in the introduction only as the author of the emendation κόλος at 31.31. Two different scholars.
- **Corrected: "internment of hostages' relatives"** (sections 6 and 8). 10.23 prescribes that the parents and next of kin of hostages *depart from the place* until the siege is over, and gives as the reason that they should not see their own sons brought forward and killed in the assaults. That is removal, not internment, and the stated motive is not surveillance.
- **Corrected: the fifteen-per-cent figure and "most frustratingly vague"** (sections 1 and 6), both previously credited to Lateiner. Lateiner quotes both from Whitehead, with page references (pp. 18 and 193; p. 105).
- **Corrected: "any single source of more than half your income owns you"** (section 8). The fraction is invented. Aeneas states the rule without a number — citizens under arms "surpassing these mercenaries in number and power" (12.2) — and section 3 of this file says so; the two passages contradicted each other. Rewritten without a threshold.
- **Corrected: the Dioscuri/Tyndaridae reading** (section 3, principle 4). The locus 24.13 was right, but the episode was being read backwards. The differing watchwords are what *exposed* Athenodorus's infiltrators; Aeneas's own moral, at 24.14-15, is that watchwords should be memorable and fitted to the operation. The general warning against two names for one concept is at 24.1.
- **Corrected: Euphratas "killing anyone who stood"** (section 3, principle 15). 27.8-9: he ordered that a man seen standing be *treated as an enemy*, and in one panic a respectable soldier was wounded and a baser one killed. The order and the demonstration are distinct.
- **Corrected: "Alexander Winterling"** (section 6). The initial in both Wheeler's footnote and Diepenbroek's bibliography is A.; the forename Alexander is unattested in anything I opened.
- **Corrected: the Paris manuscripts** (section 1). A and B are cited where M's text is lost; C is descended from B and the Loeb calls it worthless except for its copyist's conjectural corrections. The three are not equivalent.
- **Softened: "Tacticus derives from a scribal error"** (section 1). The Loeb shows that the superscription is a misapplied Aelian subscription; it does not say Casaubon drew the epithet from it. Wheeler adds that *Tacticus* has classical attestation at Vegetius 3 praef. 2. Rewritten as inference rather than fact.
- **Softened: the hydraulic-telegraph claim** (section 6). The substantive point survives — the fire-signalling system is not in the surviving treatise, 7.4 refers it to the lost book on Military Preparations, and our knowledge of it is Polybius 10.44. The previous version put the error inside a quotation attributed to unnamed "sources." I did not audit which popular sources make it, so the claim is now made about the fact, not about an offender.
- **Softened: three comparative claims about "the thirteen texts"** (section 8). This file examines one text. The claims that Aeneas is the only book on the list about the interior of a weak organisation, that 16.5-12 is the clearest statement of asymmetry in the thirteen, and that 16.19-20 is the most under-used advice on the list were not verified against the other twelve and have been removed or rephrased.
- **Softened: "a few thousand citizens under arms"** (section 2). Aeneas gives no troop or citizen numbers anywhere in the treatise, referring the disposition instead to "the size of the state and the topography of the town" (1.1). The figure was the extractor's, presented as part of the world the text assumes.
