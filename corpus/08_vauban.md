# 8. Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban — Treatises on the attack and defence of fortified places, with his fortification method and the *Dîme royale*

## 1. Identity

The load-bearing text is the **Traité de l'attaque des places**, with its shorter companion the **Traité de la défense des places**. It is a practical manual for conducting a siege from the moment the siege is decided to the moment the governor signs the capitulation: twenty-five chapters on the attack, closing with two sets of general maxims (twelve on the construction of lines, twenty-nine on the attack itself), then six chapters on the defence. There is no theory of war in it, no philosophy of the state, and almost no history. It is a procedure, written by the man who had run the procedure more times than anyone alive.

It was written at the end of his career — Augoyat's *avertissement* dates it "composé au commencement de la guerre de la succession d'Espagne," i.e. from about 1702–3, and the popular statement of 1704 is inside that window — for the instruction of **Louis, duc de Bourgogne**, the king's grandson and heir-presumptive. Vauban did not intend it for publication. The 1737 printer's preface says so flatly: *"Sa vûë n'étoit point, que ce Traité fût jamais rendu public. Il ne l'avoit composé que pour l'instruction particulière de Monseigneur le Duc de Bourgogne."* Augoyat adds that the dedication itself asked that no copies be taken. Copies were taken anyway, sold at high prices to princes and ambassadors, and one reached the Hague bookseller Pierre de Hondt, who printed the first edition in 1737, thirty years after Vauban's death.

**What survives, and in what shape.** Vauban wrote continuously — a 1669 *Mémoire pour servir d'instruction dans la conduite des sièges* for Louvois, *Le Directeur-Général des fortifications* (The Hague, 1683–85), a *Traité des mines*, *Instructions pour la défense* for particular governors, a *Traité des fortifications de campagne*, the twelve manuscript volumes of *Mes Oisivetés*, and the *Projet d'une dixme royale* (1707). Of the *Oisivetés*, the 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* reports that seven of the twelve manuscript volumes are lost. The surviving corpus is therefore partial, scattered across editors, and — this matters — **contaminated**.

### Corrections to the entry's commentary

**The blog/brief says: "Older English versions of his method exist (*The New Method of Fortification*, 1691/1722, archive.org)." The evidence says those are not Vauban, and citing them as "his method" is the single commonest error about him.** The 1691 London octavo carries his name on the title page, but the body mentions him exactly once, in a construction instruction: "draw the Faces as usually, after Vauban's manner." Its own preface calls it "THis little Tract, which was chiefly de sign'd by the Ingenious Author, for the Use and Benefit of young Gen tlemen … addicted to the Study of this part of the Mathematicks" — that is, a geometry primer with a fortification appendix. The WorldCat record for the edition identifies it as Abel Swall's translation of the *Manière de fortifier de Mr. de Vauban* by the **Chevalier de Cambray** (Amsterdam, 1689), and the 1911 *Britannica* bibliography independently lists "works by Abbé du Fay (Paris, 1681) and Chevalier de Cambray (Amsterdam, 1689), from which came various works in English." Vauban never published a treatise on fortification. He wrote the attack down; other men wrote the defence-works down and sold them under his name.

**The printed treatise contains post-1707 interpolations.** This is a finding, not a received opinion, and it is checkable. The 1737 de Hondt text includes an anecdote about "les Alliez, en 1710" drawing off the garrison of Tournai by threatening Ypres; a sentence citing "le Prince Eugene à attaquer Lille" (1708); and, in the maxims on lines, a reference to the disaster "à Denain" (24 July 1712). Vauban died 30 March 1707. Grepping Augoyat's Burgundy-manuscript edition for "Denain," "Eugène" and the Tournai passage returns nothing; the Amsterdam *Œuvres* has Denain too. The editors improved the marshal. When you quote the 1737 or the *Œuvres*, you are sometimes quoting an anonymous Dutch or German editor of the 1730s. I have avoided every interpolated passage in section 7.

**The "three systems" are posthumous.** The first/second/third-system scheme taught in every fortification textbook was a classification made after his death; the Network of Vauban's Major Sites states plainly that "His fortification system evolved and was classified after his death into three distinct systems." The 1911 *Britannica* records that Vauban's own name for the New Breisach design — the so-called third system — was *système de Landau perfectionné*, i.e. a refinement of a particular job, not a system.

**The parallels: first practised, not necessarily invented, by him.** Vauban's own claim, in his own voice, is modest and precise: the first time these lines were used "ce fut au Siège de Maëstricht, fait en 1673 par le Roi en personne. J'en conduisis les Attaques." He says he conducted the attacks; he does not say he invented the device. The 1911 *Britannica* reports the tradition that the method was "said to have been suggested by the practice of the Turks at Candia in 1668." Treat the invention claim as contested and the *systematisation* claim as safe.

**Dates and numbers.** Baptised 15 May 1633 at Saint-Léger-de-Foucheret (now Saint-Léger-Vauban), Burgundy; orphaned at ten "in very poor circumstances" (EB 1911); joined Condé's household 1650 and served in the Fronde against the king; captured by royal troops at Sainte-Menehould in early 1653 and turned; *ingénieur du roi* May 1655; *maréchal de camp* 1676; *commissaire général des fortifications* 1677; **Marshal of France 14 January 1703**; died in Paris **30 March 1707**. Counts of his sieges differ by what you count: the 1911 *Britannica* says "forty sieges and … more than three hundred combats," English Wikipedia "more than 40 sieges from 1653 to 1697," and the brief's "forty-eight" is within the range of the traditional tallies. Fortifications: EB says "more than 160"; Wikipedia notes estimates from about 60 to over 300 and says modern scholarship favours about 160. Use 160 and say "about."

**The *Dîme royale*.** Published 1707, "instantly suppressed by order of the king" (EB 1911), which dates his death to a few days after the suppression order. The archive.org copy I used is the second edition, printed after his death. It is a tax proposal: abolish the *taille*, the *aides*, the internal customs, the clerical *décimes* and the extraordinary levies, halve the price of salt, and replace the lot with a single proportional tenth on all revenue, paid by everyone.

## 2. The world the text assumes

**Siege is the normal form of war, and battle the exception.** The whole treatise assumes that campaigns are decided by the possession of fortified places on a frontier, that both sides know which places matter, and that a war is a sequence of purchases — each place bought with a known quantity of time, powder and men. Nothing in Vauban is about destroying an enemy army. The object is always a piece of ground with walls on it.

**The besieger is the stronger party and chooses.** Part I is written from inside a royal army with a treasury behind it. Chapter I opens: *"La Résolution des Sièges est une affaire de Cabinet. Elle est une suite naturelle de la supériorité que l'on croit avoir sur ses Ennemis."* A siege is what superiority does. Everything about the attack presupposes that you already have more than he does.

**Labour is requisitioned, not hired.** For a place with a four-to-five-league circumvallation he commands "au moins 15 à 18000 Païsans, & 2. ou 3000. Chariots, même 4000." They are fed double bread and nothing more, paid by their own villages under the corvée, and deserters are to be punished severely "autrement plus de la moitié vous abandonnera dès les premiers jours." He does think this is bad practice, and proposes six sols a day for the ones retained — but the assumption is a state that can conscript a county's peasantry for a month.

**Powder and shot are the consumable capital, and they are counted.** His standard magazine for one siege: eight or nine hundred thousand pounds of powder, sixty thousand heavy roundshot, eighty heavy cannon, fifteen or sixteen thousand bombs, forty thousand grenades, a hundred and eighty thousand pounds of lead, a hundred thousand flints, fifty thousand sandbags, forty thousand tools. This is not colour. It is the reason the whole doctrine is an *economics*: the man who wrote it had to sign for the stores.

**Time is seasonal and the season is short.** Winter sieges are "ruineux" and to be avoided; marshy places are attacked in the driest part of the year; a campaign opens when the enemy cannot yet take the field and closes when his troops have gone home. The clock is not an abstraction, it is a calendar with hard ends.

**Money is prior to everything.** *"L'argent est le nerf de la Guerre; sans lui on ne sçauroit réussir en rien."* He puts funds sixth in his list of six preconditions, but the sentence is unconditional.

**The technician has to fight the honour culture to be heard.** This is the social fact behind the book. Generals want to storm; governors want to be seen on the breach; the artillery resents the engineer's interference. Vauban's maxims 27 and 28 are an institutional demand that one technical head direct the attacks under the general alone, because "quand il y a plusieurs têtes à qui il faut rendre compte, il est impossible que la confusion ne s'y mette." The defence section opens by attacking governors who "se fiant trop en leur courage, ayent négligé la Science de défendre les Places." He is a low-born engineer telling dukes their courage is a substitute for competence.

**Geometry is available and cheap.** He gives a method for measuring the distance from the trench-opening to the covered way by similar triangles, with pickets and a measuring rod, explicitly *"comme on n'a pas toujours des Tables des Sinus & des Logarithmes en poche."* Knowledge is portable; instruments are not.

**Almost no place is regular.** "Il se trouve peu ou point de grandes Places régulièrement fortifiées." Every real object is a patchwork of old and new work with a strong side and a weak side. The method exists to be adapted, and he says so.

## 3. Core doctrine

Twenty principles. Loci are given by part, chapter and the printed page of the 1737 de Hondt edition; where the Burgundy-manuscript text (Augoyat 1828) differs, I say so.

**1. The decision to besiege is made at a desk, before anything moves, against six conditions.**
*Locus:* I.i, pp. 1–2.
*His reasoning:* Because execution is "une des plus sérieuses, des plus importantes & des plus difficiles parties de la Guerre," it demands, in his order: secrecy; the forces actually on foot; the enemy's disposition; the state of the nearest magazines; the season; and the funds. These are "des mesures à prendre de longue main, qui doivent être dirigées à loisir."
*Limits:* He immediately says that after all six are satisfied "souvent tout échape," because the enemy is not a party to your plan and has four ways to spoil it — he may match you, he may besiege something you value more, he may ravage your country while you are pinned, or he may fight you before you are established.

**2. Money is the nerve; without it nothing else in the list is worth having.**
*Locus:* I.i, p. 2, condition 6.
*His reasoning:* Stated as an axiom, not argued. The magazine list in chapter II is the argument: the physical requirements of one siege are so large that the financial question is not a constraint on the plan, it is the plan.
*Limits:* None given, which is itself characteristic — he treats it as the one condition that cannot be improvised around.

**3. Over-provision, and budget for a month of open trench whatever you think it will take.**
*Locus:* I.ii, "Des Magazins," p. 4 (both halves).
*His reasoning:* Two sentences on the same page, and they work together. On quantity: *"Le plus de munitions ne sçauroit rien gâter; mais le moins peut faire échouer l'entreprise"* — an asymmetry of consequence, since surplus stores are a carrying cost while deficient stores are a lost siege and a lost army. On duration: *"On ne sçauroit manquer de compter sur un mois de tranchée ouverte; parce qu'il est rare qu'une Place ne puisse tenir ce temps-là, quand elle est un peu considérable, & défendue par gens intelligens qui veulent faire leur devoir."* The quantity is sized against the duration, and the duration is set by assuming a competent opponent rather than the one you hope for.
*Limits:* He quantifies rather than sermonising, and ties the amount to the strength of the target — eight or nine hundred thousand pounds of powder "selon que la Place est plus ou moins forte." And he knows the month is pessimistic: he says elsewhere that places in fact surrender much sooner than they should. It is a planning figure, not a prediction.

**4. Two armies: one besieges, one watches.**
*Locus:* I.i, pp. 2–3; restated as general maxim 11, p. 185: *"Le moyen le plus sûr de bien réussir à un Siège, est d'avoir une Armée d'observation."*
*His reasoning:* The besieging army is immobilised inside its lines and therefore cannot choose its battles. The observation army exists to keep the relief force away, escort convoys, hold the approaches, and — crucially — to avoid fighting: it manoeuvres "afin de n'être pas obligée de combattre contre sa volonté."
*Limits:* The two must stay within supporting distance "sur-tout dans le commencement," or the enemy will take them separately.

**5. Conceal the object. If you can afford it, invest a place you do not intend to take.**
*Locus:* I.iii, p. 6.
*His reasoning:* The general's movements should "éloigner les soupçons que l'Ennemi peut avoir de ses desseins." He gives the mechanism of the payoff: a false threat makes the enemy strip the garrison of the place you actually want.
*Limits:* His illustration (the Allies feinting at Ypres to strip Tournai) is one of the **post-1707 interpolations**; the principle is Vauban's, the example is the editor's.

**6. Reconnoitre the object yourself, repeatedly, by day and by night, and distrust everything you were told.**
*Locus:* I.v, pp. 21–23.
*His reasoning:* Printed plans exist for most European places and should be used, and workmen of the country — masons, stonecutters, contractors — know things, and a man can be put inside to come back with news. But "à tout ce qu'on peut apprendre de cette sorte, & à quoi il ne faut pas trop se fier, on doit ajouter ce que l'on découvre par soi-même." Day reconnaissance shows the approaches and the works; only night reconnaissance, close enough to touch, shows the hollows, ditches, standing and running water. Then you withdraw slowly at dawn and see, in growing light, what you groped for in the dark.
*Limits:* Night work "ne se fait pas sans péril, encore ne voit-on pas grand' chose." And the decisive limit is stated outright: you will never learn from outside what the place contains inside — "vous ne sçaurez pas ce qu'elle renferme dans elle-même, si vous ne l'apprenez par d'autres."

**7. Every place has a strong side and a weak side — but "weak" is defined by your own supply, not by his masonry.**
*Locus:* I.v, p. 29 (*"on doit toujours chercher le foible des Places, & l'attaquer par préférence à tous autres endroits"*); general maxim 2, pp. 183–184, with the Valenciennes case.
*His reasoning:* A regular place equally strong everywhere — he names Neuf-Brisach — is a theoretical object. Real places are patched, half old and half new, better sited on one face than another, so a preference always exists and the whole reconnaissance exists to find it. Then he redefines the term. The king attacked Valenciennes by the Porte d'Anzin front *knowing it was the strongest face of the place*, for three reasons that had nothing to do with the wall: the paved Rhume causeway brought munitions from Dunkirk, Ypres, Lille, Douai and Tournai right up to the tail of the trenches, "ce qui ne se pouvoit par-tout ailleurs"; large woods nearby supplied fascines in abundance; and the trench on that side could be made to double as a contravallation, sealing the garrison in. *"Ce qui est le plus fort dans les cas ordinaires, se trouve le plus foible dans les extraordinaires."*
*Limits:* Two, both serious. He calls the Valenciennes grounds "raisons supérieures" — they override the ordinary rule, and you must be able to name them, not merely feel them. And he says never to attack through a side hedged with obstacles "pour peu qu'il y ait d'apparence d'approcher de la Place par un autre côté," because there you are enfiladed with no remedy. (The parallel case of "le Prince Eugène à attaquer Lille" that follows in the 1737 print is an interpolation, absent from the Burgundy manuscript.)

**8. Do not open the trench until everything is already on the ground.**
*Locus:* general maxim 3, p. 184; I.vi, p. 35.
*His reasoning:* *"Car il ne faut pas languir pour ce manquement, mais avoir toujours les choses nécessaires sous la main."* The trench is a clock that starts running the night it opens; opening it before the lines are advanced, the magazines forward and the artillery within three or four days of firing means paying in casualties for days in which nothing progresses.
*Limits:* He sets the readiness test concretely: lines two-thirds to three-quarters done, good heaps of fascines at the head of the camps, artillery able to put guns in battery within three or four days, the place reconnoitred, nothing threatening in the field.

**9. The besieger fortifies himself. Three parallels at measured distances, each a fortress against sorties.**
*Locus:* I.viii, pp. 50–56.
*His reasoning:* This is the signature. The first parallel goes in at about 300 toises from the outworks — "le plus grand éloignement où les Sorties des Ennemis puissent donner atteinte." The second at 120–150 toises further in, the third 120–145 beyond that, ending 15 or 20 toises from the salients. He lists seven uses, of which the first and last matter most: they protect the approaches pushed forward between them, and they act "d'une excellente Contrevallation contre la Place." The approach trench is no longer a thin tentacle that a sortie can roll up; it is a chain of entrenched camps creeping forward.
*Limits:* If the garrison is strong and ricochet batteries cannot be sited, the third parallel must come to grenade range (13–14 toises) and be built stronger and wider than the others, or a fourth added, "afin de n'avoir pas de longues marches à faire pour joindre l'Ennemi."

**10. Never push a work forward that cannot be supported from behind.**
*Locus:* I.ix, sortie-maxim III, p. 57; general maxim 12, p. 185.
*His reasoning:* The whole sortie chapter follows from it: sorties succeed against works that stick out beyond the fire that covers them. If the supporting position is not yet in a state to support, the forward work is a gift.
*Limits:* None. He repeats it twice, in two different lists, which is his way of insisting.

**11. Do not contest unfinished ground. Give it up, fall back, and let your prepared fire do the work.**
*Locus:* I.ix, sortie-maxims VII, VIII and X, pp. 58–59.
*His reasoning:* *"De ne jamais s'opiniâtrer à soutenir des Ouvrages imparfaits, mais céder"* — withdraw the armed men and the workers behind the nearest parallel, "laissant agir le feu de la Tranchée, qui fera beaucoup plus de mal à l'Ennemi, que la résistance qu'on pourroit lui faire en s'opiniâtrant." Then do not hurry to meet him: let him commit, let him take the fire of the parallels as long as he is willing to stand in it, and only charge him with the grenadiers when he is weakened and fully engaged.
*Limits:* Two. After beating a sortie, do not pursue "avec beaucoup d'opiniâtreté" — push it home and get back into the trench, because the place's fire is by then prepared and lethal. And accept the cost of the method: do not make an affair of watching him overturn "une douzaine ou deux de Gabions" and fire a stretch of unfinished work, "car si votre feu est bien conduit, il le payera très-chèrement."

**12. Ricochet fire: the cheapest instrument in the siege, and the one that empties the defence without destroying it.**
*Locus:* I.x, pp. 73–76; general maxims 13 and 14, p. 185.
*His reasoning:* The gun is laid to graze the crest of the parapet and drop the ball inside the work so that it bounds along the rampart behind. The charge is found empirically — increase and decrease with the same powder until the ball just skims the top — and once found, since the piece does not recoil, it is left alone. He lists eight properties. Six are tactical (dismounting guns, clearing the defences and the covered way, plunging the ditches, taking the flanks in reverse). Two are economic and are the reason the method matters: *"D'être d'une grande économie; car elles peuvent servir tant que le Siège dure, sans qu'on soit obligé de changer de Batterie"* and *"De consommer 7 ou 8 fois moins de Poudre que les autres Batteries, & de ne tirer jamais inutilement."*
*Limits:* He states exactly when it will not work: where the covered way's salients are too high (he names the Terra Nova of Namur castle, Fort St Pierre at Freiburg, Fort St André at Salins, the citadels of Perpignan and Montmédy), where marsh or rivers prevent siting the batteries, and where the glacis is too steep to be plunged. In those cases you must storm — and he treats that as a failure of the method, not an alternative to it. He also insists the guns fire one after another at equal intervals, never in salvo, so as to leave the enemy no gap to work in.

**13. Never do by force what can be done by industry, because industry works surely and force does not.**
*Locus:* general maxim 7, p. 185.
*His reasoning:* The full sentence: use the sap as soon as the trench becomes dangerous, "et ne jamais faire à découvert ni par force, ce que l'on peut faire par industrie: parce que l'industrie agit toujours sûrement, & que par la force on ne réussit pas toujours, & on hasarde pour l'ordinaire beaucoup." The Burgundy manuscript's wording is sharper on the middle term: force "est quelquefois sujette à manquer." This is the governing sentence of the whole treatise.
*Limits:* He shows where industry runs out. At the covered way (I.xiii, pp. 85–87) he sets out the choice — take it "de vive force, ou par industrie" — and when industry is unavailable the storm is necessary, lasts two or three hours in the open, and "il y a toujours beaucoup de sang répandu de la part de ceux qui attaquent & de ceux qui défendent; mais pour l'ordinaire beaucoup plus des premiers que des derniers."

**14. Haste does not shorten a siege. It lengthens it, and it always bloodies it.**
*Locus:* general maxim 17, p. 186.
*His reasoning:* *"La Précipitation dans les Sièges ne hâte point la prise des Places, la recule souvent, & ensanglante toujours la scène: témoin Barcelonne, Landau & plusieurs autres."* Two named counter-examples, both recent and both French. The Burgundy manuscript carries a sentence the 1737 printing drops, and it is the best line in the book: *"Il est des sièges et de l'attaque des places, comme de la plupart des affaires considérables de ce monde, qui demandent un point de maturité pour être résolues et bien conduites à leur fin."*
*Limits:* None attached; but see principle 20, where he refuses to pretend that method makes the trench safe.

**15. Do not destroy what you intend to own.**
*Locus:* general maxim 16, p. 186.
*His reasoning:* *"Ne jamais tirer aux bâtimens de la Place, parce que c'est perdre du temps & consommer des Munitions mal à propos, pour des choses qui ne contribuent en rien à sa reddition, & dont les réparations coûtent toujours beaucoup après la prise."* Three separate costs in one sentence: time, ammunition, and your own future repair bill. Bombarding the town is not cruelty in his account; it is accounting error.
*Limits:* He does keep mortar and stone-mortar batteries in the establishment — the prohibition is on the *buildings*, not on vertical fire against the defences and the defenders.

**16. One technical head, answerable to the commander alone.**
*Locus:* general maxims 27–28, pp. 187–188; I.xxiii, pp. 164–166.
*His reasoning:* Any siege of consequence "demande un homme d'expérience, de tête & de caractère, qui ait la principale disposition des Attaques, sous l'autorité du Général"; he directs the trench, sites every battery, and tells the artillery officers what to do, "à qui ceux-ci doivent obéir ponctuellement, sans y ajouter ni diminuer." He commands the engineers, miners and sappers and accounts to the general alone, because multiple masters guarantee confusion "au grand désavantage du Siège & des Troupes." The general keeps absolute authority but must consult the engineer before resolving anything: "le défaut d'observation de cette circonstance a souvent coûté la vie à bien du monde."
*Limits:* The general must visit the trench — but "de temps en temps seulement, & non tous les jours," lightly accompanied. The lieutenant-general of the day posts himself between the two attacks and not at their head, because a man everyone has to reach obstructs the work.

**17. Rotate the danger and pay the men who carry.**
*Locus:* I.vii, pp. 46–48.
*His reasoning:* The four leading sappers work an hour or two at full effort, then call the other four; and the man who led the head takes the tail on the next relief, "car chacun d'eux doit mener la tête à son tour, & poser une pareille quantité de Gabions, afin de partager également le péril & le travail." For the fascine and gabion carrying he proposes six deniers a fascine and a sol a gabion, paid on the spot: "Par cette petite libéralité les Sapes seroient toujours bien & aisément servies."
*Limits:* His own accounting: a hundred sandbags, husbanded by being carried forward from the tail every twenty or thirty gabions, will serve one sap from the start of a siege to the end. The generosity is calculated, not sentimental.

### The defence: a separate doctrine for the weaker party

**18. Defence is a science, courage is no substitute for it, and the science is a daily habit kept in peacetime.**
*Locus:* Part II, opening, p. 189; Part II, ch. I, p. 192.
*His reasoning:* *"Quoique plusieurs Gouverneurs, se fiant trop en leur courage, ayent négligé la Science de défendre les Places, cette Science est pourtant très-estimable."* Governors think exposing their lives discharges the duty; the record of places defended bravely and lost through incompetence has kept the error alive; they have not feared the blame they deserve for surrendering earlier than they needed to. The remedy is not resolve but routine, and it is practised when nothing is happening: in a long peace governors forget the town can be besieged and let burghers plant gardens, dig ditches, raise hedges, even build houses within cannon shot. Against that: *"Il doit s'attaquer tous les jours lui-même en secret, & chercher autant de différentes Défenses, qu'il invente de nouvelles Attaques."*
*Limits and concrete first moves.* He concedes the science is barely teachable from books — "elle veut une application plus étendue, & l'expérience seule la peut fournir." And he attaches three specifics worth as much as the maxim. Keep silent when the enemy arrives; do not mark out his camp for him with your cannon, but let him pitch it too wide or too close and have to begin his circumvallation again, "qui est tout ce que peut désirer l'Assiégé dans cette occasion." When his staff comes forward to reconnoitre, fire on the individuals and not the crowd, because a general reconnoitring detaches himself with a handful: "ce sont des têtes qu'il vaut mieux abattre qu'un plus grand nombre d'autres Ennemis d'une moindre importance." And hoard the powder — "il n'y a rien qu'il faille tant ménager dans une Place que la Poudre" — because the charge of ten or twelve battery pieces, placed under a lodgement as a mine, will destroy it more easily than a hundred rounds of cannon.

**19. Defence in depth: retrench behind the retrenchment, so that he must make several sieges for the one he planned.**
*Locus:* Part II.vi, pp. 210–216.
*His reasoning:* The best retrenchment is a small bastion inside the attacked bastion, because it "forme une seconde Place, qui a presque les mêmes Défenses." The defender has all his force united and little ground to hold, and holds it almost without risk; the attacker must leave his trench, cross the ditch and climb in the open. And the psychological mechanism is named: "rien ne ralentit tant l'ardeur du Soldat, que le dépit d'être obligé de recommencer un travail qu'il croyoit fini." Keep doing it and "l'Ennemi sera obligé de faire plusieurs Sièges, pour un seul qu'il s'étoit proposé."
*Limits:* He states the end condition honestly, and it is the closest thing in the book to an ethic: when the governor has no ground left to retrench and has destroyed the work he had to abandon, "alors" he may consent to a capitulation with honour, "puisqu'il n'abandonne aux Ennemis qu'une Place presque démolie." And of the whole counter-attack sequence: "Cette action n'est pas si difficile qu'elle paroît. Elle a plus besoin de conduite que de force."

**20. No place in the trench is safe, however well it is made.**
*Locus:* I.xxiii, p. 168.
*His reasoning:* He is explaining why sovereigns should not go past the third parallel, and in doing so he demolishes his own marketing. Nothing covers you from bombs and stones. No trench parapet exists that cannot be pierced by cannon three feet below the crest. Of the infinity of musket balls fired, many graze the parapet, lose force, plunge, and still kill. There are oblique shots, glancing shots, accidental shots. Closer in, under grenade range, it is worse.
*Limits:* This is itself the limit clause on the entire treatise, and he put it in writing.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

**The attack half of the treatise says nothing to the small actor, and pretending otherwise is the main way this text gets misused.** Part I is written from inside a state that can requisition eighteen thousand peasants, stockpile nine hundred thousand pounds of powder, and field two armies at once. Its opening sentence defines a siege as the natural consequence of superiority. If you have nothing, the attack book is not addressed to you.

**The defence half is addressed to you, directly and at length, and this is not an inference.** Part II assumes a party who is outnumbered, cut off, cannot be relieved, cannot win, and will in the end lose the ground. Vauban writes six chapters on what such a party should do anyway, and the object he sets is not victory: it is to make the stronger party pay more time and more men than the object is worth, and to arrive at terms with the position still intact enough to be worth terms. Every one of his defensive devices is a device of the weak: preparing in peacetime when nobody is watching; silence instead of display; spending your scarce resource (powder) on the single highest-yield target (a mine under his lodgement) rather than on a visible cannonade; shooting the four men who matter rather than the four hundred who do not; giving ground you cannot hold and taking it back when he is unconsolidated; and building the next position behind the one you are about to lose, so that the work he thought was finished has to be done again.

**He is also explicit that the weak party's chief enemy is its own honour and its own convenience.** The passage on premature capitulation (pp. 189–191) is a small masterpiece of institutional cynicism: the officers, "poussés par l'intérêt de conserver quelques menus équipages qui leur appartiennent," persuade the governor that he can capitulate with honour after a token appearance sword-in-hand on top of the breach; and the governor, "qui par des raisons particulières veut bien être persuadé," is glad they opened the subject. The men who surrender early do so because they have private property to protect and a story they can tell. That is a finding about weakness that generalises very well.

**Two further points are inference on my part, and I label them as such.** First, Vauban's biography is the strongest argument in the entry and it is not in the text: an orphan at ten in "very poor circumstances," educated by charity, who joined the rebel side, was captured, changed sides, and declined an early commission because he could not afford one (EB 1911). He arrived with nothing and became a Marshal of France by being the man who could reliably do a technical thing that the state urgently needed and could not get elsewhere. Second, the *Dîme royale* is the work of a man deliberately operating outside his credentialled competence and saying so: "Je ne suis ni lettré, ni homme de finances"; "je n'ai aucune Mission pour chercher ce moyen, et … je suis peut-être l'homme du Royaume le moins pourvu des qualités nécessaires à le trouver; je n'ai pas laissé d'y travailler, persuadé qu'il n'y a rien dont une vive et longue application ne puisse venir à bout." It cost him the king's favour and was suppressed within weeks. Both facts belong in the book; neither is doctrine from the treatise.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** The heaviest-weighted question in the whole text. Preparation is not a phase before the work, it is most of the work: six conditions weighed at a desk; magazines assembled and quantified before the object is named; the trench not opened until the lines are three-quarters done and the guns three days from firing; and, on the defence, the governor attacking his own walls in secret every day for years before anyone appears in front of them. The characteristic Vauban move is to convert a future risk into a present, boring, itemised cost.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Use printed plans even when inexact; use local tradesmen, who know the fabric; put a man inside if you can. Then distrust all of it and go and look yourself, twice, by day and by night, at physical risk, on foot or nearly alone. And accept a hard ceiling: from outside you will learn the approaches and the works, never what the place contains within itself. After reconnaissance, write it down — "on doit faire un petit recueil de ces remarques."

**Timing.** Seasonal and irreversible. Avoid winter. Take marshy places in the driest month. Strike before the enemy can take the field, or in the late season after part of his army has gone home. Once the trench is open the clock cannot be stopped, which is why nothing is opened until everything is present. And: haste never shortens it.

**Terrain and position.** Terrain decides the whole plan, and he enumerates rather than generalises: rock and its height, running or standing water, marsh that can or cannot be drained, causeways and whether they are enfiladed, revetted ditches and their depth, dry ditches floodable at will (which he rates the most dangerous of all to an attacker), glacis slope, whether the soil is soft enough to dig or mixed with stone. He also applies terrain reasoning to his own works: lines should use woods, rivers, ponds, marshes, ravines and sunken roads "comme autant d'avantages favorables que la Nature nous présente, & qu'il ne faut pas négliger."

**Deception and information.** Present but thin, and always instrumental. Move so as to draw suspicion away from the object; if you can afford it, invest a place you do not want so the enemy weakens the one you do; open the trench in silence, without drums or trumpets, each worker lying on his fascine, forbidden to dig until ordered. On the defence, deception is mostly silence: do not tell him by your fire where his camp should be.

**Alliances.** Silent. There is no politics in the treatise. The nearest thing is the observation army, which is a second force under the same crown, not an ally.

**Logistics and economy.** The spine of the book. Money is the nerve. The magazine is enumerated to the flint. Ricochet fire is recommended for eight reasons of which two are that it burns seven or eight times less powder and never fires uselessly. Buildings are not to be shelled because the repairs will be yours. The "weakest side" of a fortress is redefined as the side your convoys can reach on a paved road. A hundred sandbags, properly recovered and carried forward, will serve a whole siege. This is a strategist whose fundamental category is unit cost.

**Morale and discipline.** Handled as a design problem rather than an exhortation. Rotate the sappers so the danger is shared equally. Pay sixpence a fascine on the spot. Feed the peasants double bread and punish deserters, but also pay the ones you retain, because "cela leur fera prendre patience, & les empêchera de déserter." Never let troops hold unfinished work, because losing it teaches them they can be beaten. On the other side, the single most effective thing a defender can do to enemy morale is force him to redo finished work.

**The decisive point.** Explicit and double-edged. Attack always by the weak side — but the weak side is determined by approach roads, fascine woods and the possibility of sealing the garrison in, not by the height of the masonry. Where a place has both a town and a citadel, attack the citadel, "parce que la Citadelle prise, la Ville suit nécessairement: au lieu qu'en attaquant la Ville la première, on a deux Sièges à faire pour un."

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Treated from the defender's side, where it is a real question. The governor's temptation is to stop too early — after the outworks are taken and the miner is at the wall — and Vauban's answer is that there is a great deal left to do before that is honest. The stopping condition is objective, not emotional: no ground left to retrench on, the last retrenchment destroyed by your own hand, and the place surrendered "presque démolie." From the attacker's side, the corresponding discipline is negative: do not pursue a beaten sortie too far, do not spend ammunition on buildings, and do not press the covered way by storm when the guns can still be made to do it.

**When not to fight.** Repeatedly and specifically. Do not attack through marsh, causeways, narrow places or re-entrant angles if there is any dry and spacious alternative. Do not attack a side so obstructed that you will be enfiladed without remedy. Do not besiege in winter. Do not attempt anything forward that cannot be supported. Do not storm the covered way if ricochet and the trench cavaliers can take it. Do not defend unfinished works. The observation army's job is explicitly to manoeuvre so as not to be forced to fight against its will.

**Defence versus offence.** He is the ranking authority on both and does not treat them as symmetrical. Attack is a matter of method, resources and patience, and with those it is nearly certain — his own claim for the parallels at Ath is that they justified themselves by "le peu de temps & de monde que ce Siège coûta." Defence is harder, rarer, and almost never done properly: of all the places besieged by French or enemy in thirty-six years, he excepts only Kaiserswerth as well conducted. His diagnosis is that defence is a science that men substitute courage for. The 1911 *Britannica*'s verdict, which is the standard one, is that "the greatness of his services was rather in the attack than in the defence."

**The character required of the commander.** Two different characters, and he separates them deliberately. The general needs absolute authority and the self-restraint to consult a subordinate technician before resolving anything, plus the sense to visit the trench occasionally rather than daily. The director of attacks needs "expérience, tête et caractère" — and Vauban's word for him is *l'âme et le véritable mobile des Attaques*. The governor of a besieged place needs the rarer quality: the willingness to be blamed for holding on, and to keep inventing difficulties for an enemy who thought he was finished.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The bloodless siege.** The dominant myth, and the one the serious modern literature exists to correct. Vauban himself never claimed bloodlessness; his claim is comparative — attacks conducted "par les voies les plus courtes, les plus raisonnables, & les moins ensanglantées qui se puissent mettre en usage" — and he says outright that no part of a trench is safe and that storming the covered way costs the attacker more than the defender. The myth was built by the eulogists: the 1911 *Britannica* bibliography lists a whole industry of them, including d'Arçon's *Considérations sur le génie de Vauban* (1780), **Lazare Carnot**'s prize *Éloge de Vauban* (1784) — which drew a critical *Lettre à l'académie* in 1785 and Carnot's rejoinder — and further *éloges* by Dembarrère (1784), d'Autilly (1788) and Sauviac (1790).

**The serious modern critique is Jamel Ostwald's.** *Vauban under Siege: Engineering Efficiency and Martial Vigor in the War of the Spanish Succession* (Brill, 2007) is the first systematic comparison of Vaubanian theory with archival practice. I read Michael Wolfe's review in *H-France Review* 8 (January 2008), no. 10, rather than the book, and report it as such. Ostwald's argument, per Wolfe: Ostwald "resists the temptation to buy into the mythos of Vauban propagated in the marshal's memoirs, the celebrated *Oisivetés*, and by his eighteenth-century acolytes," and finds in French, British, Belgian and Dutch archives that "few generals ever actually implemented what he so fervently advocated." He sets two competing models against each other — the engineers' *efficiency*, which prices resources and prizes planning and prudence, and the generals' *martial vigor*, which prices time and prizes speed. The generals won. "Time mattered more than lives." Further, and more damaging to the standard story, Wolfe reports Ostwald's finding that "it was not Vauban's new efficient methods of attack that gave a decided advantage to besiegers, but rather the brute growth in artillery production and its increased mobility" — a development associated with the Dutch engineer **Menno van Coehoorn**, whom Ostwald reclaims. Where Ostwald does credit Vauban is on the defensive side: "Vauban's greatest influence was in the area of defense, in the concentric rings of fortresses that composed the celebrated *pré carré*. This defense-in-depth stopped the Allied advances when France's field armies could not." Wolfe's own framing is that after this book historians "will perforce need to fundamentally revise this longstanding thesis." Ostwald's dissertation supervisor was **John Lynn**, whose *Giant of the Grand Siècle* (1996) is the background.

**The "perfect siege" figures are worth checking before quoting.** Ath, 1697, is the paradigm case and the numbers circulate loosely. The secondary literature summarising Ostwald says the place was taken "with only 300 casualties"; the English Wikipedia article on the siege gives French losses as "200 killed and wounded" and defender losses as "3,700 killed, wounded, and captured" — the latter evidently including the surrendered garrison. Fourteen days of open trenches is the consistent figure. Vauban's own statement, in the treatise, is not numerical at all: Ath is where the parallels were executed with the most precision, "& le peu de temps & de monde que ce Siège coûta, en a justifié la bonté."

**The "system" misreading.** Because three systems were codified after his death, Vauban is read as a system-builder, and the whole eighteenth-century engineer corps defended that reading as caste orthodoxy. The serious attack came from **Marc-René, marquis de Montalembert**, in *La Fortification perpendiculaire* (Paris, 1776–78, eleven volumes), which rejected the bastioned trace altogether on the ground that in fortifications "à la Vauban" the artillery is badly protected, and proposed polygonal works with concentrated fire. The 1911 *Britannica* records the institutional response: "In his own country the caste-spirit of the engineer corps was roused to defend Vauban, and though Montalembert was allowed to construct some successful works at Île-d'Aix and Oléron, he was forbidden to publish his method." Montalembert's polygonal principle was taken up in Germany and became the nineteenth-century standard. The irony is that Vauban's own maxim 20 says regular places get regular attacks but irregular places must be attacked "comme l'on peut," and his own name for his most famous design was "the Landau system perfected" — he was less of a systematiser than his defenders.

**The Maginot inheritance.** Vauban is routinely invoked as the ancestor of the Maginot Line and therefore of static-defence failure, and the analogy is half right at most. What he actually proposed to Louvois in January 1673 was a *pré carré* — the pruning of a confused scatter of holdings into a compact double line of the best places, on the ground that "cette confusion de places amies et ennemies ne me plaît point. Vous êtes obligé d'en entretenir trois pour une." (I found this letter only in secondary sources and have not opened a manuscript or critical edition; see section 7, where it is marked UNVERIFIED.) That is an argument for *fewer* fortifications, held in depth, and worked in combination with field armies — which is how Ostwald credits it with actually functioning in 1701–14. Reading it as a continuous wall is a twentieth-century back-projection.

**The corrupted text.** Anyone quoting the 1737 de Hondt edition or the Amsterdam *Œuvres* on the strength of Vauban's name is at risk of quoting an editor. The Denain (1712), Lille (1708) and Tournai passages are not in the Burgundy manuscript. Use Augoyat 1828 for the attack and Valazé 1829 for the defence, which the 1911 *Britannica* already identified as "the first satisfactory editions."

**Attributed sayings that I could not verify and that should never be used as his words.** All three are absent from every Vauban text in the local cache:
- *"Place attaquée par Vauban, place prise; place défendue par Vauban, place imprenable."* The Histoire-en-citations database labels it "Proverbe du vivant de Vauban" and cites Lavisse and Rambaud's *Histoire générale du IVe siècle à nos jours* (1901) — i.e. a proverb about him, printed two centuries later, not a statement by him.
- *"La sueur épargne le sang" / "brûlons plus de poudre, versons moins de sang."* Grepping the 1737 edition, the Augoyat edition, the Amsterdam *Œuvres* and the *Dîme royale* for *sueur*, *brûlons*, *versons*, *plus de poudre* and *moins de sang* returns nothing. "La sueur épargne le sang" is a modern French slogan — the title of a 2007–08 Arte documentary about him, and a unit motto in the French army. The doctrine it summarises is genuinely his; the sentence is not.
- *"A besieged town is a taken town."* I could not trace it to any text of his, and his own doctrine contradicts it in both directions: the besieger is told to budget for a month of open trench against a competent defence, and the defender is told that places surrender far earlier than they should.
- The "twelve-step siege" sometimes quoted in popular writing is a later textbook schematisation. Vauban's own Part I has twenty-five chapters and twenty-nine general maxims and contains no numbered day-by-day schedule.

**Business and self-help misuse.** Vauban has almost no afterlife in that literature — nothing remotely comparable to Sun Tzu's. The one real crossover is linguistic: *pré carré* passed into ordinary French for one's private preserve or protected turf, which inverts the original sense (Vauban's argument was for *giving up* scattered holdings, not for defending turf). Searching for a management literature built on his siege method turned up popularisations of the "methodical process to reduce chaos and casualties," not a body of work worth criticising. I record this as an absence rather than inventing critics.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Vauban is the least romantic entry on this list and the most immediately usable, because he is the only author here whose entire subject is *how a difficult objective is actually taken, step by step, by somebody who has to sign for the cost*. What follows is what survives the transfer, what does not, and why.

**What transfers.**

**Preparation is not a stage; it is most of the work, and it is boring on purpose.** The parallels are the invention everyone remembers, but the operative principle is chapter II: assemble the magazine before you name the target, and do not open the trench until everything you will need is already on the ground. For a man with nothing, the practical form is unglamorous. Do not start the visible thing — the application, the pitch, the business, the move — until the unglamorous inventory is complete and physically present: the money for three months, the finished sample, the list of names, the skill actually tested rather than claimed. Vauban's reasoning is exactly the reason to do this and not a moral one: once the trench is open you are exposed and the clock runs, and shortfalls discovered then cannot be fixed then. *"Il ne faut pas languir pour ce manquement."*

**Over-provision where failure is asymmetric.** "Too much can spoil nothing; too little can wreck the enterprise." This is a decision rule, not an attitude. Where the downside of surplus is a carrying cost and the downside of shortfall is total loss, buy surplus. Where they are symmetric, do not. Most of the ruinous decisions a man with no reserves makes are made by treating an asymmetric risk as if it were symmetric — one month of runway instead of four, one lead instead of thirty, one route to the same client.

**Plan against the competent version of the opposition.** Count on a month of open trench "parce qu'il est rare qu'une Place ne puisse tenir ce temps-là, quand elle est un peu considérable, & défendue par gens intelligens qui veulent faire leur devoir." Not "expect it to be hard" as encouragement — a number, set deliberately high, that your resources must cover. Then be pleasantly surprised.

**Never do by force what can be done by industry, because industry works surely and force does not.** This is the single sentence to carry out of the entry, and the reason given is the important half. Force is not condemned for being brutal; it is condemned for having a failure rate. A man with no reserves cannot afford variance. Frontal effort — the cold approach at scale, the confrontation, the all-in bet, the spectacular gesture — sometimes works and sometimes does not, and when it does not you have spent what you cannot replace. The sap is slower, more tedious, visibly less impressive, and arrives. Choose the method with the lower variance even when its expected speed is worse, *until you have a reserve*. Vauban's own limit applies: when industry genuinely cannot do it, storm — and expect to lose more than the other side.

**Haste does not shorten anything; it just raises the price.** "La précipitation dans les sièges ne hâte point la prise des places, la recule souvent, & ensanglante toujours la scène." And from the manuscript: most considerable affairs "demandent un point de maturité." The reason a man with nothing hurries is that he is frightened, and the hurry costs him the reconnaissance, the preparation and the unfinished supporting position — which is to say it costs him the thing he was hurrying toward.

**Attack the weak point, but define "weak" by your own logistics.** The Valenciennes passage is the most transferable single case in the treatise. The king attacked the strongest face of the town because that was the side the paved road reached, the side with the fascine woods, and the side where the trench could double as a blockade. The analogue is exact and unflattering: the opportunity you should take is not the one that is objectively easiest, it is the one you can actually reach, resupply and sustain. A weaker target you cannot sustain an approach to is not weak. A "hard" industry where you already have a road in — one relationship, one skill, one place you can be physically present — is the weak side.

**Never advance anything that cannot be supported from behind, and never defend unfinished ground.** Both halves matter. Do not take on a commitment that your existing position cannot cover if it goes wrong. And when a forward position you have not consolidated comes under pressure, give it up and fall back on the prepared one rather than defending it out of stubbornness — "laissant agir le feu de la Tranchée." Most of the fatal losses in a life with no reserves come from defending something half-built because abandoning it feels like failure.

**Build the next position before you lose the current one.** The defence doctrine is the part of Vauban written for people in your situation, and its core is retrenchment behind retrenchment: a second small bastion inside the bastion, so that the enemy who has done the work discovers he has to do it again. Concretely: while the current job, contract or arrangement is working, build the thing behind it. Not as a hedge in the abstract — as an actual constructed position you could fall back into. The psychology Vauban names is real and works both ways: "rien ne ralentit tant l'ardeur du Soldat, que le dépit d'être obligé de recommencer un travail qu'il croyoit fini."

**Spend your scarce resource on the highest-yield target, not on the visible one.** The besieged governor is told that powder is the one thing to husband above all, that his cannon will lose the artillery duel anyway, and that the same charge placed under the enemy's lodgement as a mine will do more than a hundred rounds fired at him. And: fire on the individuals who matter, not the mass. With no money, no network and no name, almost everything you can do is a cannonade — visible, symmetrical, and lost in advance against a better-resourced opponent. The transferable move is to find the few charges that are placed under something.

**Do not destroy what you intend to own.** Bombarding the town is condemned on three grounds, all economic: it wastes time, wastes ammunition, and leaves you the repair bill. A man with nothing is constantly tempted to burn things on the way out — an employer, a partner, a former field — because it is satisfying and costs nothing today. It costs the repairs.

**Rotate the danger, and pay on the spot.** Vauban's sappers change places so that each leads the head in turn "afin de partager également le péril & le travail," and his carriers get sixpence a fascine "sur le champ." If you ever have anyone working with you, both rules are cheap and both work. If you are working alone, the first rule still applies internally: do not put the same faculty at the head of the sap indefinitely.

**Competence in a thing the powerful need and cannot get elsewhere is the only real leverage a nobody has.** This is inference from the biography, not doctrine from the text, and I flag it as such: an orphan educated by a Carmelite prior, who could not afford the commission he was offered, ended as Marshal of France because he was the man who could reliably take a fortress on schedule. He did not network into it and he did not have capital. He had one technical capability the state urgently required. Vauban's standing was never social; it was the fact that when he directed the attacks, the attacks worked.

**And the man with nothing may write outside his brief — at a price.** The *Dîme royale* is a man with no commission, no credentials and no protection publishing a tax reform that indicts the whole fiscal system, opening with "je ne suis ni lettré, ni homme de finances" and "il n'y a rien dont une vive et longue application ne puisse venir à bout." It was suppressed by royal decree within weeks and he died days later, out of favour. The lesson is not that he should not have written it. It is that the calculation is real and should be made consciously: the man who has climbed by being useful spends that usefulness when he speaks outside it, and he should know he is spending it.

**What does not transfer.**

**The attack book's economics.** Everything in Part I assumes surplus: two armies, eighteen thousand requisitioned labourers, nine hundred thousand pounds of powder, a treasury behind the plan. "La résolution des sièges est une affaire de cabinet. Elle est une suite naturelle de la supériorité que l'on croit avoir." If you do not have superiority, you are not the besieger, and reading Part I as your manual will make you attempt things at a scale you cannot supply. The man with nothing is, structurally, the garrison.

**The fixed, known objective.** A siege has a target that cannot move, cannot change shape, cannot leave. Nothing in a modern life is like that. The whole apparatus of parallels, measured distances and progressive approach depends on the object staying still while you dig toward it. Applied to a moving target — a market, a job market, a person — methodical approach at Vauban's pace is simply slowness. The principle that transfers is the low-variance preference; the *tempo* does not.

**The requisitioned labour and the punishment of defectors.** He commands peasants, feeds them double bread, has them paid by their own villages, and punishes deserters severely. There is no analogue and no need to look for one.

**Deception as a serious instrument.** It is present in Vauban but thin and expensive — the false investment of a town you do not want costs a detachment of several thousand horse. A man with nothing cannot afford a feint, because a feint requires spare force. Read Sun Tzu for that and do not try to borrow it from here.

**The "bloodless" reputation.** Do not carry away the idea that method makes the work safe. Vauban wrote the correction himself: "il n'y a aucun lieu de sûr dans la Tranchée, quelque soin qu'on puisse se donner de la bien faire," and the storm of the covered way costs the attacker more than the defender. Ostwald's finding is worse: even in his own lifetime, most commanders declined his method and bought time with lives, and what actually made besiegers dominant was more artillery, not better engineering. Method reduces the expected cost. It does not remove the cost, it does not guarantee adoption, and it can be outcompeted by brute quantity. Anyone selling you a procedure that abolishes risk is selling the eighteenth-century *éloges*, not the man.

**The last honest thing in the book.** The defence ends with the governor consenting to terms only when he has no ground left to retrench on and has destroyed the last work himself, surrendering "une Place presque démolie, & dont les débris serviront de monument à sa Gloire." That is not an encouragement. It is a definition of what losing well looks like when you were always going to lose the ground: you set the price, you made him pay it, and there is nothing left for him to use. For a man who begins with nothing, that is the realistic best case in a large number of the engagements he will not be able to avoid, and it is worth knowing in advance what it looks like.
