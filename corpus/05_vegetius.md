# 5. Publius Flavius Vegetius Renatus — De Re Militari (Epitoma rei militaris)

## 1. Identity

The *Epitoma rei militaris* is a four-book Latin handbook on the raising, organising, training and employment of a Roman army, written by a civil official whose manuscript title styles him *uir inlustris comes* — the top senatorial grade — and who tells the reader at I.8 that he claims no authority of his own. (The further statement often made, that he says he has no military experience, is not in the text: I searched all four books and found no such declaration. His inexperience is an inference from his civilian title and his avowedly second-hand method, not a confession he makes.) Book I is the selection and training of recruits. Book II is the establishment of the legion — its cohorts, officers, records, pay-deposits, music, drill, engines. Book III is operations and battle: supply, marching, encampment, the decision whether to fight, the seven battle-formations, retreat, defeat, and a closing list of general maxims. Book IV is the attack and defence of fortified places (chapters I–XXX) followed by naval warfare (chapters XXXI–XLVI).

Vegetius names his own sources at I.8: Cato the Censor on military discipline, Cornelius Celsus, Frontinus, Paternus "the most diligent assertor of military law," and the ordinances of Augustus, Trajan and Hadrian. He then says: *Nihil enim mihi auctoritatis adsumo sed horum, quos supra rettuli, quae dispersa sunt, uelut in ordinem epitomata conscribo* — "I claim no authority for myself, but write up what is scattered in those I have named above, epitomised as it were into order." This is the single most important fact about the book. It is not a record of what the late Roman army did. It is an argument, assembled from older books, that the army should go back to doing what those books describe.

**Date.** The terminus post quem is internal: at I.20 Vegetius writes *Ab urbe enim condita usque ad tempus diui Gratiani et catafractis et galeis muniebatur pedestris exercitus* — "from the founding of the city down to the time of the deified Gratian the infantry were protected with cuirasses and helmets." *Diuus* is posthumous; Gratian was killed 25 August 383. The terminus ante quem is a subscription preserved in one family of manuscripts recording that one Flavius Eutropius emended the text at Constantinople, consular-dated to 450. So: after 383, before 450.

**Dedicatee.** The blog entry's "dedicatee disputed: Theodosius I or Valentinian III" is correct as a summary of the scholarship, but it understates the problem. *The Latin text never names the emperor.* I grepped all four books for "Valentinian," "Theodos," "Honori" and "Arcadi": zero hits. Vegetius addresses only *imperator inuicte* ("unconquered emperor") and the oblique honorifics. Corrected count: *imperator inuicte* occurs **six** times, in all four books (I twice, II once, III twice, IV once), not five and not only in I, III and IV. The honorific is never nominative *maiestas tua*; the attested forms are *maiestatis tuae* (II and IV), *maiestati uestrae* (II), *maiestatis uestrae* (IV), and *tranquillitas tua* (II). The heading "To the Emperor Valentinian" that stands over the prefaces in Clarke's English is an editorial supply following one manuscript tradition and the conjecture of earlier editors — not the author's words. There are more than two camps, and the entry (and my own first draft of this section) understated that too. Theodosius I (r. 379–95) is argued by T. D. Barnes, "The Date of Vegetius," *Phoenix* 33.3 (Autumn 1979), 254–57, and adopted by N. P. Milner in the introduction to his translation (1993; 2nd ed. 1996), with François Paschoud, André Chastagnol, Santo Mazzarino and Philippe Richardot on the same side. Valentinian III (r. 425–55) goes back to Otto Seeck, "Die Zeit des Vegetius," *Hermes* 11 (1876), 61–83, and was argued afresh by C. D. Gordon (c. 1974), by Walter Goffart, "The Date and Purpose of Vegetius' *De re militari*," *Traditio* 33 (1977), 65–100, and at book length by Michael B. Charles, *Vegetius in Context: Establishing the Date of the Epitoma Rei Militaris* (Historia-Einzelschrift 194; Stuttgart: Franz Steiner, 2007). Two further candidates are in the field: Valentinian II, championed by Constantine Zuckerman — and, for what it is worth, by the anonymous 1940 introduction, which argues from Gratian being named and from Alaric's sack of 410 going unmentioned — and Honorius, defended by Claudia Giuffrida Manmana. The BMCR reviewer of Charles summarises his position as hoping to salvage no more than "a balance of probability for Valentinian III"; that formulation is the reviewer's, and I have not read Charles himself. The same reviewer's own verdict on the whole dispute: "the case for any emperor involves spinning the sources."

**Survival.** This is the most-copied military text of the Latin Middle Ages, and the blog is right to say so. Allmand's study counts more than 300 surviving manuscripts of the Latin text and its vernacular translations — a transmission comparable to Cicero's and Caesar's. First printed at Utrecht in 1473.

## 2. The world the text assumes

Vegetius assumes a **state**, not a warband. There is an imperial treasury, a tax system that renders grain and forage in kind or commutes it for gold, provinces that can be ordered to fill magazines before a campaign opens, fortified cities to garrison, roads to march on, and an emperor whose single decision can re-found the whole institution. Almost every recommendation in the book is addressed to someone who controls all of that. This is the largest constraint on what transfers.

He assumes **infantry decides battles** — "the main strength of an army consists of the latter," III.9 — at a moment when that was ceasing to be true and cavalry was rising. He assumes armour, close order, and the shock of a heavy line; he assumes the thrust rather than the cut; he assumes that a marching army builds a fortified camp every night and that failing to do so is the cause of recent disasters.

He assumes **institutional decline is moral and reversible**. His diagnosis is always the same: long peace bred negligence, the good families went into the civil service instead of the army, recruiting officers took bribes and accepted men whom their own masters would not keep as servants, the infantry asked permission to put down the cuirass and then the helmet and were shot to pieces by Gothic archery. He assumes this can be undone by an act of will plus a book. The last clause matters: at I.8 he concedes that the practical transmission is broken — *Quem inuenias, qui docere possit quod ipse non didicit?*, "Whom will you find who can teach what he himself has not learnt?" — and concludes that the custom must be recovered *de historiis ergo uel libris*, from histories and books. Vegetius is the first major Western military writer whose method is explicitly the recovery of a lost practice by reading.

He assumes a **literate administration**: daily rolls, guard rosters, pay lists, furlough registers kept "with greater exactness than the regulations of provisions or other civil matters in the registers of the police" (II.19), and a compulsory savings scheme in which half of every donative is held at the standards in ten bags, one per cohort, plus an eleventh for burials (II.20).

He assumes a **pre-industrial logistics horizon** in which an army eats what a province has already stockpiled, in which sieges are decided by who starves first, and in which the largest single variable in a campaign is food.

He assumes, finally, an **audience of one**. The book is addressed upward, to power, and is shaped throughout by the need to flatter the addressee while telling him his army is broken. That shapes the rhetoric and — Goffart's point — badly distorts the book's value as evidence.

## 3. Core doctrine

Sixteen principles. Loci are given in the standard Latin chapter numbering (Book.chapter), which I mapped from the Latin Library chapter lists onto the English section headings of the 1940 text.

**1. Victory is a product of training and discipline, not of numbers or native courage.**
*Locus:* I.1.
*His reasoning:* The Romans were outnumbered by the Gauls, outgrown by the Germans, out-muscled by the Spaniards, outspent and out-tricked by the Africans, outclassed in every art by the Greeks. The only variable they controlled was selection and daily drill. Therefore that variable is the one that produced the empire.
*Limits:* He concedes the material of men differs by region and climate (I.2) — training does not make all populations equal — and at I.7 he concedes that a natural coward is not redeemable by any amount of service.

**2. Skill converts fear into appetite for action.**
*Locus:* I.1; restated II.23.
*His reasoning:* *Scientia enim rei bellicae dimicandi nutrit audaciam: nemo facere metuit quod se bene didicisse confidit* — knowledge of war nourishes boldness; no one fears to do what he is confident he has learnt well. Confidence is downstream of competence, not a separate quality to be instilled.
*Limits:* Only of competence actually tested. At I.8 he insists the recruit be tried before being marked, "for many, though promising enough in appearance, are found very unfit upon trial."

**3. Select ruthlessly at the door; no amount of later service repairs a bad intake.**
*Locus:* I.7 for the governing sentence and the trades list; the supporting preferences are spread across I.3 (country over town) and I.5 (strength over height). My earlier locus "I.6–I.7" was too narrow for the reasoning given below.
*His reasoning:* *Numquam exercitus proficit tempore, cuius in probandis tironibus claudicarit electio* — an army whose selection of recruits limped never improves with time. He prefers countrymen to townsmen, smiths and butchers and huntsmen to fishermen and weavers, and strength to height.
*Limits:* "In cases of necessity, however, they are sometimes obliged to make levies in the cities" (I.3) — and he then gives the remedial programme for city men: entrenching, marching in ranks, carrying weight, coarse food, sleeping out, and encampment as far as possible from the temptations of the city.

**4. Train harder than the event, and with heavier tools than the event.**
*Locus:* I.11 (double-weight wicker shield and wooden club) with the rationale stated at the end of I.12; I.14 (javelins "of greater weight than common"); I.19 (weight-carrying).
*His reasoning:* Recruits practise at the post with wicker shields and wooden clubs of double weight — *dupli autem ponderis illa cratis et claua ideo dabantur, ut, cum uera et leuiora tiro arma sumpsisset, uelut grauiore pondere liberatus securior alacriorque pugnaret* (I.12) — and throw javelins heavier than service weight (I.14). The principle is deliberate overload, so that the real task is met with a surplus.
*Text note:* the marching load is often given as "sixty pounds exclusive of arms," which is Clarke's wording at I.19. The Latin says *Pondus quoque baiulare usque ad LX libras* — "up to sixty pounds" — and the reason given is *necessitas… annonam pariter et arma portandi*, the necessity of carrying rations **and** arms. Clarke's "not less than sixty pounds (exclusive of their arms)" reverses the Latin's ceiling into a floor and supplies the parenthesis. Use the Latin.
*Limits:* He attaches no limit, which is itself a weakness; and he concedes the whole practice had lapsed.

**5. Constant practice is the entire content of the art.**
*Locus:* II.23–24.
*His reasoning:* *Postremo sciendum est in pugna usum amplius prodesse quam uires; nam si doctrina cesset armorum, nihil paganus distat a milite* — practice avails more than strength; and if instruction in arms lapses, the civilian differs in nothing from the soldier. The badge, the oath, the pay and the title are not the soldier; the daily repetition is.
*Limits:* Practice must be daily and year-round — hence the covered drill halls for winter (II.23). Intermittent practice is treated as equivalent to none: troops long unemployed "must be considered in the same light as recruits" (III.9).

**6. Fortify every night.**
*Locus:* I.21–25; III.8.
*His reasoning:* A camp well chosen and entrenched is "a fortified city which they can build for their safety wherever they please." Its absence is the cause of surprise by day and night, and of an army that, checked in the field, has nowhere to fall back to. He gives the dimensions (I.24): ditch nine feet wide by seven deep for ordinary use; twelve by nine with a four-foot parapet when the enemy presses harder. The odd-number rule belongs elsewhere and I had it backwards: at III.8 the trench dug by a marching army is "nine, eleven or thirteen feet broad. Or, if they are under great apprehensions of the enemy, they enlarge it to seventeen feet (it being a general rule to observe odd numbers)" — a rule for the field camp under threat, not for permanent camps.
*Limits:* Work is timed to threat — a one-night camp gets only turf and stakes — and the digging is covered by the cavalry and the exempt half of the infantry drawn up in order of battle (I.24–25).

**7. Supply is the primary weapon; famine beats steel.**
*Locus:* III.3; III.9; III.26.
*His reasoning:* *In omni expeditione unum est et maximum telum, ut tibi sufficiat uictus, hostes frangat inopia* — in every campaign the one and greatest weapon is that food suffice for you and want break the enemy. Time repairs other misfortunes; this one has no remedy once it has happened. And at III.9: *nam fames, ut dicitur, intrinsecus pugnat et uincit saepius sine ferro* — famine fights from within and more often conquers without the sword.
*Limits:* Stockpiling is worthless without rationing from the first day: *numquam periclitati sunt fame qui frugalitatem inter copias seruare coeperunt* (IV.7) — those who begin keeping frugality in the midst of plenty have never been endangered by famine. "When provisions once begin to fail, parsimony is ill-timed and comes too late" (III.3).

**8. Know the enemy's habits, not just his numbers.**
*Locus:* III.9; IV.27.
*His reasoning:* At III.9, "It is essential to know the character of the enemy and of their principal officers — whether they be rash or cautious, enterprising or timid, whether they fight on principle or from chance." At IV.27 he makes it a universal: *Non solum in obsidionibus sed in uniuerso genere bellorum supra omnia ducitur hostium consuetudinem explorare diligenter ac nosse* — not only in sieges but in every kind of war it is reckoned above all things to scout out and know the enemy's habits. The reason is precise: opportunity for ambush cannot be found unless you know what hours he relaxes at.
*Limits:* Intelligence on one's own side is to be taken from officers of proven veracity, not informers (III.4); guides are to be guarded, bribed and threatened at once, and verified by separate interrogation and comparison (III.6).

**9. Conceal your own designs absolutely; consult widely, decide narrowly.**
*Locus:* III.6; III.26.
*His reasoning:* "The security of an expedition depends on the concealment of all motions from the enemy" — the Minotaur was a legionary ensign because the general's plans should be as impenetrable as the labyrinth. And in the maxims: consult many on what should be done, but communicate what you intend to do "to few, and those only of the most assured fidelity; or rather trust no one but yourself."
*Limits:* Consultation is still required, and flattery is to be driven out of the council (III.9). The secrecy is about execution, not about deliberation.

**10. Decline the general engagement; win by attrition, stratagem and surprise.**
*Locus:* III.9; III.26.
*His reasoning:* *Boni enim duces non aperto proelio, in quo est commune periculum, sed ex occulto semper adtemptant* — good generals do not attempt things in open battle, where the danger is shared, but always from concealment. Pitched battle is decided in two or three hours, after which the loser has no hope left; therefore everything else is to be thought of, tried and done before it is reached. "It is much better to overcome the enemy by famine, surprise or terror than by general actions, for in the latter instance fortune has often a greater share than valor."
*Limits:* Two conditions license the battle: opportunity, or necessity — "Good officers never engage in general actions unless induced by opportunity or obliged by necessity" (III.26). And one condition forbids it absolutely: an irresolute army (III.9).

**11. Fight only when your own troops expect to win.**
*Locus:* III.9; III.12; III.26.
*His reasoning:* *Numquam ad certamen publicum produxeris militem, nisi cum eum uideris sperare uictoriam.* The general is instructed to read the troops' faces, words, actions and movements on the morning of the action, to distrust the eagerness of the young, and to take the reluctance of veterans as decisive evidence against fighting.
*Limits:* Morale can be manufactured in advance — by small successful actions against weak detachments, by repeated harmless exposure to the sight of the enemy until it stops being frightening (III.12), and by a harangue in which the general himself must appear unafraid.

**12. Build the army up through graduated contact before risking it.**
*Locus:* III.10.
*His reasoning:* Raw or long-idle troops are to be blooded on dispersed foragers, sleeping quarters, river crossings, defiles — targets chosen so that "such measures should be pursued at first as can produce no very bad effects if they should happen to miscarry, but would be of great advantage if attended with success." The veterans gain experience and the young gain nerve.
*Limits:* He says this is easier with fresh men than with beaten ones: "it is easier to form young soldiers and inspire them with proper notions of honor than to reanimate troops who have been once disheartened."

**13. Position often outweighs valour; choose the ground, the sun, the wind and the dust.**
*Locus:* III.13–14; III.26.
*His reasoning:* *Amplius prodest locus saepe quam uirtus.* Weapons thrown downhill strike harder; the man climbing fights the slope and the enemy at once. Infantry wants broken, enclosed, ditched, wooded, sometimes mountainous ground; cavalry wants open plain. And the general must anticipate not the present sun and wind but where they will be later in the day.
*Limits:* Ground is chosen relative to your own arm of service, not in the abstract; the same ground that is good for your infantry is bad for your cavalry.

**14. Keep a reserve; never spend depth to buy frontage.**
*Locus:* III.15; III.17; III.26.
*His reasoning:* "It is more advantageous to engage in close order th[a]n to extend your line too much. An army that takes up too much ground in front and too little in depth, is quickly penetrated by the enemy's first onset. After this there is no remedy." (The 1940 text as transcribed reads "close order that to extend"; I have bracketed the obvious slip rather than correct it silently.) The reserve, which he credits to the Lacedaemonians and then the Carthaginians (III.17), exists so that the wedge, the pincers and the saw can be formed without drawing men out of the line — because drawing men out of the line is itself the disorder.
*Limits:* He is explicit that this matters most to the weaker side: "In armies not very numerous, it is much better to contract the front, and to have strong reserves."

**15. Give the beaten enemy a road out.**
*Locus:* III.21.
*His reasoning:* *Sed clausis ex desperatione crescit audacia, et cum spei nihil est, sumit arma formido* — in men shut in, boldness grows out of despair, and when there is no hope, fear takes up arms. He cites Scipio's maxim that a road should be built for the enemy to flee by. Once the way out is open, minds agree on flight, the fleeing throw away their arms, and the killing is done without risk to the pursuer.
*Limits:* The converse is the warning: "men when shut up, although weak and few in number, become a match for the enemy from this very reflection, that they have no resource but in despair."

**16. Defeat is a position, not an ending.**
*Locus:* III.25; III.10.
*His reasoning:* Where losses are roughly equal, "he is reputed to have the superiority who bears up against his misfortunes with greatest resolution" — so seize the spoils first and celebrate first. After a real defeat: reassemble, re-arm, re-levy, and immediately look for a chance to ambush the victors, because "the nature of the human mind is apt to be too much elated and to act with too little caution in prosperity." And the governing sentence of the whole book: "no one should ever despair of effecting what has been already performed."
*Limits:* This is contingent on having prepared for defeat before the battle — a mountain at your back, a fortified post in rear, a body of troops told off to cover the retreat (III.25). Resilience is a logistics decision made in advance, not a disposition discovered afterwards.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

Vegetius addresses this **directly and at length**, more directly than any other text on this list except Aeneas Tacticus. This is not inference. The whole book is written from the losing side of a decline, for a state that believes itself outnumbered and out-bred by the barbarians, and a large part of Book III is explicitly indexed to inferiority. The Latin chapter heading of III.20 says so: *Quot generibus pugna publica committatur et quomodo etiam qui inferior numero et uiribus est ualeat obtinere* — "in how many ways a pitched battle may be joined, and how even he who is inferior in number and strength may prevail."

What he says to the weaker party, in his own order:

**Do not fight the strong man's fight.** III.9: "if he knows himself inferior, he must avoid general actions and endeavor to succeed by surprises, ambuscades and stratagems. These, when skillfully managed by good generals, have often given them the victory over enemies superior both in numbers and strength." The general engagement is where fortune has "a greater share than valor" — i.e. it is the high-variance option, and the weaker party has the least capacity to absorb variance.

**Substitute discipline for mass, deliberately and as policy.** III.1: "The ancients, taught by experience, preferred discipline to numbers." His argument is that mass is actively a liability — a large army is slow, strung out on the march, vulnerable in defiles and at rivers, impossible to forage for, and when it breaks, it breaks catastrophically. He blames the destruction of the armies of Xerxes, Darius and Mithridates on their own numbers rather than on their enemies' bravery. This is a genuine claim that scale has diseconomies, not a consolation prize.

**Substitute composition for numbers.** III.16: if your cavalry is weaker, do not try to match it — intermingle trained light infantry among the troopers, one man between every two horsemen, and "even though the flower of the enemy's cavalry should attack you, they will never be able to cope with this mixed disposition." He calls this "the only resource of the old generals to supply the defects of their cavalry." The move is to change the kind of contest rather than the size of your force.

**Concentrate your best on one point rather than spreading quality evenly.** III.20: "If you intend to engage with your right wing only, it must be composed of your best troops... Victory in general is gained by a small number of men." Three of the seven formations (the second, sixth and seventh) are explicitly the inferior side's formations. The second is "the best disposition" precisely *because* "although your army consists of few troops, yet good and advantageously posted, it will greatly contribute to your obtaining the victory, notwithstanding the numbers and bravery of the enemy": refuse one wing, hold it out of range, and throw everything at one enemy flank. The sixth is for the man who "cannot depend either on the number or courage of his troops." The seventh is for the man whose forces "are few and weak in comparison to the enemy": anchor one flank on terrain — an eminence, the sea, a river, a lake, a city, a marsh — so that the enemy's surplus has nowhere to go.

**Let terrain do the work numbers would have done.** *Amplius prodest locus saepe quam uirtus.* The seventh formation is that maxim turned into a procedure.

**Starve him rather than face him.** III.26: "To distress the enemy more by famine than the sword is a mark of consummate skill." This is the weak party's highest-leverage move because it costs no battle and inverts the usual relationship between resources and outcome: the besieger who cannot feed himself loses to the besieged who rationed from day one.

**Take his men rather than kill them.** III.26: "an adversary is more hurt by desertion than by slaughter," and a general should "sow dissension among his adversaries, for no nation, though ever so weak in itself can be completely ruined by its enemies unless its fall be facilitated by its own distraction" (III.10). Subversion is cheaper than force and scales better from a small base.

**Recover the lost art from books.** I.8. This is the one piece of advice Vegetius gives that is addressed to a man with no teacher, no institution and no living tradition — which is, structurally, the position of the man who enters with nothing. His answer is that the tradition exists in writing and can be reconstructed by a reader. He is also honest about the limits: the historians "only relate the exploits and events of wars, and take no notice of the objects of our present enquiries, which they considered as universally known." The practical knowledge is the part that does not get written down, and that is exactly why he is writing.

What he does **not** offer the weak: any doctrine for fighting without a state. Every one of the above presupposes a force, a treasury, a fortified place, or a province. Vegetius has nothing on insurgency, nothing on raising forces outside a state, nothing on the political problem of getting the resources in the first place. His "weak" party is a weak empire, not a man with nothing.

And one passage should be quoted against any sentimental reading of him. At IV.7, listing how a besieged town survives: *Inbellis quoque aetas ac sexus propter necessitatem uictus portis frequenter exclusa est, ne penuria obprimeret armatos, a quibus moenia seruabantur* — "the unwarlike in age and sex have often been shut out of the gates because of the necessity of food, lest scarcity crush the armed men by whom the walls were held." He records it without comment. That is the register of the book: what works, at what price, stated flat.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** The dominant theme; the book is about nothing else. Peace is preparation time and is squandered by definition: "in the midst of peace, war is looked upon as an object too distant to merit consideration" (III.10). His entire causal story of Roman decline is that a twenty-year peace after the First Punic War made the Romans unfit to face Hannibal, and that the long peace of his own day did the same. The famous sentence is not a policy of deterrence but a statement about when the work gets done: *Igitur qui desiderat pacem, praeparet bellum.*

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Strong. Written topographical description of the theatre with distances in miles, road quality, by-roads, mountains and rivers; where possible, plans drawn on the spot "that they might regulate their marches by the eye" (III.6). Guides separately examined and cross-compared. Spies constantly out, and preferably by night, "for if they are taken prisoners, you have, as it were, betrayed yourself." Deserters encouraged; the enemy's men tampered with. And the character-reading of the opposing commander at III.9. What he lacks is any method for weighing conflicting reports beyond comparison for consistency.

**Timing.** Present, mostly negative. "Opportunity in war is often more to be depended on than courage" (III.26) — but the emphasis falls on not fighting at the wrong moment: not after a long march ("What can a soldier do who charges when out of breath?"), not before the troops expect to win, not before the army is clear of its own gates. The positive doctrine of timing is the choice between protracting and forcing a decision (III.9), decided by which side's cohesion decays faster with time.

**Terrain and position.** Strong, and treated as partly substitutable for force. Highest ground preferred; ground chosen to match your own arm; sun, wind and dust anticipated for the whole day, not the moment; one flank anchored on an obstacle when you are weak; camps sited for water, wood, forage, salubrity and command. *Amplius prodest locus saepe quam uirtus.*

**Deception and information.** Strong and explicit. The Minotaur ensign as the emblem of the general's impenetrability (III.6). "Those designs are best which the enemy are entirely ignorant of till the moment of execution" (III.26). "Dispositions for action must be carefully concealed from the enemy, lest they should counteract them and defeat your plans by proper expedients" (III.26). The feint-retreat-and-intercept trap (III.22). The besieger who feigns despair and withdraws, and the besieged who is therefore told to watch *harder* after the enemy leaves, with dogs and geese on the towers (IV.26). Also the countermeasure: "On finding the enemy has notice of your designs, you must immediately alter your plan of operations."

**Alliances.** Weak and hostile. He treats allied and auxiliary troops as a structural liability: "hired corps of foreigners assembled from different parts of the Empire, made up of different numbers, without knowledge of one another or any tie of affection. Each nation has its own peculiar discipline, customs and manner of fighting" (II.2). Value comes from a force "actuated with the same spirit" and governed by "one and the same order." He records as an invariable rule that auxiliaries never outnumber citizens (III.1), and he says it is cheaper to train your own than to hire foreigners (I.28). He has nothing on coalition-making as a strategic instrument.

**Logistics and economy.** The strongest practical section in the book. Calculate troop numbers and costs before opening the war; requisition in kind, commute to gold where the province cannot deliver; stockpile "in more than sufficient quantity" in the strongest and most convenient cities before the campaign; strip the countryside of anything useful to the enemy and compel the population inside the walls; repair the fortifications and engines in advance; ration from the first day (III.3, IV.7). And the political sentence underneath it: *Neque enim diuitiarum secura possessio est, nisi armorum defensione seruetur* — there is no secure possession of wealth unless it is kept by the defence of arms.

**Morale and discipline.** Central, and unsentimentally mechanical. Morale is produced by competence, by small early wins, by repeated harmless exposure to the enemy, by the general's visible confidence, and by money the soldier cannot spend — the compulsory half-donative deposit at the standards, because "the soldier who knows all his fortune is deposited at his colors, entertains no thoughts of desertion" (II.20). Discipline is produced by constant employment and by exemplary punishment of ringleaders only. And the split that matters: "Punishment, and fear thereof, are necessary to keep soldiers in order in quarters; but in the field they are more influenced by hope and rewards" (III.26). On mutiny: identify the few agitators, detach them under cover of an apparently favourable posting, "with such address that though he wants to get rid of them, they may think themselves employed by preference and favor" (III.4).

**The decisive point.** Present in operational form, not as a general theory. The doctrine is concentration of quality at one point — the wedge formed from the reserve to pierce the line, the best troops massed on the single attacking wing while the other is refused, "Victory in general is gained by a small number of men" (III.20). There is no Clausewitzian notion of a centre of gravity; the decisive point is a place on a battlefield, chosen relative to the enemy's weaker flank.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Present only as the danger of pursuit. "He who rashly pursues a flying enemy with troops in disorder, seems inclined to resign that victory which he had before obtained" (III.26). The legion does not pursue; pursuit is left to light troops and cavalry, because a line that chases breaks itself (II.17). And "A rash and inconsiderate pursuit exposes an army to the greatest danger possible... The greater the security, the greater the danger" (III.22). He has no concept of a strategic culminating point in Clausewitz's sense.

**When not to fight.** The best-developed negative doctrine in the book. Do not fight: when your troops do not expect to win; when veterans are reluctant; when the army is raw or long unemployed; immediately after a long march; while defiling from a gate with the enemy already formed; when the ground favours the enemy's arm; when you can starve, surprise or terrify him instead; and in general at all, unless opportunity or necessity compels it. "Every plan, therefore, is to be considered, every expedient tried and every method taken before matters are brought to this last extremity" (III.9).

**Defence versus offence.** He does not theorise the pair, but his practice is defensive-offensive throughout: a fortified camp every night as the base from which offensive action is launched; Book IV giving the defender the longer and better half of the siege treatment. His fortification doctrine is explicitly about geometry rather than mass — walls built not straight but with re-entrant angles and frequent towers at the corners, so that the attacker who brings up ladders or engines is caught "not only from the front but also from the flanks and almost from the rear, as if enclosed in a fold" (IV.2); the wall backed with rammed earth from the ditch so that even if the stone is destroyed the mass behind it stands in the wall's place (IV.3). The general posture is: make yourself unprofitable to attack, then attack him when he is unprofitable to defend.

**The character required of the commander.** Scattered but consistent. Diligence rather than brilliance. He must "assume the most respectable authority and maintain it by severity," punish with "the greatest rigor of the laws," and be known as inexorable — but "it is much more to the credit of a general to form his troops to submission and obedience by habit and discipline than to be obliged to force them to their duty by the terror of punishment" (III.4). He must know if possible "the name and capacity of every count, tribune, subaltern and soldier" (III.10). He must banish flattery and overconfidence from his own council (III.9). He must not change his dispositions once the action has begun (III.19). And after a defeat he must be the one who does not despair, because where losses are equal the reputation of victory goes to whoever behaves as though he has it.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The scholarly critique: he is not evidence.** The central modern objection, made most forcefully by Walter Goffart in *Traditio* 33 (1977), is that generations of historians read Vegetius as a witness to the decay of the late Roman army when he is nothing of the kind. Goffart's charge is that the error lay in the "presupposition that he was an historian." He is a prescriptive moralist writing to persuade an emperor, and his rhetorical need is a degraded present against which an idealised antiquity can shine. His "ancient legion" of 6,100 foot and 726 horse corresponds to no attested formation of any single period; it is a synthesis assembled out of Cato, Celsus, Frontinus, Paternus and imperial constitutions spanning centuries. His insistence on heavy infantry is pedagogical, not descriptive, in an era when cavalry was ascendant. Goffart also notes the paradox that Vegetius sometimes holds up barbarian practice as the model Romans should copy — the Persians entrenching their camps, the barbarians circling their wagons (III.10) — which is not what a decline narrative looks like. Anyone mining this book for facts about the fourth- or fifth-century army is mining a polemic.

**Delbrück's verdict.** Hans Delbrück, *Geschichte der Kriegskunst* vol. II (Berlin, 1921), treats Vegetius as an unmilitary compiler whose numbers and formations cannot be trusted; the 1940 introduction to the Clarke text concedes as much in passing, calling Delbrück's brief discussion "very acute" — an odd thing to print in a preface to the book itself, and a fair warning.

**Sydney Anglo's verdict: "the triumph of mediocrity."** In *The Antiquaries Journal* 82 (2002), 247–67, Anglo argues that Vegetius' millennium-long authority was a function of there being nothing else, not of quality; that as other ancient sources and modern military literature became available his standing among serious scholars collapsed; and that his greatest visual prominence, in Johann von Wallhausen's early-seventeenth-century translations and plates, was also his last. The reputation and the merit are two different objects and should be tracked separately.

**Christopher Allmand's reception history** (*The De Re Militari of Vegetius*, Cambridge, 2011) documents how thoroughly the text was reworked in transmission: more than 300 manuscripts, vernacular translations across Europe, and heavy mediation through John of Salisbury, Giles of Rome, Alfonso the Wise and Christine de Pizan. The relevant failure mode is that most medieval and Renaissance readers met Vegetius through digests and had no access to a context in which his advice made sense. In his BMCR review, Marco Formisano pushes back on Allmand for treating the text as a historical source rather than as a literary construction in its own right.

**The Caxton error.** The 1940 *Roots of Strategy* introduction states that *De Re Militari* "was first published in English by Caxton, from an English manuscript copy, in 1489." That is a conflation, and it has been repeated in a great deal of popular writing since. What Caxton printed at Westminster on 14 July 1489 was the *Boke of the Fayt of Armes and of Chyualrye*, an English version of Christine de Pizan's *Livre des fais d'armes et de chevalerie* — a book which, as its own colophon says, its author "made and drewe out of the boke named vegecius de re militari and out of tharbre of bataylles." A derivative, not the text. An English translation of Vegetius proper had been made in 1408 for Thomas of Berkeley and survives in eleven manuscripts (ed. Geoffrey Lester, 1988), but was not printed until modern times; the first *printed* English Vegetius is normally given as John Sadler's *The Foure Bookes of Flauius Vegetius Renatus*, London, Thomas Marshe, 1572.

**The "si vis pacem" problem.** The single most quoted sentence attributed to Vegetius is not his. The phrase *si vis pacem, para bellum* appears nowhere in the four books; I grepped the whole cached Latin text for it and for *para bellum* and found nothing. What he wrote is *Igitur qui desiderat pacem, praeparet bellum* (III, preface), and in context it is not a doctrine of armed deterrence at all. It stands as the third of four conclusions drawn from a paragraph about the *Lacedaemonian* professionalisation of war — Xanthippus destroying Regulus by skill alone, Hannibal hiring a Spartan adviser — and its companions are "he who wants victory, let him train his soldiers diligently" and "he who wants good outcomes, let him fight by art, not by chance." The subject of the sentence is *training*, not armament. The condensed proverb detached it from that paragraph and turned it into a slogan for arms spending; the German firm Deutsche Waffen- und Munitionsfabriken took *Parabellum* as its telegraphic address, which is how a cartridge came to be named after half a sentence of a late-Roman reformer's argument for drill.

**The self-help and business misreading.** Vegetius is thin in the business-strategy literature compared with Sun Tzu, and where he appears it is usually as this one detached maxim plus "few men are born brave; many become so through care and force of discipline," repurposed as a line about corporate training. That second one is at least a fair use of his actual argument. The systematic distortion is the first: taking a book whose central claim is that *institutions decay when they stop practising* and reducing it to a bumper sticker about preparedness. His real thesis is harder and less flattering — that the reason the thing is broken is that everyone stopped doing the boring daily repetition, that nobody now alive knows how to teach it, and that the only way back is through books and an unreasonable amount of drudgery.

**The internal failure mode.** Vegetius is credulous about his own remedy. He believes an emperor's decree plus a reading programme can rebuild a lost craft, and he was wrong: nothing in the late Roman army changed because of him. He also has almost nothing to say about war's political object, about money beyond commissariat, or about what to do when the state itself is the problem. And his own source note at I.8 — *Nihil enim mihi auctoritatis adsumo* — should be taken literally. When he asserts a Roman practice, he is reporting what an older book said, and the older book is usually lost.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

**Start with the hardest thing Vegetius actually says, which is about him and not about armies.** At I.8 he faces exactly the situation of a man starting from zero: the craft he wants exists, but there is no one left who can teach it. *Quem inuenias, qui docere possit quod ipse non didicit?* His answer is that the practice must be reconstructed from books, and then — this is the part usually skipped — that the books will not be enough, because writers record outcomes and omit the procedures they assume everyone knows. That is an accurate description of what a man with no network faces when he tries to learn anything from the outside. The transferable conclusion is not "read more." It is: expect the written record to be missing precisely the operational middle, and budget for reconstructing it yourself by repetition and failure, because nobody wrote it down.

**Competence produces confidence, not the reverse.** *Scientia enim rei bellicae dimicandi nutrit audaciam: nemo facere metuit quod se bene didicisse confidit.* This inverts the standard advice given to people with nothing, which is to act confident until opportunity arrives. Vegetius' claim is the opposite and is better supported: nerve is a by-product of drilled competence, and a man who has not drilled will discover that his nerve is not there when he needs it. The practical form: do not try to manufacture the feeling; manufacture the skill and take the feeling as a symptom.

**Daily repetition is the whole of it, and intermittent effort counts as none.** *Postremo sciendum est in pugna usum amplius prodesse quam uires; nam si doctrina cesset armorum, nihil paganus distat a milite.* And at III.9, troops that have merely been idle for some years "must be considered in the same light as recruits." This is the least flattering and most useful thing in the book for someone starting with nothing, because the one resource he certainly has is time he is not being paid for. The Roman answer to bad weather was to build a covered hall and drill in it, which is the correct posture toward every reason not to practise today.

**Overload deliberately.** Double-weight wooden swords, heavier javelins, sixty pounds on the march — so that the real event is met with margin. What transfers is the structure, not the kit: rehearse the thing you will have to do under conditions worse than the ones you will face, so that the actual occasion is the easy version.

**Refuse the fair fight.** This is the most directly portable doctrine in the text, and it is stated for exactly the man who has less: "if he knows himself inferior, he must avoid general actions and endeavor to succeed by surprises, ambuscades and stratagems." The general engagement is the high-variance option, and variance is a luxury good — it is affordable only by whoever can survive losing. A man with no capital, no reputation and no second chance cannot afford a contest decided in two or three hours against a better-resourced opponent, however much the contest flatters him by being offered. Vegetius' rule is the discipline of declining: keep taking the low-variance, asymmetric action until conditions change.

**Concentrate quality on one point and refuse everywhere else.** The second formation — hold the weak wing back out of range and throw your best at one enemy flank — is the concrete version. "Victory in general is gained by a small number of men." The failure mode of a man with nothing is spreading a thin resource across every opportunity, because each one looks cheap. Vegetius' arithmetic says the opposite: thin frontage is penetrated at the first shock, "after this there is no remedy," and therefore depth at one point beats presence at many.

**Anchor a flank.** The seventh formation is for the man who is simply outnumbered: put one side of yourself against something the opponent cannot get around — an eminence, a river, a city, the sea. The transferable form is structural, not motivational: find the one fixed thing in your situation that cannot be taken from you (a skill nobody else in your reach has, a place you have standing, a constraint that binds your competitor and not you) and build so that your exposed side is the only side.

**Ration from the first day, not from the day it starts to hurt.** *numquam periclitati sunt fame qui frugalitatem inter copias seruare coeperunt* — and its companion, "when provisions once begin to fail, parsimony is ill-timed and comes too late." This is the most concrete money advice in any of the thirteen texts. Frugality adopted while there is still a surplus is a strategic weapon; frugality adopted at the point of shortage is a symptom. The man with nothing usually gets this exactly backwards, spending on the first inflow and economising only under pressure, at which point the economy buys nothing.

**Do not start until you can finish.** "An exact calculation must therefore be made before the commencement of the war as to the number of troops and the expenses incident thereto." Sieges run longer than you think; so does everything else. The specific transferable form: before committing, compute the running cost of the whole thing to its end, then stockpile "in more than sufficient quantity," because the failure will not be at the start.

**Build a fallback before you need one.** III.25: a prudent officer never risks a general action "without taking such precautions as will secure him from any considerable loss in case of a defeat" — a mountain at the back, a fortified post in the rear, a body of troops told off to cover the retreat. Resilience is a decision taken before the event, not a quality discovered during it. The man with nothing tends to treat having no fallback as proof of commitment. Vegetius treats it as the reason armies are destroyed rather than merely beaten.

**Say less about what you are going to do.** "Consult with many on proper measures to be taken, but communicate the plans you intend to put in execution to few... or rather trust no one but yourself." Note the split: he is not telling you to stop taking advice. Deliberation should be wide and should have flattery driven out of it; execution should be silent. Announcing intentions buys the feeling of progress at the price of the surprise that was your only advantage.

**Learn the other party's habits before his strength.** IV.27: the opportunity does not exist until you know what hours he relaxes at. III.9: know whether the man across from you is rash or cautious, enterprising or timid, whether he decides by calculation or by chance. For a man with no position, this is the cheapest edge available — it costs attention, which is free, and it is what the well-resourced consistently neglect because their resources have so far excused them from needing it.

**Build the record.** The pay lists, duty rosters and furlough registers of II.19, kept "with greater exactness" than civil registers, and the deposit scheme of II.20 where the soldier's own money is held where he cannot reach it. Both are institutional answers to individual weakness of will, and both transfer without modification: measure what you do, and put the money somewhere your future self cannot get at it casually.

**Defeat is a position.** "They who were victorious in the end were often unsuccessful in the beginning," and "no one should ever despair of effecting what has been already performed." I would not include these if they stood alone, because as slogans they are worthless. They are worth including because in Vegetius they are load-bearing on a specific mechanism: the victor becomes careless in prosperity, and that carelessness is the beaten party's next opportunity. The claim is not that persistence is rewarded. The claim is that success degrades the successful, and that this is a resource for whoever is still standing.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

*The entire logistics and administrative apparatus.* Requisitioning provinces, commuting taxes to gold, garrisoning cities, building magazines — these presuppose a state. A man with nothing has no province to requisition. What survives the amputation is only the principle underneath: compute the full cost first, rationed consumption from day one, and treat supply as the primary weapon rather than a support function. The machinery does not come with it.

*The selection doctrine.* Books I.2–I.7 are the largest single block of the book and are useless to someone with no one to select. Worse, they are where Vegetius is at his weakest intellectually — climate determinism, physiognomy, the judging of men by face and limb "as in horses and dogs," the exclusion of weavers and confectioners as unmanly. Inverted into self-assessment it becomes fatalism, which is the opposite of his own thesis that discipline makes the man. Leave it.

*The battle formations as formations.* The seven *modi* of III.20 are geometry for a heavy-infantry line and do not map onto anything. Only two abstractions survive: concentrate on one point, and anchor the flank you cannot cover. Anyone extracting more than that is doing what the business-strategy literature does to Sun Tzu.

*The decline narrative.* Vegetius' account of why things are broken — moral laxity, a long peace, better men going into the civil service — is rhetoric aimed at an emperor, and Goffart is right that it is not evidence. A man diagnosing his own situation should not borrow this frame. It is emotionally satisfying and explains nothing, and its practical effect is to locate the cause of failure in other people's degeneracy.

*Restoration as a strategy.* The deepest structural limit. Vegetius' entire programme is: there was a working system, it has been lost, recover it. Nothing changed; the late Roman army did not reform. A man entering the modern world with nothing has no golden age to restore and no emperor to petition, and the one thing Vegetius has no doctrine for is building an institution where none existed. He tells you how to maintain and how to recover. He does not tell you how to found. That is the gap this book cannot fill, and it should be filled from Machiavelli, who is about exactly that.

*The addressee problem itself.* Every recommendation in this book is made to someone who already holds the power to implement it. Vegetius' own strategy — persuade the man at the top to act — failed completely. That is the most honest warning the text gives a reader with nothing: the plan that consists of convincing someone powerful to do the work for you is the plan that did not work for the author.
