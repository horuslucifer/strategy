# 3. Miyamoto Musashi — The Book of Five Rings (Go Rin no Sho)

## 1. Identity

*Go Rin no Sho* (五輪書) is a short technical treatise on swordsmanship and strategy written in Japanese by Shinmen Musashi Fujiwara no Genshin — Miyamoto Musashi — in his last two years. It is roughly 20,000 words in English: five scrolls named Earth (地, *chi*), Water (水, *sui*), Fire (火, *ka*), Wind (風, *fū*) and Void (空, *kū*). Earth lays out the frame and the trade analogies; Water is the manual of single combat; Fire is the tactics of fighting, stated at both the one-on-one and army scale; Wind is a polemic against nine practices of rival schools; Void is two pages of conclusion.

**Composition dates.** Musashi's own opening states the date of beginning: 「時に寛永二十年十月上旬の頃」 — "in the first ten days of the tenth month of Kan'ei 20", i.e. November 1643 — and gives his age as sixty (「年つもりて六十」). Four of the five scrolls carry the colophon 正保二年五月十二日 — Shōhō 2, fifth month, twelfth day — 12 May 1645, a week before he died. So: begun 1643, dated 1645.

**Place and recipient.** He began it at 巌殿山 (Ganden-zan / Mt. Iwato) in Higo, Kyushu — the Reigandō cave behind Unganzen-ji, west of Kumamoto. The recipient is Terao Magonojō (寺尾孫之丞), his senior student. Victor Harris romanises the name as "Teruo Magonojo" and elsewhere "Teruo Nobuyuki"; the standard modern reading is Terao.

**What survives.** No autograph. The text exists only in later copyist lines — principally the Hosokawa-house transmission (generally treated as closest to the original because it descends from the domain's official copying), plus Kusunoki and Maruoka lines with variant readings and passages absent from one another. Alexander Bennett's 2018 translation is advertised as the first English version built on a reconstruction that reconciles the copies and corrects over 150 characters; every earlier English version, Harris's included, works from one uncorrected copy line. Textual variance is not trivial: the Japanese text I consulted dates the Water scroll 五月十三日 (thirteenth day) where Harris has the twelfth, and closes the Water scroll with a sentence Harris does not render at all (see §6).

**Translation used.** I read the complete Victor Harris translation (first published Overlook Press, 1974), from a scanned plain-text copy on the Internet Archive (item `MiyamotoMusashi-BookOfFiveRingsgoRinNoSho`), saved locally as `sources/musashi_harris.txt`. That translation is in copyright; the Archive copy is an unauthorised scan, so I have used it to read and to grep, and have kept every quotation from it under twenty-five words with the translator named. I could not obtain Cleary, Wilson, Tokitsu or Bennett. To compensate, I scraped the Japanese original (all 89 sections) from koten.net's 日本古典文学摘集 edition and checked the load-bearing passages against it directly; where Harris and the Japanese diverge I say so and quote the Japanese. For the Dokkōdō I used Colin Hyakutake-Watkin's 1992 translation at hyoho.com, which is signed and dated and gives the romanised Japanese beside each precept, but is loose and self-described as "a summary of the basic meanings" — usable for sense, not for wording. For the Bushidō context I used the Project Gutenberg text of Nitobe, `sources/nitobe_bushido.txt` (13th edition, 1908; first preface dated Malvern, Pennsylvania, Twelfth Month 1899).

**Correcting the blog.** The foundation list's source (Saucedo, dogmadoctrineblog.org, 10 Feb 2021) covers Musashi across entries 5, 6 and 7. Its errors, in order of seriousness:

- **The blog says** Musashi "abandoned metal swordplay for a wooden two-handed style he mastered and called 'Two Heavens, One Style.'" **The evidence says** this inverts the doctrine. Niten Ichi-ryū (二天一流, "two heavens, one school") is a **two-sword** style — one sword in each hand — and the whole point of the Earth scroll section titled 「此一流二刀と名付る事」 ("On naming this school Two Swords") is an argument *against* holding one sword in two hands. Musashi lists where the two-handed grip fails: on horseback, at a run, on marsh, pond, stony ground, steep roads, in a crowd. He writes 「太刀を両手にて持てあしき事」 ("holding the long sword in both hands is bad") and 「両手に太刀を構ふる事実の道に非ず」 — Harris: "To hold the long sword in both hands is not the true Way." The blog has reported the opposite of what the text argues. The "wooden" half of the error has a real fact under it — Musashi fought Sasaki Kojirō with a bokken cut from a boat's oar, and after 1612 appears to have stopped using live blades in duels — but "wooden" has nothing to do with *ni-ten*, and the treatise itself is about cutting and killing with steel.
- **The blog says** he "killed his first person by the sword at thirteen." **The evidence says** the weapon was not a sword. Musashi's own text says only that at thirteen he "struck down" (勝負を為す / uchi-otoshi) Arima Kihei of the Shintō school. The detailed account, from the *Niten-ki* and repeated by both Harris and G. Cameron Hurst III, has the boy carrying a short sword and a six-foot stick, throwing Arima to the ground and beating him to death. Hurst calls the story "probably an exaggeration."
- **The blog says** "His father trained Musashi at an early age in the art of Kenjitsu." **The evidence says** there is no record of it, and Musashi denies having a teacher. The preface states it flatly: 「兵法の利にまかせて諸芸諸能の道となせば万事に於て我に師匠なし」 — in all matters I have no teacher; Harris renders it "all things with no teacher." Hurst is explicit: "we have no other record of a teacher," and offers the father's possible survival past 1600 only as a *hypothesis* to explain the boy's precocity. Munisai was a *jitte* man, not a swordsmanship instructor to his son on any evidence we have.
- **The blog says** he "lived the rest of his days in a cave." **The evidence says** roughly the last two years of a sixty-year life, and probably not to the end. He was a guest of the Hosokawa at Kumamoto from about 1640, retired to Reigandō in 1643 to write, and per the *Niten-ki* was back at his residence inside Chiba castle under treatment when he wrote the Dokkōdō on 12 May 1645 and died a week later.
- **The blog says** the book was "written in 1645." **The evidence says** begun in the tenth month of Kan'ei 20 (1643) by Musashi's own dating and completed under the date 12 May 1645. Two years, not one act.
- **The blog says** it "is not a military strategy of being a great commander like Arthashastra and The Art of War," then immediately concedes Musashi thought it scaled. **The evidence says** the concession should be the main clause. The Fire scroll states almost every maxim twice, once for 大分 (large-scale) and once for 一分 (single combat); the Earth scroll's carpenter-foreman section is a short theory of personnel selection and command; Wind discusses battle array. What is genuinely absent is not the claim to command but any evidence Musashi ever exercised it — see §8.
- **The blog says** Bushidō was "a complex Japanese warrior code that evolved throughout the medieval era in Japan." **The evidence says** this is the standard popular error. Karl Friday: "the term was not used to designate a code of warrior behavior until the early modern era" and was rare in that sense before the late nineteenth century — Nitobe, whose 1899 book created the Western idea of it, believed he had coined the word himself. Oleg Benesch's *Inventing the Way of the Samurai* (OUP, 2014) argues bushidō emerged in the late 1880s out of a Meiji identity search, borrowing heavily from contemporary European chivalry and gentleman ideals. Musashi died in 1645, generations before any of this. He does not write "bushidō"; he writes 兵法 (*heihō*, strategy) and 武士の道 (the warrior's way), and what he means by the latter is technical and competitive, not ethical. Nitobe himself puts Musashi on the far side of the line: "The knight errantry of Miyamoto Musashi is now a tale of the past."
- The blog's "Harima province" is, for once, better than its critics. Musashi's own first line calls him 「生国播磨の武士」 — a warrior born in Harima. The competing Mimasaka claim (Miyamoto village) comes from the Kokura monument tradition and is what Harris and Hurst follow. The birthplace is genuinely contested; the blog picked the reading Musashi himself gives.

## 2. The world the text assumes

Musashi writes at the end of the world that produced him, for men who will not live in it. Japan had been consolidated by Nobunaga, Hideyoshi and Ieyasu; the Tokugawa peace was forty years old and would last two hundred more. The last substantial violence in Musashi's lifetime was the Shimabara rising of 1637–38, in which he served in a non-combatant staff role and, per one account, was knocked off his horse by a thrown stone.

The conditions he takes for granted:

**A frozen society with a class of surplus fighters.** Hideyoshi's sword-hunt fixed the four estates — warrior, farmer, artisan, merchant — and gave only the samurai two swords. The wars then ended, leaving thousands of masterless *rōnin* with a licensed skill and no demand for it. Musashi was one, from boyhood until he was about fifty-seven. His entire method is an answer to a specific market failure: how a man with no lord, no land and no stipend converts a skill into a reputation that a lord will eventually buy.

**Duelling as the marketplace.** A *rōnin*'s route to employment was to beat a school's students or its master in public. Contests were held at shrines, in streets, in castle grounds and on borrowed islands, with real or wooden weapons, to disablement or death, sometimes under domain supervision. Musashi's Kojirō match in 1612 was an official one, arranged through a Hosokawa retainer. This is the assumed world of §4 below: reputation is won by direct, witnessed, individual defeat of a named holder of position.

**The fencing school as a business.** Every domain sponsored a *dōjō*. Two — Ittō-ryū and Yagyū-ryū — enjoyed Tokugawa patronage; the Yagyū eventually staffed the shogunate's intelligence apparatus. Musashi is writing against this industry and says so: 「兵法をうりものにしける」 in effect — Harris renders it "Men use equipment to sell their own selves," and adds "As if with the nut and the flower, the nut has become less than the flower." His hostility to secret transmissions, written pledges, licence grades and "interior" teachings is hostility to a competitor's business model, not neutral philosophy. Read him as an unfunded outsider attacking the credentialing system of an industry that had no place for him.

**Weapons with known, narrow envelopes.** Bow, matchlock gun, spear, halberd, long sword, companion sword, horse. He costs each out by range and terrain: the gun supreme from behind fortifications and before the ranks close, useless afterwards; the bow inferior past about forty yards and in sieges, but you can watch the arrow and correct, which you cannot do with shot; the halberd defensive against the spear's initiative; neither spear nor halberd any use indoors or for taking prisoners. This is an inventory of instruments with hard limits, in a world where the instruments do not change year to year.

**A Buddhist–Shintō cosmology he uses as a filing system and not much else.** The five scroll names are the Buddhist *godai*; he opens by praying to Kannon and bowing to heaven. But he says outright he wrote the book without recourse to Buddhist law, Confucian teaching, old war chronicles or military manuals, and the Void scroll spends most of its length attacking the idea that not-understanding is the same as emptiness. The religious furniture is real and is not load-bearing.

**Nothing resembling a state, a supply train, an alliance or a treasury.** Musashi never commanded a unit, never held a fief, never had subordinates beyond students. The gaps in §5 follow from this and are not oversights.

## 3. Core doctrine

Eighteen principles. Loci are by scroll and by Musashi's own section headings, which the Japanese numbers (地之巻 一–九, etc.); English section titles are Harris's.

1. **The art is one, and scales; the principle is the unit, not the technique.** *Earth, "Outline of the Five Books"; Water, preamble.* His reasoning: the spirit of defeating one man is the same as the spirit of defeating ten million, so a man who can beat one man freely can beat any man; the strategist makes small things into big things "like building a great Buddha from a one foot model." Limit, stated by him: "I cannot write in detail how this is done" — the scaling is asserted, demonstrated nowhere, and he knows it. He offers instead a rule of thumb: large bodies cannot change position quickly, so their movement is predictable; an individual changes his mind instantly, so his is not.

2. **Winning proves nothing about method.** *Earth, Introduction.* He won more than sixty duels between thirteen and twenty-eight or twenty-nine and then, at thirty, looked back and judged that the victories were not due to mastery — "perhaps it was natural ability, or the order of heaven, or that other schools' strategy was inferior." Only after twenty more years of study did he claim to understand. Condition: the audit is retrospective and self-administered; he gives no test by which you could run it on yourself before the fact.

3. **Master a trade, and read strategy through it.** *Earth, "Comparing the Way of the Carpenter to Strategy."* The commander is a foreman carpenter: he must know the architectural theory, keep the master plan, grade his timber (straight unknotted wood for visible pillars; sound but gnarled wood used "discreetly"; weak or knotted wood for scaffolding and then firewood), and allot men by ability — the poorest lay floor joists and carve wedges. Limit: this is a theory of *sorting*, not of developing; Musashi has no notion that a man might be moved up a grade.

4. **Weapons have envelopes; fit the instrument to the ground, and own no favourite.** *Earth, "The Benefit of Weapons in Strategy."* Reasoning: each arm has a range and a terrain where it dominates and outside which it is dead weight, so preference is a defect in itself — "To become over-familiar with one weapon is as much a fault as not knowing it sufficiently well." Two attached conditions: do not copy others, use what you can actually handle; and "Weapons should be hardy rather than decorative."

5. **Free the other hand; train the hard way so the easy way becomes easy.** *Earth, "The Name Ichi Ryu Ni To."* Reasoning: a two-handed grip fails on horseback, at a run, on bad ground and in a crowd, and is impossible if your left hand holds a bow or spear; therefore learn to cut one-handed, and the training method for one-handed cutting is to drill with a sword in each hand. Conditions he himself attaches: this does not apply to spear or halberd; and "when it is difficult to cut an enemy down with one hand, you must use both hands." The two-sword style is explicitly for crowds and for taking prisoners — Harris's note observes that Musashi "never used two swords when up against a skilled swordsman."

6. **Timing is the master category, and the timing that matters is the one underneath.** *Earth, "Timing in Strategy."* Reasoning: timing governs dance, music, archery, riding, the rise and fall of a merchant's capital, and the arc of a warrior's own thriving and declining; within any encounter you must distinguish applicable from inapplicable timing, large from small, fast from slow, and above all read 「間の拍子」 and the *background* timing — "otherwise your strategy will become uncertain." You win by acting in a timing the enemy does not expect. Limit: he concedes it cannot be taught in writing and says all five scrolls are chiefly about it.

7. **Nine standing rules for the student.** *Earth, closing section.* In Harris: do not think dishonestly; the Way is in training; become acquainted with every art; know the Ways of all professions; distinguish between gain and loss in worldly matters; develop intuitive judgement; "Perceive those things which cannot be seen"; "Pay attention even to trifles"; "Do nothing which is of no use." Condition: he prefaces them with a scale requirement — "If you do not look at things on a large scale it will be difficult for you to master strategy."

8. **No second self. Fighting posture is daily posture.** *Water, "Spiritual Bearing" and "Stance."* Reasoning: an elevated spirit is weak and a low spirit is weak; the spirit must be neither tense nor slack, and must not be visible — "Do not let the enemy see your spirit." He then gives a physical checklist (head level, brow unwrinkled, eyes slightly narrowed and unblinking, neck straight, shoulders down, abdomen braced, companion sword wedged against the belly) and demands you "make your everyday stance your combat stance." Limit: none offered; this is stated as unconditional.

9. **Perception over sight; the gaze wide, the attention not on the object.** *Water, "The Gaze"; Wind, "Fixing the Eyes in Other Schools."* Reasoning: fix on the sword, the hands, the face or the feet and your attention is captured and your strategy thwarted. See distant things as near and near things as distant. His analogy in Wind is that ball-players do not watch the ball, and skilled musicians do not stare at the score. In large-scale fighting, "fixing the eyes means gazing at the man's heart."

10. **Attitude-no-attitude: no fixed guard, no fixed formation.** *Water, "The 'Attitude-No-Attitude' Teaching"; Wind, "Use of Attitudes."* Reasoning: the five guards exist, but a guard is "the spirit of awaiting an attack," which is a defensive disposition and therefore a losing one in a duel; guards are for things that should not move — garrisoned castles, battle array. The large-scale statement is blunt: "Fixed formation is bad."

11. **Every movement must terminate in the decisive act.** *Water, "Attitude-No-Attitude."* Reasoning: whether you parry, hit, spring, strike or touch the enemy's sword, you must cut him in the same movement; "If you think only of hitting, springing, striking or touching the enemy, you will not be able actually to cut him." Limit: he distinguishes cutting from slashing — slashing is "nothing more than touching," even if it kills; the difference is whether the spirit was resolved.

12. **Take the ground before the fight and keep taking it during.** *Fire, "Depending on the Place."* Reasoning: put the sun behind you or on your right; keep the entrance behind or to your right; keep your rear unobstructed and your left free; stand higher. Then drive the enemy leftwards into bad footing, thresholds, pillars, obstacles, and do not let him look around to see his own situation.

13. **Three ways to hold the initiative, and no fourth.** *Fire, "The Three Methods to Forestall the Enemy."* *Ken no Sen*, attack first; *Tai no Sen*, forestall him as he attacks (feign weakness, indicate a jump aside, then go in when he relaxes); *Tai Tai no Sen*, move as he moves. Reasoning: "In strategy, you have effectively won when you forestall the enemy." Limit, explicit: "This does not mean that you always attack first."

14. **Suppress the intention, not the act.** *Fire, "To Hold Down a Pillow."* Reasoning: check his attack at the syllable "at…", his jump at "ju…", his cut at "cu…". The discriminating rule is what makes this more than turtling: "suppress the enemy's useful actions but allow his useless actions." Limit, stated: doing only this is defensive; you must also act according to your own Way and command him.

15. **Occupy his position mentally; it is worse in there than it looks.** *Fire, "To Become the Enemy."* Reasoning: people think of a robber cornered in a house as a fortified enemy, but from inside, the whole world is against him and there is no escape. "He who is shut inside is a pheasant. He who enters to arrest is a hawk." Corollary he draws for himself: if you think "here is a master who knows the principles," you have already lost.

16. **Break rhythm, then pursue to the end.** *Fire, "To Know 'Collapse'", "To Crush", "To Penetrate the Depths".* Reasoning: houses, bodies and enemies all collapse when their rhythm is deranged; if you do not exploit the collapse he recovers and will not be negligent again; if you crush lightly he recovers; and a man beaten on the surface may be undefeated in spirit, so you must destroy the depth as well. Only condition: "Once we have crushed the enemy in the depths, there is no need to remain spirited."

17. **Never a third attempt with a failed method.** *Fire, "The Mountain-Sea Change" and "To Renew."* Reasoning: if an attack failed once there is little chance the same approach works again; twice may be unavoidable, three times is not. "If the enemy thinks of the mountains, attack like the sea; and if he thinks of the sea, attack like the mountains." The related principle, "To Renew," is for deadlock: change your own spirit without changing your circumstances and win in the new rhythm.

18. **Speed is not the Way; rhythm is — and the Void is on the far side of competence, not instead of it.** *Wind, "Speed in Other Schools"; Void.* Reasoning: "Speed is not part of the true Way of strategy," because fast and slow are only functions of being in or out of rhythm, and "the master of strategy does not appear fast." The Void scroll then closes the whole book by refusing the mystical read: people think what they cannot understand must be the void, and "This is not the true void. It is bewilderment." Condition, explicit and severe: the void arrives only after you have studied other arts fully, not deviated, and accumulated practice day by day and hour by hour.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

Musashi addresses this **directly**, more than any other text on the foundation list except Aeneas Tacticus, and for the plain reason that he was the small actor. He had no lord for most of his life, no land, no income, no school, no teacher and no name until he made one. What follows is what the text says outright; where I infer, I say so.

**Direct: talent is not the entry requirement.** "Even if a man has no natural ability he can be a warrior by sticking assiduously to both divisions of the Way" (Earth). This is the first substantive sentence of the Earth scroll after the survey of the Ways, and it is a deliberate door held open.

**Direct: the short weapon is not the losing weapon, and the small force is not the losing force.** The Wind scroll attacks schools that prefer extra-long swords: their preference "shows the inferior strategy of a weak spirit," they think to win from a distance by reach, and at close quarters the length becomes an encumbrance. He then states the scaled version as a rhetorical question and answers it: "Cannot few men give battle against many?" — "There are many instances of few men overcoming many." He immediately guards it: he does not dislike long swords, he dislikes the *inclination* to them, i.e. dependence on a material advantage.

**Direct: strength as a strategy is self-defeating.** *Wind, "The Strong Long Sword Spirit."* If you rely on strength you cut coarsely and hit too hard, and your own sword is carried past the target. "The strongest hand wins," he says, "has no meaning." Scaled: if you have a strong army and the enemy also has a strong army, the battle is fierce and nothing is decided; "Without the correct principle the fight cannot be won."

**Direct: the training does not require resources you do not have.** *Fire, opening.* "Of course you cannot assemble a thousand or ten thousand men for everyday training. But you can become a master of strategy by training alone with a sword." This is the single most important sentence in the book for a man with nothing, and it is stated as an explicit answer to an explicit resource objection.

**Direct: the benchmark is yourself.** *Water, closing.* "Today is victory over yourself of yesterday; tomorrow is your victory over lesser men." Note the order: self first, lesser men second, skilled men only after further training. He is describing a ladder for someone starting at the bottom of it.

**Direct: fighting outnumbered has a method, and the method is aggression.** *Water, "There are Many Enemies."* Draw both swords, take a wide left-and-right stance, read the attacking order and meet the first attacker first, cut alternately left and right, drive them together "as if tying a line of fishes," and then cut them down without room to move. "Waiting is bad." Related, from Fire, "To Mingle": strike one strong point, break off, strike another on the periphery, "like a winding mountain path," and do not withdraw one step. Whether these actually work against real numbers is not something the text proves; it asserts them.

**Direct: the credentialed are selling something.** *Earth, "The Way of Strategy."* The schools are "concerned with colouring and showing off their technique, trying to hasten the bloom of the flower… They are looking for profit." The Wind scroll's attack on "interior" and "surface" teachings, secret transmissions, written pledges and grade licences is the same argument at the institutional level: Musashi says he refuses to pass on his Way "through written pledges and regulations." For an outsider with no access to credentials, this is the useful half of the book's polemic — it is an argument that the credential is not the skill.

**Inferred (mine, not his).** Three things follow from the text but are not stated:
- The carpenter section implies that a man of poor ability is still *used* — for joists, wedges, scaffolding — and Musashi treats that as correct allocation, not as failure. Read from below rather than from the foreman's chair, it says: there is work at your grade, take it, and the grading is about the job, not about you.
- The Wind scroll's method — study the rival schools in detail in order to know your own — is the small actor's cheapest available advantage. Musashi devotes a fifth of the book to it. "It is difficult to know yourself if you do not know others."
- His hostility to fixed guards and formations is more available to the poor than to the rich, because a fixed position is something you can only afford to hold if you have something to hold.

**What he does not offer the small actor.** No counsel to avoid a fight you cannot win; no doctrine of delay; no use of allies, patrons or intermediaries; no notion of accumulating before engaging. He has no theory of survival, only of victory. That absence is real and is dealt with in §5 and §8.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Total and lifelong, and defined as repetition rather than planning. Practice "day by day, and hour by hour"; treat training as a part of normal life with the spirit unchanging; "Step by step walk the thousand-mile road." The Japanese text closes the Water scroll with the line Harris does not render: 「千日の稽古を鍛とし、万日の稽古を錬とす」 — a thousand days of practice is *forging*, ten thousand days is *tempering*. Preparation in Musashi is a quantity of days, not a stock of materiel.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Reconnaissance is personal and immediate, not organisational. "To Know the Times" (Fire) asks whether the enemy's disposition is flourishing or waning and requires you to recognise his school, his quality and his strong and weak points *before* you forestall him. "To Move the Shade" is deliberate probing: when you cannot read him, indicate a strong attack to make him reveal his resources; in single combat, feint so he shows his sword. There is no spy, no messenger, no network — the intelligence apparatus is the practitioner's own eyes.

**Timing.** The book's central category; see principles 6, 13 and 18. Distinctive contribution: the layering of *distance* timing, *interval* timing and *background* timing, and the insistence that victory comes from a timing the enemy does not expect rather than from being faster.

**Terrain and position.** Substantial and concrete. "Depending on the Place" (Fire): sun behind you, entrance behind or right, rear clear, left free, stand higher, drive him left into obstacles and bad footholds, and — the sharpest line — do not let him see his own position. "Crossing at a Ford" (Fire) is the positional principle stated as navigation: know the route, the soundness of your ship and the favour of the day; sail when the conditions are met even though your friends stay in harbour; if the wind fails within a few miles, row the rest. He says explicitly that this "occurs often in a man's lifetime" and applies to everyday life.

**Deception and information.** Constant and unembarrassed, though small-grained. Feints ("To Move the Shade," "To Cause Loss of Balance" — start slow, then attack suddenly). Contagion ("To Pass On": sleepiness, yawning and calm are catching; show complete calmness to an agitated enemy, then attack when it has taken; "getting someone drunk" is similar). Fear ("To Frighten": shout, make a small force seem large, threaten the flank without warning). Concealment of one's own state: "Do not let the enemy see your spirit." What he does not have is strategic deception — no false campaigns, no disinformation, no planted agents.

**Alliances.** Silent. There is no ally in this book. Musashi has enemies, students and a lord in the abstract; he has no coalition, no confederate and no third party. The word for the gap is not oversight: he was a *rōnin*.

**Logistics and economy.** Almost silent, and what there is is ambivalent. He names the merchant's Way as living by taking profit and treats it neutrally as one of the four estates; he includes "Distinguish between gain and loss in worldly matters" among the nine rules; he notes that timing governs "the rise and fall of capital." Against that, his whole polemic is against men who make accomplishments "their means of livelihood… in order to sell them." There is no supply, no money, no provisioning, no sustainment of a force over time anywhere in the text.

**Morale and discipline.** Present, and located in the individual rather than the unit. The foreman "should know their morale and spirit, and encourage them when necessary," and should ask nothing unreasonable. The Three Shouts (Fire) are an explicit morale instrument: loud at the start, low-pitched during, and after the contest a shout "in the wake of our victory." "The Commander Knows the Troops" inverts it: think of the enemy as your own troops and you can move him at will. But there is no discipline in the Roman or Vegetian sense — no punishment, no drill of formations, no oath.

**The decisive point.** Explicit and doubled. "To Injure the Corners" (Fire): strong things cannot be moved by pushing directly, so strike the corners of the enemy's force; if the corners fall the spirit of the whole body falls. "Crossing at a Ford": cross at the advantageous place, which means attacking the weak point and putting yourself in an advantageous position — and he names this as how you win at large scale. "To Penetrate the Depths" adds that the decisive point may be psychological rather than positional.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Silent in the Clausewitzian sense, and its opposite is doctrine. There is no point at which Musashi advises stopping. "To Know Collapse," "To Crush" and "To Penetrate the Depths" all say the same thing from different angles: pursue, do not let him recover, crush all at once, destroy the spirit in its depths. The only stopping rule in the book is the negative one in principle 17 — stop repeating a *method* that has failed twice — which is about technique, not about ending a war.

**When not to fight.** Silent, and the silence is the single largest gap between Musashi and Sun Tzu. There is no "supreme excellence in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting" here, no calculation of whether the fight is worth its cost, no advice to decline. He assumes you are in the fight.

**Defence versus offence.** A settled and one-sided doctrine. "Attitude is the spirit of awaiting an attack," and he dislikes it; in duelling "you must always be intent upon taking the lead and attacking"; aiming for an opponent's unguarded moment is "completely defensive, and undesirable"; holding down the pillow alone "is defensive" and must be converted into command. Defence is conceded only for the static case — garrisoned castles and battle array. His image for the difference: bearing an attack well is like making a wall of spears and halberds; attacking means pulling the stakes out of the wall and using them.

**The character required of the commander.** Composite and drawn from the carpenter. He must know natural rules, the rules of the country and the rules of houses; know the theory of what he builds; grade his material honestly; allot work by ability; circulate among his men "asking nothing unreasonable"; know their morale and encourage them. Personally: settled spirit, unreadable, neither elevated nor low, free of likes and dislikes in weapons, able to "look at things from a high point of view," and above all willing to be wrong about his own past victories (principle 2). He closes the Earth scroll by extending this to government: the superior man will "manage many subordinates dextrously, bear himself correctly, govern the country and foster the people."

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The Wall Street misuse, and the scholar who documented it as it happened.** Harris's 1974 Overlook translation sold poorly outside the martial-arts world. In 1981–82, as Japanese industrial competition frightened American executives, the publishers re-jacketed it with the banner "Japan's Answer to the Harvard MBA: The Classic Guide to Strategy Corporate America is taking into the Boardrooms," and the back cover asserted that the Japanese entrepreneur "studies, works, and lives according to" the book. Bantam produced a rival version subtitled *The Real Art of Japanese Management*, with a cover pairing a woodblock samurai against an American executive in a raincoat holding a *Wall Street Journal*. *Time* ran a long piece (19 October 1981); the Book of the Month Club and Fortune Book Club carried it.

G. Cameron Hurst III demolished the premise in 1982 (reprinted in *InYo: Journal of Alternative Martial Arts*, January 2001) and his objections are the durable critique:
- The claim that Japanese businessmen live by it is false. Hurst's answer to whether the Japanese treat it as a corporate bible is "an unambiguous no." In the late 1960s he found it hard to buy in Japanese bookshops, usually available only as an excerpt inside anthologies of Japanese thought, mostly in unmodernised Tokugawa language. The first popular modern-Japanese paperback appeared in **1980** — i.e. American hype increased its Japanese readership, not the reverse.
- It was never canonical. "When it came to military strategy everybody read Sun Tzu, not Musashi." It was transmitted as a secret text inside one school, written for a named student, and no major figure of the period lists it as required reading.
- It was an anachronism when written — a real swordsman's book in the longest peace in Japanese history, when the samurai was "more administrator and scholar than warrior, and more likely to read Mencius than Musashi."
- The content is not esoteric. Hurst: "Musashi is hardly original or especially profound, certainly not very mystical, and not especially foreign to Americans," and the advice is "all too familiar from British and homegrown sources: Samuel Smiles, Benjamin Franklin, McGuffy's Reader, and the Boy Scout Manual."
- Musashi is a bad model for the thing he was sold as explaining. He is silent on loyalty, harmony, group cohesion and mutual support — the traits associated with Japanese firms. He was a masterless, teacherless, family-less individualist. Hurst's closing turn is the sharpest: "Perhaps *Five Rings* tells us more about its American than its Japanese reader."

**The Bantam list.** The Bantam team pulled Musashi's nine Earth-scroll rules to the front under the heading "The Winning Strategy." Their rendering of rule five is "Be discreet regarding one's commercial dealings." The Japanese is 「第五に物毎の損徳を弁ふる事」 — discern the gain and loss in all things — and Harris gives "Distinguish between gain and loss in worldly matters." The business framing was manufactured in the translation, not found in the text.

**The Yoshikawa problem.** Most of what circulates as Musashi's biography is Yoshikawa Eiji's novel, serialised in the *Asahi* 1934–39, which covers only his first thirty years and adds a moral arc that no Edo-period play contained — the Zen priest Takuan curing his temper, the renunciation of Otsū, the conquest of self. Hurst notes there is no record that Musashi ever knew Takuan or Yagyū Munenori, both of whom Yoshikawa brings on stage. The self-help literature routinely treats the novel's Musashi as the historical one.

**The source-chain problem.** The biographical tradition rests on late compilations: the *Tanji Hōkin Hikki* (c. 1727, about eighty years after his death), the *Bukōden* (1755, ~110 years), and the *Niten-ki* (1776, ~130 years), which is the source that most shaped the popular image and in turn fed Yoshikawa. The high-reliability items are few: the Kokura monument (小倉碑文) erected in 1654 by his adopted son Miyamoto Iori, who as a senior Kokura retainer had a great deal to lose by inscribing falsehoods in public, and the Numata family record, an administrative document with no motive to glorify him, which dates the Kojirō match to the thirteenth day of the fourth month, 1612. The famous "Musashi arrived late to unsettle Kojirō" detail appears only in the *Niten-ki*, 130 years on, and in none of the earlier sources. Kojirō's age is disputed. (This source-grading comes from miyamoto-musashi.org, a site whose translations and commentary are signed only "SATORATOS" — I treat it as unverified secondary commentary, but its dates for the *Niten-ki*, *Bukōden* and Kokura monument are consistent with Hurst and Wikipedia.)

**Translation problems in the version most people read.** Two I confirmed myself by setting Harris against the Japanese:
- *The death sentence.* Harris: "Generally speaking, the Way of the warrior is resolute acceptance of death," followed by a very long footnote quoting the *Hagakure*. The Japanese reads 「大形武士の思ふ心をはかるに、武士は只『死ぬる』と云ふ道を嗜む事と覚ゆる程の儀也」 — as far as one can gauge what warriors think, it amounts to about this: that the warrior merely cultivates the way of dying. Musashi is *reporting a common opinion*, and the next sentence deflates it: 「死する道に於ては武士ばかりに限らず…其差別なきものなり」 — the way of dying is not limited to warriors; priests, women and peasants alike know duty and shame and resolve on death, and in this there is **no** difference. Harris renders that clause "this is a different thing," which reverses it. What Musashi says distinguishes the warrior is in the following sentence and is not death at all: it is excelling, winning, and thereby raising one's name. Harris's rendering plus his *Hagakure* footnote assimilate Musashi to a death-cult bushidō that the passage is explicitly pushing away from. A very large amount of popular "Musashi philosophy" descends from this sentence.
- *The omitted line.* The Japanese Water scroll ends 「千日の稽古を鍛とし、万日の稽古を錬とす。能々吟味有べきもの也」 before its colophon. Harris's Water Book ends at the preceding sentence and does not render it. The line is the most-quoted sentence in the whole book in English ("a thousand days of practice to forge, ten thousand to temper") — quoted, that is, from translations other than the one most people own. I note the fact of the divergence; I cannot say whether the cause is Harris's copy line or Harris.
- *A name that is a province.* The preface says Musashi at sixteen beat 「但馬国秋山といふ強力の兵法者」 — a powerful strategist named Akiyama, of **Tajima province**. Harris gives "an able strategist, Tadashima Akiyama," reading the province name 但馬国 (Tajima) as part of a surname. Small, but it indicates the level of control in the 1974 version.
- More generally: Bennett's 2018 Tuttle edition is advertised as the first English version built on a reconciled reconstruction of the variant copies, correcting over 150 characters, which is a direct statement that all earlier English versions — Harris, Cleary, Wilson, Kaufman, Tokitsu — rest on uncorrected copies. Chris Hellman, a practitioner who has compared the versions at ichijoji.blogspot.com, calls Bennett "authoritative" and warns against the reviews, but did not publish the detailed comparison he promised.

**The bushidō misframe.** Treating *Go Rin no Sho* as a document of "the samurai code" is an anachronism twice over: the codifying literature (*Hagakure*, Daidōji Yūzan's *Budōshoshinshū*) is early-eighteenth-century, written under a peace by administrators asking what a warrior class is for; and the packaging of that literature as a unified national ethic called *bushidō* is late Meiji. Karl Friday, "Bushidō or Bull?" (*The History Teacher* 27.3, 1994): the term "was not used to designate a code of warrior behavior until the early modern era" and was rare even then; Nitobe was "able to believe that he had invented it himself." Friday's second point is the more damaging one for anyone who wants to read Musashi as chivalry: reading the medieval military record, "one is struck far more often by the efforts of samurai to use deception and subterfuge to catch an opponent off guard" than by self-sacrifice. See also G. Cameron Hurst III, "Death, Honor, and Loyalty: The Bushidō Ideal," *Philosophy East and West* 40:4 (October 1990), and Oleg Benesch, *Inventing the Way of the Samurai* (Oxford, 2014). Nitobe's own book confirms the framing from the inside: he wrote it in English, in Pennsylvania, prompted by a Belgian jurist's astonished question about moral education without religion and by his American wife's questions; he calls his subject "Chivalry," glosses the word by reference to *noblesse oblige*, illustrates throughout by European parallel, and concedes that what he is describing is "not a written code."

**Popular attributions that fail.** Two that circulate widely and should never be quoted as Musashi:
- "You must understand that there is more than one path to the top of the mountain." Not in Harris, and reported by the Martial History Team (December 2020) as absent from Harris, Cleary, Wilson, Tokitsu/Kohn and Bennett; it traces to Stephen F. Kaufman's 1994 *The Martial Artist's Book of Five Rings*, an avowed *interpretation*, later marketed by Tuttle as *Musashi's Book of Five Rings*. It also contradicts the Dokkōdō's last precept.
- "Bushidō" as Musashi's own frame. The word does not govern the text; 兵法 (*heihō*) does.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Musashi is the most directly applicable text on the foundation list to a man with no money, no network, no position, no credential and no reputation, and also the one most likely to wreck him, for the same reason: it is the manual of a man who had exactly nothing and got out by winning sixty-odd fights in public. That is a real strategy and it is available. It is also survivorship bias with a colophon. Sixty men lost those fights and did not write books.

**What transfers.**

*Solo practice is legitimate practice, and the resource objection is answered.* "You cannot assemble a thousand or ten thousand men for everyday training. But you can become a master of strategy by training alone with a sword." A man with nothing has one asset — unsupervised hours — and Musashi's claim is that the hours are sufficient, provided the count is very large. The Japanese puts the count in numbers: a thousand days to forge, ten thousand to temper. Ten thousand days is twenty-seven years. That is the actual bargain on offer, and the reason it transfers is that it requires no capital, no permission and no institution. It transfers cleanly to any skill whose practice can be done alone and whose results are externally checkable — code, writing, an instrument, a trade, sales calls, a craft. It transfers badly to anything whose output cannot be tested without other people, because his whole feedback mechanism was contact with live opponents.

*The audit at thirty.* This is the most useful and least-quoted thing in the book. He won sixty-odd fights and then judged that the wins told him nothing about whether he had a method — natural ability, luck, or weak opposition would produce the same record. A man with nothing who gets an early result will be told that the result validates him. Musashi's position is that a run of wins is compatible with having no method at all, and the only way to know is to go back to study for twenty years. Apply it: your first success is evidence about the market, not about you.

*The credential is not the skill, and the schools are selling.* Musashi's polemic against *dōjō* licences, secret transmissions and written pledges is the argument a man outside the credentialing system needs and cannot easily get elsewhere: the grade certifies attendance and payment, not capacity. This transfers because the structure is identical — bootcamps, certifications, memberships, mastermind groups, the whole business of selling access to a Way. His test is the useful part: what can you actually do, against whom, watched. Do not read the polemic as permission to skip learning. He studied constantly and fought constantly; he objected to paying for the badge, not to the work.

*Fitness of instrument, and no favourites.* "You should not have a favourite weapon." A man with nothing tends to pick one tool — one platform, one tactic, one pitch — and defend it, because it is the only thing he has and it has become part of his identity. Musashi treats attachment to an instrument as a defect of the same order as ignorance of it, and prices every weapon by range and terrain. The version that transfers: know what each of your methods can and cannot reach, and drop the one that is out of its envelope even if it is the one you are known for.

*Take the ground before the fight.* "Depending on the Place" is about sun, doorways and footing, and it generalises exactly. Choose where the meeting happens, arrive early, know the exits, put the awkwardness on the other side. His hardest line — do not let him see his own position — is a real tactic and one of the places where the text turns nasty; see below.

*The initiative, with his own caveat intact.* Three ways to hold it, and none of them is always attacking first. He says so: "This does not mean that you always attack first; but if the enemy attacks first you can lead him around." The transferable content is that passivity is a decision with a cost, not a neutral state, and that the man with nothing usually loses by waiting for a situation to clarify.

*Two failures and stop.* "If you once make an attack and fail, there is little chance of success if you use the same approach again… do not try it a third time." The commonest failure mode of a man with no resources is repeating a cheap method past the point where the evidence is in, because repeating is free and changing is not. Musashi puts a hard number on it: two.

*Occupying the other side's position.* "He who is shut inside is a pheasant. He who enters to arrest is a hawk." The person you are approaching from below — the employer, the buyer, the gatekeeper — looks fortified from outside and feels besieged from inside. Musashi's rule is to do the mental transfer deliberately and to distrust the reading that the other man is a master who knows what he is doing.

*Talent is not the entry requirement, and reliance on strength is a defect.* Both of these are stated for the weak party's benefit and both survive the transfer. "The strongest hand wins" has no meaning; the party with more resources, relying on resources, fights fiercely and settles nothing. That is an accurate description of what it feels like to compete against a funded incumbent, and the operative conclusion — that the advantage has to come from principle rather than from mass — is correct.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

*The absence of any doctrine of when not to fight.* This is the largest gap. Sun Tzu calculates before engaging and prefers to win without fighting; Musashi has no such chapter and no such sentence. In a world of duels that is coherent, because the duel is the only mechanism by which an unknown man converts skill into reputation, and declining is the same as not existing. A man in the modern world is not in that world. He faces a very large number of avoidable conflicts — arguments, lawsuits, rivalries, contracts, jobs taken to prove a point — and almost all of the ones he can avoid he should. Transplanting Musashi's silence produces a man who takes every fight offered. Take the silence as a fact about seventeenth-century Japan, not as advice.

*The pursuit doctrine.* "To Know Collapse," "To Crush" and "To Penetrate the Depths" all instruct you to destroy a beaten opponent so thoroughly that he cannot recover, in his spirit as well as his position. In a duel to the death this is correct and there is no cost to it, because the loser will not be in the room again. In every modern context there are repeat interactions, reputations, references and third-party observers, and the man who finishes people has a short career. This is the single most dangerous passage in the book for a man with no position, because he has no reserves of goodwill to burn and will burn them fastest. Discard it.

*The deception repertoire, mostly.* "To Frighten," "To Cause Loss of Balance," "To Pass On" and "getting someone drunk" are techniques for manipulating a person's state in a contest they know they are in. Outside a declared contest they are simply manipulation, and used on people who are not your opponents — colleagues, clients, anyone you need twice — they destroy the thing you are trying to build. The exception that does transfer is the defensive half: "Do not let the enemy see your spirit," i.e. do not broadcast your state to a counterparty during a negotiation.

*"Do not let him see his own position."* Keep this only as a description of what is being done to you. As a practice it is deceiving someone about their own situation, which is fraud in most of the contexts where a man without a position operates.

*The two-sword doctrine as literal advice.* It is a real technical argument about grip and terrain and it does not carry a generalisable content. The commonly extracted moral ("be versatile," "keep a hand free") is imported, not found. Musashi's own framing is narrower than the moral: the two-sword style is *for* crowds and for taking prisoners.

*The whole of the large-scale claim, taken on trust.* Musashi asserts constantly that one man beating ten is the same principle as a thousand beating ten thousand, and he never demonstrates it. He never commanded a unit. His only campaign service was at Sekigahara and the Osaka sieges as a combatant in the ranks, and at Shimabara in a staff or non-combatant role in his mid-fifties. His claim that the duelling principles scale to armies is a hypothesis from a man with no command experience, and when the modern self-help literature transposes it a second time — from armies to companies — it is a hypothesis about a hypothesis. Clausewitz, Machiavelli and Vegetius earn the organisational register; Musashi does not.

*The biography as a life plan.* Seven of his sixty years were spent employed. He did not marry, had no children of his own, adopted two sons, kept no household, reportedly did not bathe for fear of being caught unarmed, and by Harris's account beat a defeated man on the ground and cut down a boy who had not reached his teens. Hurst's assessment stands: the very estrangement that makes him attractive makes him a poor model. What the man with nothing should take is his method of practice, not his method of living.

**The honest summary.** Musashi answers one question precisely, and it is the right question for a man with nothing: *what do you do when you have only time and a body?* His answer — a decade of unsupervised repetition, public tests against people better than you, no attachment to a single instrument, take the ground early, hold the initiative, and stop repeating anything that has failed twice — is sound and is not sentimental. He answers almost nothing else. He has no allies, no money, no supply, no exit, no reason to stop, and no way to decline a fight. Use him for the practice and the early tactics. Go elsewhere — to Sun Tzu for whether to fight at all, to Clausewitz for the cost of continuing, to the Arthashastra for money and people — for the rest.
