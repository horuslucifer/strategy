# 6. Emperor Maurice (attrib.) — Strategikon

## 1. Identity

The *Strategikon* is a Greek handbook of land warfare written in the East Roman empire at the end of the sixth century or the very beginning of the seventh. Twelve books. Its subject is a cavalry-centred field army: how to recruit, arm, organise, drill, march, camp, ambush, besiege and fight it, and — in Book XI — how to fight four specific enemies who each require a different method. Book VIII is a collection of short maxims. The Greek is deliberately plain and heavily Latinised, because Latin was still the command language of the army.

**What survives.** Very few manuscripts. The principal one does not name Maurice: it attributes the work to Urbicius, a fifth-century tactician, possibly a copying error from *Maurikios*; other witnesses give Maurice (McCotter). The critical edition is *Das Strategikon des Maurikios*, ed. G. T. Dennis, German trans. E. Gamillscheg, CFHB 17 (Vienna 1981). Scholars cite by book, chapter and line of that edition (e.g. *Strat.* 12.B.16.30–38), which is the form used below wherever a source supplied it.

**The translation problem, stated plainly.** George T. Dennis, *Maurice's Strategikon: Handbook of Byzantine Military Strategy* (Philadelphia 1984) is the only complete English translation, and it is in copyright. It is **not** the only complete modern-language translation: Romanian (Mihăescu 1970), Russian, German (Gamillscheg, facing the 1981 Greek) and Spanish versions exist, with a Modern Greek one in preparation (Różycki 2017; Gyftopoulou 2013). Philip Rance's two-volume *The Roman Art of War in Late Antiquity* — a new translation with commentary — has been announced as forthcoming since at least 2004 (Rance, *GRBS* 44 [2004] 265 n.3: "forthcoming 2005"); Gyftopoulou cited it as London 2006; Routledge listed it under ISBN 9781138084506. As of this writing the Routledge page returns 404 and the book appears never to have been published; Conor Whately, reviewing in 2021, was still "eagerly awaiting" it. In July 2025 Penn Press reissued Dennis with a new foreword by Whately (ISBN 9781512828375).

**Which translation I used: none directly.** Dennis 1984 is not public domain and the only complete text online is an unauthorised upload. **I built this file from the primary text as quoted, with loci, inside open-access scholarship I actually opened**: Rance's *GRBS* article (his own translations, with CFHB line numbers); Łukasz Różycki's two open-access studies (Dennis's English and his own, with line numbers); Sophia Gyftopoulou's *Byzantina Symmeikta* article (Greek, German and Dennis's English side by side); Stephen McCotter's De Re Militari paper (a long Dennis block from Book XI); and Charles C. Petersen's 1992 *Military Review* article (some two dozen Dennis quotations, each footnoted to a Dennis page). **This is the thinnest evidentiary base of any text in this canon and the reader should discount accordingly.** I have not read the *Strategikon* end to end in any language; my sense of its proportions comes from the tables of contents plus the parts scholars chose to quote. Where I reason rather than report, I say so.

**Correcting the blog.** Saucedo's entries 12–13 read, in full: *"Emperor Maurice reigned for two decades as a Roman Emperor (582 – 602). In 602, Maurice's reign was cut short, overthrown, and killed. One of Maurice's most significant accomplishments was the Byzantine Army's restructuring and its reforms written in the Strategikon, one of the most important medieval period publications."* — *"The purpose of the Strategikon was to serve as a manual to train the mounted troops of the Byzantine army. Military analyst and government consultant Charles C. Peterson writes the Strategikon applies in today's military organizations and its philosophies to Sun Tzu's Art of War. The Strategikon consists of 12 chapters of instructions on how commanders can train and maneuver their Soldiers on the offensive with excellent efficacy."*

1. **The blog says the book is Maurice's; the evidence says the author is unknown.** The principal manuscript names Urbicius. Wiita's 1977 Minnesota dissertation proposed Maurice's brother-in-law, the general Philippicus, who survived Phocas's purge in a monastery and had the time. Różycki (2017, 114–15) concludes flatly that the author is still unknown and the work is at least partly a compilation; scholars routinely write "Pseudo-Maurice." This matters: if it is not Maurice's, it is not one emperor's reform programme but a snapshot of what a competent staff officer already knew — a stronger claim for its reliability, a weaker one for its originality.

2. **The blog says Maurice's reforms were "written in" the Strategikon; the relationship runs sideways.** Oman asserted in 1885 that the reorganisation of the eastern army "was mainly due to him," and the claim has been repeated since. Modern work qualifies it: Rance reads the treatise as largely descriptive of existing late-Roman practice; Różycki shows Book XII.B is probably lifted from an earlier infantry manual of Justinian's reign (the equipment named — Herulian *spatha*, Gothic shoes — is anachronistic by 590), while the cavalry material is demonstrably post-581 and heavily Avar-influenced. The book records, borrows and prescribes practice in proportions still argued about.

3. **"One of the most important medieval period publications" is wrong three ways.** It is a late-antique manuscript, not a publication; it was written c. 590–600 (Whately) or c. 592 (Dennis); and it was not important in the medieval West, surviving in a handful of copies, unprinted until Scheffer's Greek edition of 1664 and untranslated into English until 1984. Its real afterlife is internal to Byzantium — Leo VI's *Taktika* (c. 900) recycles it heavily. Gyftopoulou (2013, 71–74) adds that even the title is a modern artefact: the original was probably headed *Taktika*.

4. **The blog says the twelve books instruct commanders "on the offensive"; the text's posture is the opposite, and this is the entry's largest error.** Its governing analogy is hunting, not assault: game is taken by scouting, snares, ambush and stalking, "not brute force" (VII.A pr. 45–53). It states that defeating the enemy face to face is risky even when you win, and that a wise commander will not fight a pitched battle "unless a truly exceptional opportunity presents itself."

5. **Small errors.** They are "books," not chapters, each subdivided into chapters; the original work ended at Book XI, with Book XII an appendix (McCotter). "Peterson" is Charles C. **Petersen**, and the substance cited is from his "The Strategikon: A Forgotten Military Classic," *Military Review* 72.8 (Aug 1992) — the blog cites a later reprint (*Hampton Roads Military History* 2, Winter 2008) without naming the original.

## 2. The world the text assumes

A permanent state with a treasury, a standing professional army, an archive, and a chain of command that appoints officers centrally. The author takes for granted that his reader is a salaried officer who will be given troops he did not raise and did not pay for, and who can be removed by the emperor for losing them — the text notes that a commander could lose his position over a single lost pitched battle (VIII.B.45). Nothing in the book is addressed to a man acting on his own account.

Strategically it assumes a state on the defensive on several fronts and short of money. Recruitment of foreign *foederati* had collapsed with the revenues (Oman). The empire cannot replace an army quickly, therefore it must not lose one. That single fiscal fact generates most of the doctrine: battle-avoidance, ambush, small-unit raiding, second lines, and a preference for buying, splitting and subverting enemies rather than destroying them.

Technologically: cavalry with lance and composite bow, mail to the ankle, horse armour of iron or felt, stirrups (the *Strategikon* is the earliest clear European evidence for them — see §6), uneven infantry, no gunpowder, no usable maps, communication at the speed of a horse. The empire's kit is openly derivative — Avar horse-armour, neck-guards and lance-heads, Herulian swords, Gothic shoes, Slavic javelins (Różycki 2017, citing 1.2; 12.B.1; 12.B.4; 12.B.5; 12.5). The army improves by stealing from whoever beats it.

Psychologically it assumes soldiers are frightened, that fear is manageable rather than eliminable, and that morale is manufactured — by speeches given unit by unit at convenient times (VII.A.4.2), by law, by demotion within a man's own unit (I.8), and by making the enemy more frightened than you are. It also assumes your own side leaks: deserters, defectors and spies are permanent conditions to be used, not lamented.

Culturally it assumes peoples have stable characters that can be operationalised, while writing inside an ethnographic tradition of *topoi* going back to Herodotus that it sometimes reproduces and sometimes cuts against (Różycki 2017, 117–19). And it assumes the empire is eternal and its officers are not.

## 3. Core doctrine

Loci are given in the form the source used. Where the only available locus is a Dennis page number (because Petersen cited it that way), I give the page and flag any book attribution as inference.

1. **The handbook exists because ordinary men must be made competent by procedure, not selected for genius.** *Praefatio* (quoted in Różycki 2017, 109 n.2, Dennis's translation). The author says the armed forces have been neglected so long that men taking command "do not understand even the most obvious matters," with soldiers blamed for poor training and generals for inexperience. He writes "succinctly and simply," drawing partly on ancient authors and partly on his own limited active service, "with an eye more to practical utility than to fine words." *Limit:* he calls it a modest elementary handbook for men taking up generalship, not a science.

2. **Make war the way you hunt, and do not fight a pitched battle without an exceptional opportunity.** VII.A pr. 45–53; the maxim at Dennis 90. Reasoning is arithmetic, not aesthetic: beating an enemy face to face in the open is expensive even when it works, and a victory bought that way brings "glory [that] rings hollow." *Limits he attaches:* the rule holds "regardless if the enemies are many or few" — it is not a counsel for the weak only; and advantages must still be pressed once gained (principle 11).

3. **Method beats mass.** Dennis 64 (Book VII by inference). "A ship cannot cross the sea without a helmsman, nor can one defeat an enemy without tactics and strategy." With them one can overcome a force not merely equal but greatly superior in number; wars are not decided by courage and numbers, as the inexperienced believe, and time spent mobilising large numbers is largely wasted. *Limit:* he pairs the claim with "and the aid of God," and method is bought with training, not free.

4. **Hold a reserve, at two scales.** Dennis 23–24 (Book II) and Dennis 15, 26, 76. Committing everything in one line "is the mark of an inexperienced and absolutely reckless man": a single line outflanked or surprised has no support and must retire in headlong flight. About a third is kept in a second line, whose two functions are stated separately — if the first line is pushed back the second is "a support and a place of refuge" where troops can be rallied and turned around; and when pursuing, the second line lets you pursue safely because it can hold if the enemy turns. The same logic is built inside each division: one third are *cursores*, assault troops in open order on the flanks who run out at a retreating enemy; two thirds are *defensores* in close order who follow "not charging out or breaking ranks" and catch the assault troops if they fall back. *Reasoning (mine, following Petersen):* this makes the defensive base as mobile as the striking arm, so a pursuit cannot separate them — the classic way a winning force gets annihilated.

5. **Attack flanks and rear; frontal combat is never safe.** Dennis 27. Well-timed attacks on flanks and rear are "much more effective and decisive than direct frontal charges"; even against a numerically superior enemy, direct operations go against rear or flanks. *Limit he states himself:* purely frontal combat is "dangerous and uncertain under all conditions and against any people." He exempts no case.

6. **Ambush is the highest-leverage operation available.** Book IV entire; Dennis 52–55. Ambushes "have in a short time destroyed great powers before they had a chance to bring their whole battle line into action." The reciprocal is a duty: patrol far and frequently for the enemy's ambushes, and never pursue in a disordered way. *Limit:* his named example, the "Scythian ambush" (a feigned rout onto a hidden force), was already ancient and he knows it. Ambush works on the unwary.

7. **Be unreadable. Vary unit sizes; never repeat a method because it worked.** Dennis 16–17 and 80. "All of the tagmas should definitely not be of the same size. If they are, the enemy can easily estimate the size of the army by counting standards." And separately: a general must not always use the same methods "even though they seem to be working out successfully," because the enemy becomes used to them, adapts, "and inflict[s] disaster upon us." *Limit:* variation is bounded by command and control — a *moira* may not exceed 3,000, a *meros* not "more than six or seven thousand," or units "may prove to be disorderly and confused" (Dennis 16).

8. **Information discipline: drill in public, plan in private, and turn your own leaks into weapons.** Dennis 40, 63, 80, 81, 82, 88–89. The army must never draw up in its full combat formation while merely drilling, because the real dispositions "ought not be made known ahead of time during drill"; a whole chapter gives several mock drills plus one for actual use, so observers cannot tell which is real. The general "should never take the rank and file of his own troops into his confidence," and major operations are known "to just a few and [only] those very close to you." Then the active half: spread rumours that you plan one thing and do another; tell suspected deserters the opposite of your intention so they carry it; write letters to men who have already defected, arranged to fall into enemy hands, reminding them of a prearranged treachery, so the enemy destroys its own new assets. *Limit:* each instrument presumes you know who your leaks are.

9. **Decide slowly; once decided, move without hesitation.** Dennis 79–80. "It is essential to be cautious and take your time" in planning, then carry it out at once: "Timidity after all is not caution, but the invention of wickedness." His reason elsewhere: "in war opportunity is fleeting, and cannot be put off" (Dennis 85).

10. **Assume nothing; budget for what is not probable.** Dennis 86, 87, 88, 91. "A general should not have to say: 'I did not expect it.'" "A general who takes nothing for granted is secure in war." "The sharp general takes into account not only probable dangers, but also those which may be totally unexpected."

11. **Finish — and leave the other side a way out.** Dennis 79–80; IX.2. Being satisfied with merely driving the enemy back is "a mistake made by inexperienced leaders," who comfort themselves with the saying "Be victorious but do not press your victory too hard"; by not seizing the moment they make more trouble for themselves and leave the result in doubt. *Limit, and it is a real tension in the text:* in the night-attack chapter he deploys against a camp from three sides and deliberately leaves a line of retreat, because complete encirclement makes a trapped enemy close ranks and fight (Różycki 2023, 64–65). The book wants total exploitation of a broken enemy and no encirclement of an unbroken one.

12. **Study this enemy and aim at his specific weakness — there is a procedure.** Dennis 65. The text runs an explicit menu: superior in cavalry, destroy his forage; superior in numbers, cut his supplies; an army of diverse peoples, corrupt them with gifts, favours, promises; dissension among them, deal with their leaders; relies on the spear, lead him into difficult terrain; relies on the bow, close to hand-to-hand; reckless, undisciplined and not inured to hardship, feign attack and drag things out until his ardour cools, then strike; superior in infantry, entice him into the open and hit him with javelins from a safe distance. *Limit:* every entry presumes reconnaissance good enough to know which case you are in.

13. **Ethnography is operational intelligence, political intelligence is part of it, and you copy from whoever beats you.** Book XI in four sections — Persians (11.1), Scythians i.e. Avars and Turks (11.2), fair-haired peoples i.e. Franks and Lombards (11.3), Slavs and Antes (11.4) — each following one structure: political description, everyday habits useful in war, methods and favoured stratagems, equipment, recommended Roman response (Różycki 2017, 119). The political half does real work: the Avar khaganate has no unity, so encourage defection (11.2.74–78); the Slavs have many kings always at odds, so win the nearer ones with persuasion or gifts and then attack the rest, so their common hostility never unites them under one ruler (11.4). The empire meanwhile re-equips itself from its enemies, and uses close-quarters skill learned from Franks and Lombards against Persians and Scythians, and archery learned from Persians and Scythians against Franks and Lombards (Petersen's reading). *Limits:* the descriptions are partly literary convention — the Avar portrait follows the standard nomad *topos*, while the Slav portrait does not and may derive from diplomatic reports in the imperial archive (Różycki 2017, 115–19); and Petersen notes the copying worked partly because the empire's central position kept its enemies from learning from each other at the same rate.

14. **Protect the train; keep it out of sight of the battle.** Book V; V.4. Baggage carries what makes a force able to operate at all, including servants and families. It is kept 30–50 miles from the battle in defensible ground with water and grass, garrisoned by two *banda*, with a relay of known men carrying messages and an intermediate camp between. In transit it marches separately; with enemies near, it moves to the centre of the column. *Reasoning:* capture of the train in view of the army breaks morale, and reserve horses at the front only add confusion.

15. **Morale is a manufactured commodity, produced by law and by speech.** I.6–I.8; VII.A.4.2. Military crimes and penalties are read to the troops and separately to the commanders; punishment includes reduction to lower rank inside a man's own unit. Positively, the troops are assembled by *meros* or *moira* at a convenient time and given suitable speeches to encourage them — a chapter of eight lines that Gyftopoulou links to the separate late-antique genre of *rhetorica militaris*.

16. **Night operations have three phases, and the first is the longest.** IX.2. Phase one dulls the enemy's vigilance: open peace negotiations that appear to be going well; march out in formation every morning and turn back as if afraid, for days, until the enemy stops reacting; send deserters to report your collapsing morale, then strike camp and withdraw a short distance so the report is confirmed. Phase two is the approach — in marching column, not battle order, because battle order makes too much noise; commands by whistle or by striking a shield; a scout who knows the ground leading; moonlight preferred so men do not collide. Phase three is the clash, just before dawn when alertness is lowest, from three directions, with trumpets to sound more numerous than you are, and reserves held for the case where the panic does not take. (Różycki 2023, 62–65, citing IX.2.1–7, 11–17, 18–23, 26–30, 33–51, 48–51.)

17. **Infantry hold by closing up, not by manoeuvring.** 12.B.16.30–38. When enemy cavalry come within bowshot and try to break the line, the first three men of each file form a *phoulkon* — "one shield upon another" — thrust their spears forward past the shields and fix them in the ground so that anyone who closes is impaled, and lean their shoulders into the shields to bear the pressure from outside; the third and fourth men, standing more upright, stab or throw and then draw swords; light infantry and cavalry to the rear shoot over (Rance's translation).

18. **Train on bad ground, or plan your own defeat.** Dennis 78. Horses must be accustomed not only to rapid manoeuvre in open, level country but to hilly, thick and rough ground and quick ascents and descents; once used to all of it, "neither men nor horses will be surprised or troubled by any sort of land." Then the sentence with teeth in it: "The men who spare their horses and neglect drills of this sort are really planning their own defeat."

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

The *Strategikon* addresses the outnumbered directly and the resourceless not at all. Those are two different things, and the difference is the whole of this section.

**Directly addressed: how to fight someone stronger.** Almost the entire doctrine is a system for the side that cannot win a symmetrical contest. The claim is explicit — with tactics and generalship it is possible to overcome "not only an enemy force of equal strength but even one greatly superior in numbers," and it is a mistake to spend time mobilising large numbers instead (Dennis 64). The mechanisms are all asymmetric: ambush, night attack, feigned flight, flank and rear, the enemy's forage, the enemy's supply, the enemy's internal dissension, the enemy's characteristic habit. The advice on the Franks is the cleanest instance — brave, undisciplined, bribable, so drag things out until their ardour cools, ambush them, and pay them.

**Directly addressed: how to survive being poorer.** The hunting analogy is at bottom a budget argument. Battle is the most expensive way to get an outcome; scouting, waiting and stalking are cheaper; a costly victory "brings only empty glory" and is ridiculous to seek. Luttwak, reading the whole corpus, states the resulting norm as: attack mostly with small units, prefer patrolling, raiding and skirmishing, and replace the battle of attrition with the non-battle of manoeuvre — keep close to a superior invading army, stay just out of its reach, and pounce on outnumbered detachments, baggage and looting parties.

**Not addressed: the man with no position.** Every instrument in the book is institutional. The reader has troops, standards, a treasury to draw bribes from, an archive of diplomatic reports, a chain of command that appoints his officers for him, a train with four days' provisions, and the authority to demote a man inside his own unit. He is short of margin, not short of everything. The book never speaks to someone with no force to economise, no reserve to hold back a third of, no reputation to conceal his plans from, and no subordinates to lie to. **This is my inference rather than the author's statement**, but a safe one: the preface names its reader as men who assume command of troops.

**One place where the text does look at the man with nothing, and it is unflattering.** Book XI notes that Slav hospitality and way of life may look attractive to Roman deserters seeking a better life, and that this is a real problem; the prescribed response is brutal punishment for those who defect and rewards for those who escape back (11.4.131–36). The manual's view of the man who has nothing and walks away is that he is a security problem to be deterred.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Central and continuous. The premise of the book is that commanders arrive ignorant and must be trained by text; the premise of the drill chapters is that horses and men must be exercised on ground worse than they expect to fight on, and that neglecting it is "planning their own defeat" (Dennis 78). Luttwak's formulation — train intensively and keep everything ready, but do not be eager to fight, because the highest purpose of maximum readiness is to raise the probability of not fighting at all — is a synthesis, not a quotation, but it fits.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Strong and unusually layered: patrols far and frequent in all directions round the battlefield (Dennis 64); scouts who personally know the ground leading night approaches (IX.2.33–36); political intelligence on enemy factions (11.2.74–78; 11.4); and an ethnographic file on four peoples that may itself derive from diplomatic reporting. The maxim under all of it is "A general who takes nothing for granted is secure in war" (Dennis 87).

**Timing.** Explicit. "In war opportunity is fleeting, and cannot be put off" (Dennis 85); plan slowly, execute immediately, and timidity is not caution (Dennis 79–80). "Watch for the right opportunities and pretexts" and "strike at the enemy before they can get themselves ready" (Dennis 93). The night attack is timed to just before dawn, when alertness is lowest.

**Terrain and position.** Present but subordinate to tempo and formation. Terrain enters chiefly as something to train on (Dennis 78) and something to lead the enemy into — spear-armed enemies into difficult ground, bow-armed enemies into the open and then into contact (Dennis 65). Book XI goes further and reads settlement as terrain: Slav villages sited by water and woods with multiple escape routes and strung along a river, so that an army halting at the first gives the rest time to evacuate — answered by splitting your force and entering the settled belt from both ends (11.4.165–72, 180–91).

**Deception and information.** The strongest theme after battle-avoidance: mock drills (Dennis 40, 63); rumours of one plan while doing another (80); suspected deserters told the opposite (82); planted letters to defectors (81); feigned fear repeated daily until it reads as routine (IX.2.11–14); trumpets to inflate apparent numbers (IX.2.48–51). And the wrestling image — feint one way, then use what opens (Dennis 89).

**Alliances.** Present, mostly as subversion rather than coalition: corrupt a diverse army with gifts, favours and promises; deal with their leaders where there is dissension (Dennis 65); win the nearer Slav kings so the rest never unite (11.4); encourage Avar contingents to change sides (11.2.74–78). Luttwak generalises this into a claim that subversion is the cheapest path to victory and an enemy's own allies are the most useful recruits — again his synthesis of the wider corpus, not a line of this text.

**Logistics and economy.** Book V is entirely the baggage train: distance, defensible ground with water and grass, garrison, message relays, intermediate camp, four days of supply, separate march order except when enemies are near. Offensively the economic instrument is blunt: ravage the enemy's lands to cause famine (VIII.B.4); destroy the forage of a cavalry-superior enemy and cut the supplies of a numerically superior one (Dennis 65).

**Morale and discipline.** Manufactured by two mechanisms held apart. Law: crimes and punishments read to troops and to commanders separately, with demotion inside one's own unit (I.6–I.8). Speech: assemble by *meros* or *moira* at a convenient time and encourage them (VII.A.4.2). Negatively: keep the baggage — which holds servants and families — out of sight, so its capture cannot dishearten the army.

**The decisive point.** Not a concept the text uses as Clausewitz would. Its nearest equivalent is the enemy's flank and rear, named as categorically more effective than the front (Dennis 27), and the specific structural weakness identified by the Book VII procedure (Dennis 65). Decisiveness sits in the enemy's seam, not in a geometric point.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Genuinely divided, and instructively so. On exploitation, maximal: do not be satisfied with driving the enemy back, do not slacken after a short distance, "there can be no rest until the enemy is completely destroyed," and "in war, as in hunting, a near miss is still a complete miss" (Dennis 79–80). On encirclement, restrained: strike a camp from three sides and leave a way out, because a fully surrounded enemy closes ranks and fights (IX.2). The reconciling principle is not stated; my reading is that he wants the enemy to break and then be pursued, and understands that surrounding him prevents the break.

**When not to fight.** The most developed answer in the book: not without an exceptional opportunity (Dennis 90); do not emulate men "admired for their brilliant success" who operate recklessly (87); hunt rather than assault (VII.A pr.). And the institutional enforcement — a commander could lose his position for one lost pitched battle (VIII.B.45) — which makes the doctrine a career incentive as well as a theory.

**Defence versus offence.** The book refuses the dichotomy structurally. Assault troops and defenders inside every formation, a second line behind every first: the offensive is safe only when it carries its own defensive at the same speed.

**The character required of the commander.** Patient in planning and instant in execution; unsurprised; unwilling to be admired; secretive to the point of lying to his own soldiers; opportunistic without limit (Różycki: deceiving his own men, starving provinces and torturing prisoners are all in scope); and above all not in love with battle. The negative portrait is sharper than the positive: the man who says "I did not expect it," the man who forms one line, the man who repeats a winning trick, the man who spares his horses.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**Gibbon's dismissal and the over-correction it provoked.** Gibbon wrote that the vices of Byzantine armies "were inherent, their victories accidental" — quoted verbatim by Charles Oman in 1885 (*The Art of War in the Middle Ages*, citing "Gibbon, v. p. 382"; verified by grepping the Project Gutenberg text, ebook 44308, line 1053), who immediately reversed it: "it would be far more correct to call their defeats accidental, their successes, normal." Petersen opened his 1992 article with the same line and the same rebuttal. *I searched Gibbon's chapter 46 at ccel.org for the wording and did not find it, so I cannot confirm Oman's volume-and-page citation against the primary text.* The rebuttal has its own failure mode: Oman went on to claim the *Strategikon* shows the reorganisation of the eastern army "was mainly due to him [Maurice]," which modern scholarship does not support. Gibbon's contempt and Oman's rehabilitation are both stronger than the evidence.

**The stirrup.** The *Strategikon* is the earliest clear European evidence for the stirrup, and Lynn White, Jr., *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962), built on that a thesis linking stirrup, shock cavalry and feudalism — the "Great Stirrup Controversy." The reverse move was made too: because the technology looked anachronistic, historians from the late nineteenth century argued the whole treatise must be eighth- or ninth-century (White lists some of the philological arguments at p. 144). Both lean a very large structure on a very small text. Current consensus dates the work to c. 590–600 (Whately) or between 582 and 602 (Różycki), on internal evidence: no Arabs anywhere in it, and the Antes described as a live people, which they ceased to be around 602.

**Luttwak's "operational code," and Kaldellis's demolition.** Edward Luttwak, *The Grand Strategy of the Byzantine Empire* (2009), gives the *Strategikon* a chapter and extracts a seven-point code: avoid war but stay ready; gather intelligence continuously; campaign with small units and avoid battle; replace attrition with manoeuvre; recruit allies, preferably the enemy's; subvert, because it is cheap; and when you must fight, fight around the enemy's strengths. Luttwak says himself that the code is "nowhere stated in any Byzantine source as comprehensively as in what follows" and is imputed from behaviour and scattered recommendations. Anthony Kaldellis, in *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* 2010.01.49, was severe: Luttwak "is no Byzantinist," relies entirely on translations including online ones and on outdated scholarship; his claim that the post-Attila army was primarily cavalry rests largely on Procopius' preface taken at face value and is contradicted by manuals Luttwak himself quotes; he calls the *Strategikon* a cavalry manual when the text refers to a separate work on infantry (Kaldellis cites 2.2); and he dates the *Strategikon* to "the late part of the reign of Justinian" on p. 237 and to Maurice's reign on p. 267. **Practical consequence:** Luttwak's seven points are a good aide-memoire and a bad citation. They are his synthesis. Do not quote them as the *Strategikon*.

**Rance on Dennis.** The only complete English translation has known defects, identified by the scholar who has worked longest on the Greek. In *GRBS* 44 (2004) Rance records that Dennis's translation "is confused" at p. 146 on the mechanics of the shield-wall (12.B.16.23–24), renders the rearguards incorrectly at 12.B.16.27–29, mistranslates a verb at p. 134, and that Dennis's printed Greek at 12.B.24.12–14 is distorted by incorrect punctuation which Gamillscheg's facing German and Dennis's English both reproduce (p. 163). None of this is scandalous — it is normal philology on a hard text — but a reader working from Dennis alone is working from a good translation with identified soft spots, and the passages most likely to matter to a lay reader (formations, drill mechanics) are among them.

**Positivism about Book XI.** The ethnographic book has been mined for a century as a documentary source on the early Slavs and Avars, especially in Balkan and Central European historiography; Zástěrová (1971), Wiita (1977) and Labuda (1954) are the founding studies and, Różycki notes, still the first reference point despite needing replacement. Kaldellis's objection, reported by Różycki (2017, 113, citing Kaldellis 2013, 82), is that the *Strategikon* is treated "in a much too positivistic manner" — as a bag of facts rather than a text with a genre, a rhetoric and inherited *topoi*. Różycki's corrective is worth copying as method: the Avar portrait is schematic and conforms to the nomad stereotype running back to Herodotus; the Slav portrait does not conform, is longer than military need requires, and may therefore derive from diplomatic reports — and when Leo VI paraphrased the passage three centuries later he put nearly all of it into the past tense except the hospitality, which is itself a clue that the original observation was real.

**Presentism in the military-professional reading.** Petersen's 1992 article is the best short introduction in English and the clearest instance of the risk. It reads the *meros* as "a combined arms formation — as versatile, in terms of the combat requirements of its day, as Napoleon's corps d'armée," finds its nearest counterpart in the Mongol *tumen*, and closes by comparing it to Guderian's panzer division. Each comparison illuminates something; together they invite the reader to believe a sixth-century cavalry division was solving a twentieth-century problem. The same article quietly converts a manual about not fighting into a manual about mobile offensive power, which is very likely where the blog's "on the offensive" came from.

**Popular misattribution.** "The nation which forgets its defenders will itself be forgotten" is sometimes attached to Maurice. It is Calvin Coolidge's, from his acceptance speech for the Republican vice-presidential nomination on 27 July 1920; it attached itself to Maurice because Stephen McCotter used it as the title of a paper about Maurice and the Persians. It is not in the *Strategikon* and no scholar claims it is.

**The self-help literature.** Honest answer: there barely is one. Unlike Sun Tzu, Machiavelli and Musashi, the *Strategikon* has almost no popular management afterlife — no English text until 1984, then out of print, with a readership that has been professional-military and academic. I looked for a business or leadership literature misusing it and found none worth naming. **The absence is itself informative:** the text has no popular afterlife because it offers no slogans, only procedures, and procedures do not travel.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

**What transfers.**

**1. Competence is installed by procedure, not issued at birth.** The preface says the army has decayed until men taking command "do not understand even the most obvious matters," and the remedy offered is a plain written handbook, brief, simple, useful rather than fine. That is the whole theory of the text: you cannot rely on getting a genius, so you write down what a mediocre man must do and you drill it. The man with nothing should assume he is the mediocre commander in that sentence, and convert every skill he needs into a written repeatable procedure rather than an intention. It costs an afternoon. He has no other asset that cheap.

**2. Never let one engagement be able to end you.** This is the load-bearing transfer. The manual's constant is that a general engagement is a bet with an unbounded downside and a bounded upside, taken only on exceptional terms, and that a victory bought frontally "brings only empty glory." A man with nothing has no reserve, so his rule is stricter than the emperor's: no single interview, lawsuit, loan, confrontation, business bet, relationship or move should be structured so that losing it removes his ability to try again. Most people with nothing lose not because they chose badly once but because they arranged their life so that one bad choice was terminal.

**3. Hold back a third, for rallying rather than for winning.** The second line exists so a broken first line has "a support and a place of refuge" where troops can be turned around. Not a war chest; a rally point. Concretely: a third of the money unavailable to the current plan, a third of the week unscheduled, a relationship or skill or income the current venture cannot consume. Keep the reasoning precise — its job is not to make the win bigger, it is to make the loss survivable and reversible.

**4. Put the reserve inside the venture, not beside it.** The *cursores*/*defensores* split solves a specific problem: a force that wins and then pursues gets separated from its own base and destroyed. The same move in a life is entering the new job, city or venture before the old one is fully abandoned — the notice period, the sublet, the retained client, the unburned bridge. The book's point is not caution; it is that the pursuit can be pressed harder precisely because the base moves at the same speed.

**5. Stop competing frontally.** "Purely frontal combat" is called dangerous and uncertain "under all conditions and against any people." The frontal engagements in a modern life are the advertised job with four hundred applicants, the saturated market, the credential race entered ten years late, the argument won by status. A man with nothing loses all of them by definition, because frontal contests are decided by the resources he does not have. The flank is the unadvertised role, the adjacent market, the person who needs what he can already do, the problem nobody has bothered to name. This is not cleverness; it is the only geometry available to him.

**6. Run the Book VII procedure on a real opponent, in writing.** Superior in cavalry, destroy his forage; superior in numbers, cut his supplies; composed of diverse peoples, corrupt them with gifts and promises; dissension among them, deal with their leaders; relies on the spear, take him into rough ground; relies on the bow, close with him. The form is what transfers: *name the specific advantage, then name the specific thing that negates it.* Most people with nothing never do this and instead compare themselves with an abstraction. Twenty minutes usually reveals that the competitor's advantage is structural and narrow — funded, credentialed, incumbent — and that each of those has a documented failure mode.

**7. Be unreadable, and retire a winning move before it is read.** The tagmas are deliberately unequal so the enemy cannot count standards; the general must not reuse a method merely because it worked, since the enemy adapts and "inflict[s] disaster upon us." This cuts against the standard advice to systematise everything that works. Systematise the procedure; vary the approach. The visible pattern is what other people price you on.

**8. Drill in public, plan in private, and assume you leak.** Never draw up in the real formation while drilling; never take the rank and file into your confidence; keep major operations to a few close people; assume some of the people around you carry information outward, and give them what you would like carried. In modern terms: what you do visibly should be work, not strategy; the number of people who know the actual plan should be countable; and the people most likely to repeat what you say should get the version you do not mind repeated. This is unpleasant, and it is what the text says.

**9. Decide slowly, then move the same day.** "It is essential to be cautious and take your time" deciding; then execute "right away without any hesitation or timidity. Timidity after all is not caution." Most men with nothing have the two backwards — impulsive about commitments, slow about execution. Reversing them costs nothing.

**10. Finish, and leave the other party a way out.** "A near miss is still a complete miss" applies to the applications not followed up, the invoices not chased, the drafts at ninety percent. The second half is equally operational: in the night attack you strike from three sides and leave a line of retreat, because a fully surrounded enemy stops fleeing and starts fighting. Applied to negotiation, to leaving an employer, to ending anything — take the result, do not take the person's dignity as well. The cost of a cornered opponent is unbounded and you have no reserve to pay it with.

**11. Train on ground worse than you expect.** Horses accustomed to hills, thickets and rough descents are not troubled by any sort of land; men who spare their horses "are really planning their own defeat." The practical form is unpopular: practise under worse conditions than the real event — the pitch when tired, the conversation when it is hostile, the work when the tools are bad. This is the one place where the manual moralises, and it earns it.

**12. Take your equipment from whoever is beating you.** The empire's cavalry wore Avar armour, carried Avar lance-heads and used Avar neck-guards, and the manual says so without embarrassment. A man entering with nothing has no reputation to protect and should copy openly and immediately from whoever is currently better than him, including people he dislikes. This is not humility. It is arithmetic.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

**1. Nearly every instrument requires an institution.** The reserve presumes a force; the bribes presume a treasury; the ethnographic file presumes an archive; the discipline presumes the power to demote; the intelligence presumes salaried scouts. The man with nothing has no *tagma*. What survives the subtraction is a disposition — avoid the decisive engagement, hold back a third, strike the flank, be unreadable — not the machinery. Any chapter that borrows the machinery's vocabulary without its substance is writing horoscope prose.

**2. The state is permanent and the commander expendable; for a man alone the reverse holds.** Luttwak's closing note on the Byzantine code is exact: as soon as one enemy is gone another takes his place, "for all is constantly changing as rulers and nations rise and fall. Only the empire is eternal." That is a sound basis for trading time and territory. It is not a sound basis for a finite life with a culminating point the empire did not have. Byzantine patience applied to a forty-year working life is simply delay. Outlasting your opponent requires that you outlast him.

**3. Subversion is unavailable.** "Corrupt them with gifts, favors, promises" presumes you have gifts, favours and promises that are credible. A man with nothing cannot buy allies; he can only be useful to them, which is a different instrument with different rules — it requires him to be reliable and legible, in direct tension with transfer 8 above. The book does not help with that tension, because its author never faced it.

**4. The discipline technology is coercive and has no private analogue.** Morale here runs on military law read aloud, demotion within the unit, and brutal punishment of desertion. Translating that into "discipline yourself" is a category error, and it is the specific error this canon should avoid: the manual describes an apparatus, not willpower. A man alone must substitute structure — commitments to other people, deadlines with external consequences, money already spent — because the apparatus is the active ingredient, not the exhortation.

**5. The ethnographic method is a live hazard.** Book XI works because it concerns enemies whose behaviour was constrained by real shared material conditions, and it is still partly wrong: the Avar portrait is inherited stereotype, and Leo VI recycled the Slav passage three centuries later in the past tense, as though the observations had ossified. Applied to people in a modern life it produces confident, cheap, expensive errors. Take from Book XI the *procedure* — political structure, habits, methods, equipment, then your response — and apply it to institutions, firms and markets, where it is testable. Not to categories of persons.

**6. Battle-avoidance has a floor, and the book knows it.** The empire avoided battle and still had to hold a frontier; it fought constantly. A commander who never fights is not following the *Strategikon*, he is failing to hold his sector, and the treatise's own institutional answer is that he loses his command (VIII.B.45). A man with nothing has to take engagements that produce money and standing, because avoidance produces neither. The manual can tell him how to choose the engagement, how to enter it with something held back, and how to leave it. It cannot tell him how to skip it forever, and anyone who reads it that way has read Petersen's summary rather than the text.

**7. The last thing, which is not motivational.** The prudence in this book did not save the man whose name is on it. In 602 the army was ordered to winter beyond the Danube, refused, and mutinied; Phocas took the throne, and Maurice and his sons were killed that November. Różycki records the order in one dry footnote: it "cost Emperor Maurice his life in 602." Whoever wrote the *Strategikon* — Maurice, Philippicus, or a staff officer whose name is gone — wrote a manual about not overreaching and then watched an empire overreach one winter and kill its author's patron for it. The lesson is not that doctrine is useless. It is that a correct doctrine is a description of what would work, held by people who do not always do it, and the gap between the two is where most lives are actually lost. Hold the doctrine anyway, expect to fail it, and build a second line for that too.
