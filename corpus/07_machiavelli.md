# 7. Niccolò Machiavelli — The Prince and The Art of War

## 1. Identity

Two books by one unemployed man, written six or seven years apart, for two different purposes.

**The Prince** (*De principatibus*, later *Il Principe*) is twenty-six short chapters, roughly 25,000 words, written in the second half of 1513 at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, the smallholding seven miles south of Florence where Machiavelli had been living since the Medici restoration cost him his office in November 1512. Its first mention is in his letter to Francesco Vettori of 10 December 1513, where he calls it "De principatibus." It was drafted for Giuliano de' Medici; after Giuliano's death in 1516 the dedication was redirected to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici, Duke of Urbino (1492–1519) — the grandson of Lorenzo il Magnifico, not il Magnifico himself. It circulated in manuscript for nineteen years and was printed only after Machiavelli's death: Rome, January 1532, by Antonio Blado, under a privilege granted by Clement VII on 23 August 1531, with a derogating indult to the Florentine Giunti of 20 December 1531. In 1559 all of Machiavelli's works went onto the Index of Prohibited Books.

**The Art of War** (*Dell'arte della guerra*) is a preface and seven books in dialogue, and it is the only major prose work Machiavelli published in his lifetime: printed by the heirs of Filippo di Giunta in Florence, dated 16 August 1521. It was written between 1519 and late 1520 — it opens with a eulogy for Cosimo Rucellai, who died in 1519, and Filippo de' Nerli had read it by 17 November 1520. It is dedicated to Lorenzo di Filippo Strozzi. The setting is the Orti Oricellari, the Rucellai gardens; the speakers are Fabrizio Colonna, who does nearly all the talking, and four Florentines who question him in rotation — Cosimo Rucellai (Book I), Luigi Alamanni, Zanobi Buondelmonti, and Battista della Palla. Fabrizio Colonna was real: a condottiere of the Roman baronial house, Grand Constable of the Kingdom of Naples, and, as the text's own framing says, returning from Lombardy "where longe time greatly to his glorie, he had served in the warres the catholike kyng" — Ferdinand of Aragon.

**Correcting the blog.**

*The blog says* "Both of his parents were of the old nobility of Florence." *The evidence says* this overstates it twice over, *because* (a) the Machiavelli were an old Florentine family that had held the priorate and the gonfalonierate since the thirteenth century, but Bernardo di Niccolò Machiavelli, Niccolò's father, was a doctor of laws among the poorest members of that family, barred from public office as an insolvent public debtor, living off a small property at Sant'Andrea and an almost clandestine legal practice; and (b) Florence had no nobility in the operative sense — the magnate class had been legally excluded from the chief offices since the Ordinances of Justice of 1293, and "nobility" there named ancestry, not standing. The sentence is very close to the one in the Marriott edition's own 1908 introduction ("Both parents were members of the old Florentine nobility"), which is the likely source of the error; it is exactly the kind of Edwardian smoothing a modern biography corrects. The correction matters to this book. A poor lawyer's son who borrowed books is a different starting position from a nobleman's, and the reading of *The Prince* as a bid for employment by a man with no cushion depends on getting it right.

*The blog says* he "first entered public office when the Medici fell from power and Florence became a Republic." *The evidence says* he entered four years later. The Medici fell in November 1494; Machiavelli was appointed head of the Second Chancery in June 1498, in the weeks after Savonarola's execution on 23 May 1498, and was made secretary to the Ten — the magistracy handling war and foreign affairs — shortly after. The Marriott introduction again makes the same compression ("The downfall of the Medici in Florence occurred in 1494, in which year Machiavelli entered the public service"), and again the blog has inherited it. The second half of the blog's sentence is correct: he lost office when the Medici returned in 1512.

*The blog says* "He wrote two books, The Art of War and the Prince." *The evidence says* this is badly wrong and distorts the whole reading. He also wrote the *Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livy* (written c. 1514–19, published 1531), which is roughly three times the length of *The Prince* and is the work on which the scholarly case for a republican Machiavelli rests; the *Florentine Histories*, commissioned by Cardinal Giulio de' Medici in 1520 and presented to him as Clement VII in 1525; *The Life of Castruccio Castracani*; the *Discursus florentinarum rerum*; the comedies *Mandragola* and *Clizia*; *Belfagor*; verse; and a large body of diplomatic dispatches and legations. Reading *The Prince* as if it were half the man's output is the single commonest route to misreading him.

*The blog says* the *Art of War* "was a favorite of many of the greatest military philosophers, such as Napoleon Bonaparte and Carl von Clausewitz." *The evidence says* half of this is defensible and half rests on a documented forgery. Clausewitz did read Machiavelli closely and wrote a long, anonymous letter to Fichte in 1809 responding to Fichte's 1807 essay on Machiavelli; the letter is printed in *Carl von Clausewitz: Historical and Political Writings* (1992), and scholarship on it holds that Machiavelli supported Clausewitz's developing view of the mutual dependence of war and politics — though the object of his admiration is at least as much the *Discourses* as the *Art of War*. The Napoleon claim, however, descends from *Le Machiavel commenté par Napoléon Buonaparte* (1816), a pamphlet published anonymously by the Abbé Aimé Guillon de Montléon which purported to be an annotated copy of the *Prince* and the *Discourses* found in Napoleon's carriage on the field of Waterloo. It was a fabrication by a political opponent, intended to convict Napoleon of Machiavellianism, and it was widely reprinted. That forgery is the origin of most "Napoleon's favourite book" claims.

*The blog says* the *Art of War* is "in fictional dialogue format between four men and a real mercenary who served the papacy, Fabrizio Colonna." *The evidence says* the structure is right — four Florentine interlocutors and Colonna — and the "real" is right, which is more than most summaries manage. The service is misdescribed: at the dramatic moment of the dialogue the text has him returning from service to the Catholic King, Ferdinand of Aragon, as Grand Constable of Naples. The Colonna served many masters, the papacy among them, so this is imprecision rather than invention.

*One thing the blog gets right and most summaries do not*: that the dedicatee of *The Prince* is a Lorenzo de' Medici. It is worth keeping the distinction that it is Lorenzo di Piero, the young Duke of Urbino, a man of no great capacity who died at twenty-six, and not his famous grandfather.

## 2. The world the text assumes

The controlling fact is the Italian Wars. In 1494 Charles VIII of France crossed the Alps and walked the length of Italy against no effective resistance — "with chalk in hand," in Alexander VI's phrase, meaning his quartermasters had only to chalk up billets. The five-power Italian equilibrium of the fifteenth century was over. For the rest of Machiavelli's life the peninsula was a theatre in which France, Spain, the Empire and the Swiss fought, and Italian states were auxiliaries, bystanders or loot. Both books are written by a citizen of a small, rich, militarily contemptible republic inside that theatre. The emotional weather of both is humiliation.

The texts assume there is no standing state in the modern sense. There is no permanent bureaucracy that survives the ruler, no national army, no public credit. *Lo stato* is closer to "the apparatus of holding" than to "the state": something possessed, and therefore losable at a stroke. A principality can be acquired in an afternoon and lost in a week. This is why the whole of *The Prince* is about acquisition and retention rather than administration, and why the analysis is so relentlessly short-horizon.

They assume armed force is bought. The condottiere system — a captain contracting his company to an employer for a season — is the normal supply of soldiers, and both books treat it as the disease. Machiavelli's charge is not that mercenaries are cowards but that their incentives are wrong in a structural way: a capable mercenary captain is a threat to his employer and an incapable one is useless, so there is no good case. He extends this to auxiliaries, meaning a stronger patron's troops lent to you, where the structure is worse: lose and you are undone, win and you belong to them.

They assume a specific military technology in mid-transition, and this is where the *Art of War* is most dated. Swiss pike squares and Spanish sword-and-buckler infantry have just displaced the armoured horseman as the decisive arm. Gunpowder artillery is present, loud, and — at Ravenna in 1512, at Novara, at Marignano — beginning to matter, while the bastioned fortification that would answer it is not yet standard. Machiavelli sees the infantry revolution clearly and the gunpowder revolution not at all.

They assume a particular psychology, stated flatly and without apology. Men are ungrateful, changeable, false, cowardly and covetous; they are yours while you succeed and not otherwise. The desire to acquire is natural and blameless; only the attempt to acquire beyond one's means is folly. Men judge by the eye rather than the hand, because everyone can see you and few can touch you. Every city divides into two humours: a people who wish only not to be oppressed, and a nobility who wish to oppress. None of this is offered as cynicism; it is offered as the operating conditions.

They assume *fortuna* is real, powerful and partly manageable. Machiavelli's settlement — half to fortune, half or a little less to us — is the frame for the whole doctrine of preparation. Fortune is a river in flood: she breaks where no dykes have been built, and the time to build dykes is in fair weather.

Finally — and this is what makes the books transferable at all — they assume an actor whose position is *new and insecure*. Machiavelli says outright that hereditary states are easy and need no book. Everything sharp in *The Prince* is written about and for a man who has just arrived somewhere he has no traditional right to be.

## 3. Core doctrine

Eighteen principles. Loci are chapter (Prince, Roman numerals as in Marriott), book (Art of War, as in Whitehorne), or book-and-chapter (Discourses, Thomson). Line numbers in the cached files are given where a locus is otherwise hard to find.

**1. Have arms of your own.** (*Prince* XII–XIII, XXIV, XXVI; *Art of War* I) The chief foundations of all states are good laws and good arms, and since you cannot have good laws where arms are bad, the arms are the prior question. Mercenaries are "disunited, ambitious, and without discipline, unfaithful, valiant before friends, cowardly before enemies," because a stipend is not a reason to die. Auxiliaries are worse because they are united and obey someone else. *Limits:* Machiavelli concedes mixed forces are better than either pure mercenary or pure auxiliary, and he grants that the Venetians and Florentines were at times lucky with hired captains — he explains the luck rather than denying it.

**2. War is never avoided, only postponed on someone else's terms.** (*Prince* III, line 766) He attacks the maxim of "the wise ones of our time," *let us enjoy the benefits of the time*: "war is not to be avoided, but is only to be put off to the advantage of others." The Romans chose to fight Philip and Antiochus in Greece so as not to fight them in Italy. *Limits:* the claim is about a conflict already latent, not about picking fights; the same chapter insists a prince must know how to distinguish troubles and take the lesser evil.

**3. Diagnose early, when the evidence is thin.** (*Prince* III) The hectic-fever analogy: at the beginning the disease is easy to cure and hard to detect; later it is easy to detect and impossible to cure. Foresight is therefore a technical skill with a cost — you must act on weak evidence and will sometimes act wrongly. *Limits:* he says this insight "is only given to a wise man," which is less a limit than an admission that he cannot teach it.

**4. Do not make another powerful.** (*Prince* III, line 886) "He who is the cause of another becoming powerful is ruined; because that predominancy has been brought about either by astuteness or else by force, and both are distrusted by him who has been raised to power." The chapter's entire case study is Louis XII, who committed five errors and then a sixth, and lost Lombardy. *Limits:* Machiavelli allows you may have no choice, as the Florentines did when the Pope and Spain moved on Lombardy.

**5. Treat men well or crush them; never in between.** (*Prince* III, line 703) "Men can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot; therefore the injury that is to be done to a man ought to be of such a kind that one does not stand in fear of revenge." *Limits:* this is stated about a conquering power settling a new acquisition, and Chapter XVII adds a strict condition on the crushing — it must be justifiable and must not touch property, or it converts into hatred.

**6. Injuries at one stroke; benefits by instalments.** (*Prince* VIII, line 1543) "Injuries ought to be done all at one time, so that, being tasted less, they offend less; benefits ought to be given little by little, so that the flavour of them may last longer." The usurper who does not do the necessary harm at once is "always compelled to keep the knife in his hand" and can never be trusted or trust. *Limits:* severities are "properly used" only if applied once, necessary to security, and not repeated; Agathocles is the demonstration and is also, Machiavelli says, excluded from glory for it.

**7. Lay the foundations before you have to.** (*Prince* VII) Those who rise by luck "fly" upward and then have nothing beneath them. The foundations others lay before becoming princes, they must lay after, "with trouble to the architect and danger to the building." Cesare Borgia is the entire chapter: praised not for succeeding but for the correctness of his method, which failed only because he was himself mortally ill at the wrong moment. *Limits:* Machiavelli names Borgia's one real error — allowing Julius II's election, on the false theory that new benefits erase old injuries.

**8. The innovator's structural disadvantage.** (*Prince* VI, lines 1099–1102) "There is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things, because the innovator has for enemies all those who have done well under the old conditions, and lukewarm defenders in those who may do well under the new." The asymmetry is causal: incumbents have the laws on their side and men do not believe in new things until long experience. *Limits:* it applies to orders, not products; and he immediately attaches principle 9.

**9. Armed prophets conquer; unarmed ones are destroyed.** (*Prince* VI, line 1117) The question to ask of any innovator is whether he can compel or only persuade: "whether, to consummate their enterprise, have they to use prayers or can they use force?" Savonarola is the counter-example — ruined the moment the multitude stopped believing, with no means to make them believe again. *Limits:* Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus are all founders with followers already; the maxim describes the transition from belief to institution, not the acquisition of belief.

**10. Opportunity and capacity are each useless alone.** (*Prince* VI) The founders "owed nothing to fortune beyond opportunity, which brought them the material to mould"; without the opportunity their powers would have been extinguished, and without the powers the opportunity would have come in vain. Israel had to be enslaved for Moses, the Persians discontented for Cyrus, the Athenians dispersed for Theseus. *Limits:* this is descriptive, not a method; he offers no way to generate opportunity, only the archer's advice in the same chapter — aim above the mark, because your arm will fall short.

**11. Study war in peace, by action and by study.** (*Prince* XIV, line 2184) "A prince ought to have no other aim or thought, nor select anything else for his study, than war and its rules and discipline." Action means hunting — hardening the body and learning how hills, valleys, rivers and marshes behave, so that knowing one country you can read another. Study means histories, and the specific practice of imitation: Alexander imitated Achilles, Caesar Alexander, Scipio Cyrus. The Philopoemen anecdote is the model — walking the country with friends, posing tactical problems aloud, so that nothing in war arrives unrehearsed. *Limits:* none stated. This is the least qualified chapter in the book.

**12. Work from the effectual truth; learn how not to be good.** (*Prince* XV, line 2279) "How one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation." Hence "it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity." *Limits:* the qualification is load-bearing and is almost always dropped in quotation — *according to necessity*, and in XVIII "not to diverge from the good if he can avoid doing so." The doctrine is conditional, not dispositional.

**13. A reputation for liberality ruins the man who has to pay for it.** (*Prince* XVI) Liberality that is recognised requires magnificence; magnificence exhausts the revenue; exhaustion forces taxation; taxation produces hatred. Better the reproach of meanness, which "brings reproach without hatred." *Limits:* explicit and important — "either you are a prince in fact, or in a way to become one. In the first case this liberality is dangerous, in the second it is very necessary to be considered liberal." And a prince spending *other people's* property should be lavish, because squandering what is not yours adds to reputation.

**14. Better feared than loved; never hated.** (*Prince* XVII, line 2442) Love is held by a bond of obligation which men break whenever it serves them; fear is held by dread of punishment, "which never fails." The operative reasoning is about control: "men loving according to their own will and fearing according to that of the prince, a wise prince should establish himself on that which is in his own control and not in that of others." *Limits:* the hard boundary is Chapter XIX — abstain from the property and the women of your subjects, because "men more quickly forget the death of their father than the loss of their patrimony," and hatred is the thing that actually kills princes. Also: the Hannibal/Scipio comparison is offered for an army, not a population.

**15. Fox and lion; and manage the appearance.** (*Prince* XVIII, lines 2536, 2619, 2622) Two ways of contesting: by law, proper to men; by force, proper to beasts. Since the first is often insufficient, the prince must use both natures, and of the beasts choose the fox and the lion, "because the lion cannot defend himself against snares and the fox cannot defend himself against wolves." Faith need not be kept "when such observance may be turned against him, and when the reasons that caused him to pledge it exist no longer." And because men judge by the eye, "in the actions of all men, and especially of princes, which it is not prudent to challenge, one judges by the result." *Limits:* the premise is reciprocal — "if men were entirely good this precept would not hold" — and the chapter says possessing the virtues and always observing them is injurious, while *appearing* to have them is useful, which is a claim about display, not about interior corruption.

**16. Never be neutral.** (*Prince* XXI, line 3184) Declare for one side. If you do not, the winner will treat you as prey and the loser will not shelter you; "he who is not your friend will demand your neutrality, whilst he who is your friend will entreat you to declare yourself with arms." The Achaean council and the Roman legate's reply is the case. *Limits:* one exception, stated flatly — do not ally with someone stronger than yourself for the purpose of attacking a third party, unless necessity compels, "because if he conquers you are at his discretion."

**17. Fortune governs half; adapt or be ruined.** (*Prince* XXV, line 3453) "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but... she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." She shows her power where no dykes have been raised. Success comes from conforming to the spirit of the times; two men acting oppositely can both succeed, and two acting alike can diverge, depending only on the fit between method and moment. *Limits:* the tragic clause. A man "is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change," because he cannot deviate from his nature and because having always prospered one way he cannot believe in another. Julius II succeeded only because he died before the times turned against his impetuosity.

**18. War may not be a trade; capability is made, not born.** (*Art of War* I, lines 2138, 2437; VII, lines 8211, 8214, 8276, 8280) This is the *Art of War*'s spine and it is a political claim before it is a military one. No good man can make war his profession, because a man who intends to live by it must be "ravenyng, deceiptfull, violente"; and no well-ordered state permits its citizens to, because such men must either keep war going or seize the state — "warre maketh Theves, and peace hangeth theim up." The alternative is a conscripted militia of one's own subjects, drilled on holidays. The enabling belief is that soldiers are manufactured: "where nature lacketh, the industry supplieth," "Nature breedeth few stronge menne, the industrie and the exercise maketh manie," "Discipline maie doe more in warre, then furie." And the resource hierarchy follows: "Men, yron, money, and bread, be the strengthe of the warre, but of these fower, the first twoo be moste necessarie: because men and yron, finde money and breade: but breade and money fynde not men and yron," with the corollary that "The unarmed riche man, is a bootie to the poore souldiour." *Limits:* Fabrizio concedes throughout that this cannot be done except by a prince who can raise fifteen or twenty thousand of his own subjects, and Book VII closes by admitting Machiavelli himself was never in a position to do it.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

*The Prince* addresses the man who starts with less than his opponents more directly than any other text in this corpus, and it does so in his own voice. This is stated, not inferred: the dedication defends the right of "a man of low and humble condition" to discuss the concerns of princes (line 527), on the ground that you must stand in the plain to see the mountains. Chapters VI through IX are a taxonomy of ways up from private station, and the examples are chosen for their lowness — Agathocles, "the son of a potter," who "became King of Syracuse not only from a private but from a low and abject position"; Hiero of Syracuse; Francesco Sforza; Oliverotto da Fermo, an orphan raised by an uncle he later murdered at dinner.

The taxonomy is four routes: by your own arms and ability (VI), by another's arms or fortune (VII), by crime (VIII), by the favour of fellow citizens (IX). Machiavelli's ranking is explicit — "he who has relied least on fortune is established the strongest."

Chapter IX contains the most useful structural claim in the book for someone without position. Every city has two humours; a man can be raised by the nobles or by the people. Raised by the nobles, "the former finds himself with many around him who consider themselves his equals, and because of this he can neither rule nor manage them to his liking." Raised by the people, "he finds himself alone, and has none around him, or few, who are not prepared to obey him." And the people are satisfiable by fair dealing, because they want only not to be oppressed, while the nobles are not, because they want to oppress. The person with no standing should therefore build on the broad constituency that wants something modest, not on the small one that wants to use him.

Chapter X is Machiavelli's explicit doctrine for the actor who cannot meet his opponent in the field. Those who "cannot show themselves against the enemy in the field" should provision and fortify the town "and not on any account to defend the country" — hold a defensible perimeter, concede the open ground, and rely on the attacker's inability to keep an army in the field for a year. The German free cities are the model: walls, ditches, artillery, a year's stores, and public works to keep the population employed and fed.

Chapter XXIV gives the negative rule: the Italian princes lost their states through sloth and then hoped the people would recall them. "That deliverance is of no avail which does not depend upon yourself; those only are reliable, certain, and durable that depend on yourself and your valour" (line 3434).

The *Discourses* is blunter than *The Prince* on the means available at the bottom. II.13 is titled "That Men rise from humble to high Fortunes rather by Fraud than by Force" and says it without hedging: "I hold it as most certain that men seldom if ever rise to great place from small beginnings without using fraud or force... Force, however, will never suffice by itself to effect this end, while fraud often will." He reads Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* as a manual of deceit and concludes that "the prince who would accomplish great things must have learned how to deceive." This is Machiavelli's own answer to the question this section asks, stated by him, and it is not a flattering one.

II.10 supplies the resource doctrine: "it is not gold, as is vulgarly supposed, that is the sinews of war, but good soldiers; or while gold by itself will not gain you good soldiers, good soldiers may readily get you gold." The Venetians "with a full treasury, lost their whole dominions without deriving the least advantage from their wealth." Money is "most necessary in a secondary place."

III.16 is the one passage in the corpus that tells a man with no credentials where to go. In quiet times republics neglect their best men and prefer "those who are recommended by wealth or connection"; in times of difficulty "true Worth is sought after," because then there is no one else. His illustration is his own city: after 1494 no Florentine succeeded in command until chance produced Antonio Giacomini, and "so long as there were dangerous wars on foot, all rivalry on the part of other citizens was suspended." When the wars looked safe and profitable, the competitors returned in force.

III.30 names the first obstacle correctly: envy, which "often disables men from acting to the best advantage, not permitting them to obtain that authority which it is essential they should have." Machiavelli's two remedies — a danger great enough that people forget their ambition, or the death of your rivals — are both outside your control, which is itself the finding.

The *Art of War* speaks to scarcity chiefly through the resource hierarchy already quoted, and through its closing lament. Fabrizio ends Book VII by saying that a man who knows how to order a state's arms but commands nothing can do nothing with the knowledge: "I lamente me of nature, the whiche either ought not to have made me a knower of this, or it ought to have given me power, to have been able to have executed it" (line 8524). Machiavelli is writing in his own situation here and barely disguising it. It is the most honest sentence either book contains about being competent and unemployed.

**What I am inferring rather than reading.** Machiavelli nowhere addresses a person with no political constituency at all — no city, no faction, no army, no office. Every "small actor" in his books is small relative to a state but is still an actor within a political field. The extension of his doctrine to an individual in a modern economy is mine, not his, and section 8 marks where it breaks.

**One further reading, and it is the most valuable thing here for a weak actor.** *Prince* XIX and *Discourses* III.6 together are the longest sustained analysis in the corpus of the weak attacking the strong directly, and Machiavelli's verdict is negative. "Many have been the conspiracies, but few have been successful," because the conspirator cannot act alone and every confederate he recruits has a better immediate payoff from betraying him: "as soon as you have opened your mind to a malcontent you have given him the material with which to content himself, for by denouncing you he can look for every advantage." Machiavelli had been arrested, imprisoned for around three weeks and given the strappado in February 1513 on suspicion of complicity in the Boscoli conspiracy — the two principals were executed; he was released under the amnesty for Leo X's election in March — and Marriott's note observes that his condemnation of conspiracies may get its edge from that. The finding is a real one: coordinated action by several weak parties against an entrenched one is structurally unstable, and knowing this is worth more to a person with nothing than any of the maxims about cruelty.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Central and unqualified. *Prince* XXV: build dykes and barriers in fair weather, because the flood comes where none were raised. *Prince* XIV: never stand idle in peace, but "increase his resources with industry in such a way that they may be available to him in adversity." *Prince* XXIV: the Italian princes fell because "in quiet times they never thought there could be a change." The whole *Art of War* is a preparation doctrine — its subject is not battle but the ordering, arming and drilling of men in peacetime so that battle is survivable.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Two kinds, treated separately. Knowledge as study: histories, the actions of illustrious men, the causes of their victories and defeats, and the practice of imitating one named predecessor (*Prince* XIV). Knowledge as terrain reconnaissance: the same chapter's hunting doctrine. Intelligence in the espionage sense is thin in *The Prince* and practical in the *Art of War*, where the emphasis is counter-intelligence: know at nightfall exactly who lodges in your camp and who has arrived, punish absentees as fugitives, and to detect a spy who has entered during the day, "cause everie man to goe to his lodgynge" (Book VII, line 8248, and Book VI on the nightly count). *Prince* XXIII adds the doctrine of counsel: question widely, but only when you choose, and let it be known that speaking freely is preferred — yet "good counsels, whencesoever they come, are born of the wisdom of the prince, and not the wisdom of the prince from good counsels" (line 3379). Advice cannot substitute for judgement; it can only be sorted by it.

**Timing.** *Art of War* VII: "To know in the warre how to understande occasion, and to take it, helpeth more then anie other thynge" (line 8208). *Prince* XXV: the fit between a man's fixed method and the changing times decides outcomes, and almost no one can change method. *Prince* III: the fever must be caught early. *Art of War* VI adds a whole seasonal doctrine — never campaign in winter, because cold and broken ground destroy the very order and discipline you have built, and the attacker suffers more than the defender.

**Terrain and position.** Thin in *The Prince* beyond Chapter X's fortification advice and XIV's terrain-reading. Detailed in the *Art of War* IV: match the width of your front to your numbers, and if you are fewer, make the formation deep and narrow rather than wide and thin; if fewer still, anchor on rivers, marshes or ditches so you cannot be enveloped; take the high ground, but not where enemy artillery commands you; keep the sun and wind at your back, and calculate how the sun will move over the course of the day; if you are weak in cavalry, fight among vines and trees. And a rule about retreat that is the position doctrine in miniature: a captain who wants to fight with assurance of not being broken should array where he has "refuge nere, and safe" — between marshes, among hills, or by a strong city.

**Deception and information.** *Prince* XVIII is the doctrine of managed appearance; *Art of War* VII gives the operational form: "No purpose is better then that, whiche is hidde from the enemie untill thou have executed it" (line 8205), and "Chaunge purpose, when thou perceivest that the enemie hath forseene it." *Art of War* VI catalogues the techniques without embarrassment — false deserters, forged signals sent for days and then withheld, raiding your own country under the enemy's colours so his men rush out to share the plunder, wine left where he will find it. Fabrizio treats deception in war as unproblematic while insisting on scrupulous justice toward civilians, and cites Caesar paying for the timber he cut in Gaul and Scipio returning a captive woman to her family as acts that won provinces more cheaply than arms.

**Alliances.** *Prince* XXI: declare, always. *Prince* XXI again: do not ally with the stronger for offensive purposes, because winning puts you at his discretion. *Prince* XIII on auxiliaries is the same warning in military form: "for losing, one is undone, and winning, one is their captive" (line 2045). The whole treatment is governed by one asymmetry — an alliance with an equal is a contract, an alliance with a superior is an acquisition of you.

**Logistics and economy.** *Prince* XVI is fiscal: revenue is the base of independence, and the prince whose economy is sound "can defend himself against all attacks, and is able to engage in enterprises without burdening his people." *Art of War* VII: "He that prepareth not necessarie victualles to live upon, is overcome without yron" (line 8241), and the four-part hierarchy of men, iron, money and bread. *Discourses* II.10 supplies the argument in full: treasure does not produce force, force produces treasure, and states that believed otherwise — Darius, Croesus, Charles of Burgundy, Venice — lost.

**Morale and discipline.** *Prince* XVII: an army in the field requires a commander willing to be thought cruel, because Hannibal held a polyglot army together in foreign country by "inhuman cruelty," and Scipio's easy nature produced mutiny in Spain. *Art of War* VI is the systematic version: death for failing the watch, for leaving your assigned place, for false claims of valour, for fighting without orders, for throwing away weapons; decimation by lot for a whole cohort; the condemned man beaten and then stoned by his own comrades, on the reasoning that "intendyng, that one be not a defendour of an evill doer, the greateste reamedie that is founde, is to make hym punisher of thesame." Matched rewards for every named act of valour, publicly conferred. No women and no games in camp, enforced by leaving no idle hours. *Art of War* IV adds the softer instruments: the captain must be an orator, because speech "taketh awaie feare, incourageth the mindes, increaseth the obstinatenes to faight, discovereth the deceiptes"; and the strongest bond of all is engineered necessity — take away every route of escape, so that the only way out is through.

**The decisive point.** *Art of War* IV: set your strong wing against his weak and your weak against his strong, hold the strong wing back, let the weak give ground and draw him in, and envelop. Scipio against Hasdrubal is the case. The general principle Machiavelli draws from it is broader and better: "the greatest remedie that is used againste a devise of the enemie, is to dooe willingly thesame, whiche he hath devised that thou shalt dooe perforce" (line 5602) — if he means to open your line with elephants, open it yourself, in order, at your advantage.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Machiavelli has no culminating-point doctrine in Clausewitz's sense, and on pursuit he says the opposite of restraint: when the field is won "the victorie ought with all celeritie to be folowed, and in this case to imitate Cesar, and not Aniball," who rested after Cannae and lost the empire of Rome (*Art of War* IV). The restraint he does teach is political and appears in the *Discourses* (II.27, that prudent states should be content with a victory) and in the one operational limit of *Art of War* VI: "regarde ought to be had not to brynge the enemie into extreme desperacion" (line 7431), because men who cannot flee become strong — Caesar opened a road for the Germans rather than pay the price of cornering them.

**When not to fight.** The fullest treatment in either book. *Art of War* IV: "a capitaine ought never to faight a battaile, except he have advauntage, or be constrained" (line 5619). Advantage comes from position, order, or better and more numerous men; necessity comes when not fighting means certain loss — money running out, famine arriving, the enemy about to be reinforced — and then you fight at a disadvantage, because attempting fortune beats watching certain ruin. And the absolute prohibition: "thou oughtest to take hede, not to conducte the armie to faight when it feareth... for that the greatest signe to lose, is thei beleve not to be able to winne." The escape routes are Fabius's — camp in strong places and offer no opportunity — or disperse into your towns and let the tedium of reducing them exhaust him. Machiavelli is honest that these have limits: an enemy determined to fight cannot be avoided except by keeping fifty miles between you.

**Defence versus offence.** *Prince* III argues for forward offence as the cheaper defence: fight Philip in Greece rather than in Italy. *Prince* X argues for pure defence when you cannot field a force. *Art of War* VI gives the defender the structural advantage in bad weather and broken country, because the attacker must either concentrate and suffer or disperse and be beaten in detail. The *Discourses* devotes II.12 to the question and reaches no single answer, which is the honest result.

**The character required of the commander.** *Art of War* VII: he must know everything the seven books contain, and that is not enough, because "no man without invencion, was ever excellent in anie science" — the capacity to devise something not in the manual is the differentiator, and Machiavelli's examples of it are small and unromantic (Alexander signalling a night march with a hat on a lance instead of a trumpet). *Art of War* IV: he must be an orator, and he must keep around him faithful, expert men with whom he continually reasons through the comparison of forces. *Prince* XIV: he must never be idle. *Prince* XXV: he must, impossibly, be able to change his nature with the times. *Discourses* III.31 gives the character in one borrowed sentence, Livy's Camillus: "My courage came not with my dictatorship nor went with my exile."

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**"The end justifies the means."** It is not in *The Prince*. I searched the Marriott text (eBook 1232), the Whitehorne *Art of War* and the Thomson *Discourses*, flattened and grepped case-insensitively for "end justifies," "ends justify the means" and every variant; nothing. What is actually in *The Prince* XVIII is a narrower and stranger claim about judgement in the absence of a tribunal: "e nelle azioni di tutti gli uomini, e massime de' Principi, dove non è giudizio a chi reclamare, si guarda al fine" — in Marriott, "in the actions of all men, and especially of princes, which it is not prudent to challenge, one judges by the result," followed by "let a prince have the credit of conquering and holding his state, the means will always be considered honest, and he will be praised by everybody." That is a sociological observation about how the vulgar judge, not a moral licence issued to the prince.

The phrase that actually approaches the proverb is in the *Discourses* I.9, and it comes with conditions that the proverb strips off. Thomson renders it: "although the act condemn the doer, the end may justify him; and when, as in the case of Romulus, the end is good, it will always excuse the means." The Italian is "Conviene bene, che, accusandolo il fatto, lo effetto lo scusi; e quando sia buono, come quello di Romolo, sempre lo scuserà" — where "lo scusi" excuses *him*, the doer, and "the means" is Thomson's interpolation. And the conditions, in the same paragraph: the case is the founding or wholesale refounding of a state, which Machiavelli says can only be done by one man; the founder must intend "to volere giovare non a sé ma al bene comune"; and he is bound not to leave the sole authority as an inheritance. Machiavelli also adds the discriminating test — "colui che è violento per guastare, non quello che è per racconciare, si debbe riprendere," blame belongs to the one who is violent to wreck, not to the one who is violent to repair. A doctrine with four conditions attached, about constitutional founding, has become a slogan about convenience.

**The manufacture of "Machiavellian."** The posthumous reception is a case study in how a reputation is made by opponents. The works went onto the Index in 1559. Innocent Gentillet, a Huguenot jurist in Geneva, published the *Discours contre Machiavel* in 1576, attacking Catherine de' Medici's policy through Machiavelli; it was the first book devoted to blackening him, and its English circulation, including Simon Patericke's 1602 translation, disseminated the ideas it was refuting. In Elizabethan and Jacobean drama "Machiavel" became the stock tag for a stage personification of evil; "Old Nick" attached itself to him. Frederick II of Prussia wrote the *Anti-Machiavel* in 1739, published September 1740 with Voltaire's revisions, setting out benevolent rational statesmanship — and invaded Silesia three months later.

**The Napoleon forgery.** Discussed in section 1: *Le Machiavel commenté par Napoléon Buonaparte* (1816) was written anonymously by the Abbé Aimé Guillon de Montléon and presented as a manuscript found in Napoleon's carriage at Waterloo. It was reprinted across Europe. Most popular claims that Napoleon carried, annotated or loved *The Prince* trace to it.

**The serious scholarly disputes about *The Prince*.** They are about what kind of book it is, and they do not resolve. Garrett Mattingly argued it was satire, incompatible with everything else Machiavelli wrote. Rousseau had already made the republican reading in the *Social Contract*, that Machiavelli was a republican whose book for princes is really a book for peoples. Isaiah Berlin's "The Originality of Machiavelli" locates the disturbance not in immoralism but in the discovery of two incompatible and equally coherent moralities, pagan-civic and Christian, with no rational way to choose. Leo Strauss called him a teacher of evil and meant it as a serious philosophical claim, not abuse. Hans Baron and Quentin Skinner's line of argument treats the *Discourses* as the real political work and *The Prince* as an occasional piece produced by unemployment. Harvey Mansfield's translation and commentary made "effectual truth" the standard English for *verità effettuale*. I have not opened these works in this session and name them as positions in the field, not as things I have verified.

**The specific failures of the *Art of War*.** These are less contested. Machiavelli was wrong about gunpowder, and the argument he gives is worth reading precisely because it is well-reasoned and wrong. In Book III Luigi puts the objection to him directly — that artillery breaks formations and pierces armour, so ancient orders and armour are pointless — and Fabrizio's answer is that the remedy is to charge the guns immediately and take them, that field artillery mostly shoots over infantry because men are low and the ground uneven, that the smoke blinds the side firing it, and that if close order were fatal the Swiss would not use it. He therefore fires his own artillery exactly once in his model battle and never mentions it again. The same position is argued at length in *Discourses* II.17. It held up badly. Marcia Colish's 1998 *Renaissance Quarterly* reconsideration surveys the charge sheet: Michael Mallett, the leading historian of Renaissance warfare, calls Fabrizio a caricature of the man he is named for; J. R. Hale, Charles Oman and Piero Pieri press the failure to acknowledge contemporary technology and practice; the call for citizen militia and Roman models is read as antiquarian rather than realistic. Colish's own resolution is that the irony is deliberate and follows fifteenth-century dialogue convention, in which a speaker is given arguments contrary to his known views — note that Cosimo Rucellai is allowed to raise the objection from contemporary practice inside the text, and that Fabrizio is himself a professional soldier of fortune arguing against professional soldiers of fortune. I also recall Felix Gilbert's essay on Machiavelli in *Makers of Modern Strategy* as a standard treatment of the same problem; I did not verify it in this session and mark it as unverified recollection.

**The practical test, which he failed.** From 1506 Machiavelli was the moving force behind the Florentine *ordinanza*, the citizen militia, and was made chancellor of the Nove della Milizia that administered it. In late August 1512 Spanish troops of the Holy League stormed Prato. The militia broke, the city was sacked, Soderini fled, the Medici returned, and Machiavelli lost his job. The *Art of War*, written nine years later, argues at length for the institution that had just failed under his own administration, and explains the failure in Book VII as a defect of execution rather than of design — "it maie be greeved with you, who have made a counterfaite thereof, and no perfecte figure." Whether that is a fair defence or special pleading is the reader's judgement. It should be in view before anyone treats the book as tested doctrine.

**The modern commercial misuse.** There is a sixty-year industry translating *The Prince* into office politics: Antony Jay's *Management and Machiavelli* (1967), which sold a quarter of a million copies and reads corporations as states; Michael Ledeen's *Machiavelli on Modern Leadership* (1999); Stanley Bing's *What Would Machiavelli Do?*, HarperBusiness 2000, whose subtitle — *The Ends Justify the Meanness* — is built on the misquotation and is at least honest about being a joke. The deeper problem with the genre is structural, not tonal. Machiavelli's advice works because the prince can kill, exile and confiscate; strip the coercion and what remains is manipulation with no enforcement behind it, which in his own scheme is the fox without the lion — the man who "cannot defend himself against wolves."

**The psychological misuse.** Richard Christie and Florence Geis, *Studies in Machiavellianism* (1970), built the Mach-IV scale, a twenty-item self-report measure of manipulative, exploitative and distrustful attitudes; it later became one leg of the Dark Triad (Paulhus and Williams, 2002). A personality trait now carries his name. It does not correspond to his doctrine. Machiavelli's prescriptions are conditional on circumstance — "according to necessity," "not to diverge from the good if he can avoid doing so" — and he explicitly says that cruelty badly used, repeated rather than concentrated, destroys the man who uses it. A stable disposition to manipulate is, in his own terms, the failure mode, not the doctrine.

**The misreading inside this project's own frame.** Reading *The Prince* as a book about how to get ahead ignores that it was written to get its author a job and did not work. He never regained office. When the Medici finally gave him something, it was a commission to write history.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Start with the structural fact, because everything else depends on it.

*The Prince* is the only book in this corpus written by a man who was, at the time of writing, broke, jobless, politically disgraced, recently tortured, and living on a small farm he could not afford to leave. That is the closest any of these authors comes to the condition this book addresses. It is also why the book is so useful and so dangerous: the advice in it is not addressed to men in his situation. It is addressed to a twenty-one-year-old Medici duke, by a man hoping to be hired. The gap between the author's position and the reader's position is the whole interpretive problem, and every self-help version of Machiavelli collapses it.

So: what does a man with no money, no network, no position, no credential and no reputation actually get?

**The resource hierarchy transfers, and it is the single most valuable thing here.** "Men, iron, money and bread are the strength of war, but of these four the first two are most necessary, because men and iron find money and bread, but bread and money do not find men and iron." Restated without the metaphor: capability generates capital, capital does not generate capability. *Discourses* II.10 makes the same argument at length with Croesus, Darius and Venice as the failures. The concrete instruction for someone starting with nothing is to acquire a capability that other people demonstrably pay for *before* raising money, taking on a partner, or buying tools. This is unglamorous and it is the opposite of what the current culture suggests, which is to raise first and become competent later. Machiavelli would say that is an auxiliary force: win and you are their captive.

**"Where nature lacketh, the industry supplieth" is the *Art of War*'s real thesis, and it is anti-talent.** Fabrizio spends Book I arguing that you should recruit from your own subjects rather than take volunteers, because volunteers are self-selected from the worst of a province, and Book II arguing that the difference between a good soldier and a bad one is the post he practises against with a weighted club, not his constitution. "Nature breedeth few stronge menne, the industrie and the exercise maketh manie." The man with nothing has no inherited advantage by definition; this is the doctrine that says the inherited advantage was never the operative variable. The honest caveat is that Machiavelli is talking about a trainable manual skill in a stable technique, and the modern equivalents are narrower than motivational readings suggest: crafts with a feedback loop tight enough to drill.

**"The unarmed rich man is booty to the poor soldier" transfers, but read it correctly.** It is not consolation. It is an observation about who holds the decisive capability in a given exchange, and it cuts both ways: the poor soldier is only dangerous because he is armed. A poor man who is *also* unarmed — no skill, no leverage, nothing anyone needs — is not in the sentence at all. The line tells you what to become, not what you already are.

**The innovator's paragraph (VI) is the most accurate thing in the corpus for anyone starting something.** Those who did well under the existing arrangement will oppose you actively; those who would do well under yours will defend you tepidly, partly from fear and partly because people do not believe in new things until long experience. The practical consequences are three, and they are unflattering. First, do not build a plan that requires early enthusiasm — it will not arrive on the schedule the plan needs. Second, resistance is not feedback about quality; *Discourses* III.30 says the same thing in a different register, that envy disables men from getting the authority they need, and that it comes from people whose position your success would alter. Third, and this is the hard one, principle 9 applies: an unarmed prophet is destroyed. Translated, that means an idea that depends entirely on other people's continuing belief, with no mechanism that operates when belief lapses, will not survive the first cold month. Build something that works when nobody is excited about it.

**Discourses III.16 is the most actionable structural claim in the whole corpus for an uncredentialed person, and almost nobody quotes it.** In quiet times, positions go to those recommended by wealth or connection; in difficult times, "true Worth is sought after," because there is nobody else to ask. Machiavelli's illustration is Antonio Giacomini, who got command in Florence only while the wars were dangerous and was crowded out the moment they looked winnable. The instruction is plain and it is the opposite of what is usually advised: go where things are going badly. A man with no credential cannot get into a stable, prestigious, well-supplied field, because in that field credentials are the sorting mechanism and he loses by definition. In a field that is failing, understaffed, unglamorous or actively on fire, the sorting mechanism breaks down and the question becomes who will actually do the work. The corollary is also his: when the trouble passes, the competitors return, and you will be crowded out unless you have converted the opening into something durable.

**Prince XXIV transfers directly.** "That deliverance is of no avail which does not depend upon yourself." The Italian princes he is describing spent years assuming that when the bad times came, the people would recall them. This is the single most common posture of a person with nothing: waiting to be found. Machiavelli's judgement is that the wait is itself the error, and that the rescue, if it comes, arrives on terms that leave you worse off than before.

**Prince XXI transfers with modification.** Never neutral: a weak party that stays out of a contest becomes "the guerdon of the conqueror," disliked by the winner for being useless and by the loser for being absent. Applied to a person with no standing inside an organisation or a field undergoing a fight, this is right and costly — both factions discount the abstainer. The modification is his own exception, and it is the more relevant half for someone weak: do not attach yourself to someone much stronger for the purpose of going after a third party, because if he wins "you are at his discretion." For a man with nothing, almost every available alliance is with someone stronger. The rule that survives is: take a side in a fight you are already inside, and refuse to be recruited into someone else's offensive.

**Prince IX transfers as a choice of constituency.** Rise by the many who want only not to be oppressed, not by the few who want to use you. The few can never be satisfied by fair dealing, because what they want is to oppress, and satisfying them requires you to injure others; the many can be, because what they want is modest. Concretely: build a base of ordinary customers, colleagues, readers or clients who want a decent thing done decently, rather than attaching yourself to a small number of powerful patrons who will want returns you cannot supply without corrupting the thing.

**Prince XXV transfers, and the sting is in the second half.** Prepare when the weather is fair. The part that is usually dropped is the tragic observation: a man who has prospered by one method cannot believe in changing it, and cannot deviate from his nature anyway, so he is ruined when the times turn. Julius II was saved only by dying before impetuosity stopped working. The uncomfortable corollary for someone starting out is that the method which produces the first result is the thing most likely to end you later, and that noticing this in yourself is very close to impossible. The only defence Machiavelli hints at is the one in *Prince* XXIII — keep a small number of people who are permitted to tell you the truth, ask them directly, and let it be known that concealment will be punished.

**The conspiracy analysis transfers as a prohibition, and it is worth more than the famous maxims.** *Prince* XIX and *Discourses* III.6: conspiracies almost always fail, because the conspirator cannot act alone and every person he recruits has an immediate, certain payoff from betrayal against a distant, uncertain payoff from success. Generalised: any plan that requires several weak parties to coordinate against a strong one, where defection pays better than cooperation, is structurally unstable and will leak. This kills a very large number of the schemes that occur to people with nothing — the group exit, the coordinated walkout, the collective approach to a common enemy. If you cannot make the payoff from staying in exceed the payoff from selling you out, do not start.

**Discourses II.13 is where the honest reading gets unpleasant, and it should not be laundered.** Machiavelli says men rise from low beginnings by fraud far more often than by force, that force alone will not do it, and that the prince who would accomplish great things must have learned how to deceive. He is describing, not endorsing, but he is not squeamish either. The accurate translation is this: a man at the bottom cannot compel anyone to do anything, so his only lever is what others perceive — what they believe he knows, can do, or is worth. Managing that perception is inescapable, and it shades continuously into misrepresentation. The honest position is not that this is clever but that it is a real cost with a real risk: the fraud that gets you in the door is a liability from the moment you are inside, and Machiavelli's own examples of rise-by-fraud — Agathocles, Oliverotto — end in exclusion from honour or strangulation. What transfers usefully is the narrow version: the beginner's leverage is informational and presentational, he should take it seriously, and he should keep the gap between presentation and substance small enough to close quickly.

**Now the things that do not transfer, and why.**

*The coercive apparatus, which is most of the book.* Every mechanism that makes *The Prince*'s advice work runs on the credible capacity to kill, exile, imprison, confiscate and field armed men. Ramiro d'Orco cut in two on the piazza at Cesena is not a management technique; it is a demonstration that the Duke could do that. "Better feared than loved" is not available to a man who cannot make anyone fear anything — it is not an instruction to him, it is an ambition, and an ambition without means is just a disposition. This is where the entire business-Machiavelli genre fails: it keeps the manipulation and drops the army, which in Machiavelli's own image is the fox without the lion, the animal that cannot defend itself against wolves.

*The zero-sum frame.* Machiavelli's world is fixed territory: what you gain, some other state loses, and the acquisitive appetite he calls natural is an appetite for other people's land. Most modern economic activity is not like that, and a man who enters it reading everything as a contest for a fixed prize will pick fights he did not need, make enemies who cost more than the ground gained, and miss every arrangement where both parties end up better. This is a real and expensive distortion, and it is the frame the book will install if you read it uncritically.

*The one-blow doctrine of eliminating opposition.* "Injuries all at one time" works in the Romagna because you can exterminate the family. The modern analogue — burning a relationship or a reputation decisively so it cannot retaliate — fails, because the injured party remains in the same market, keeps talking, and has the same lifespan you do. The part that survives is smaller and duller: deliver bad news at once and in full rather than in instalments, because a sequence of small injuries is remembered as a policy while one is remembered as an event; and spread favours over time rather than discharging them at once.

*The whole tactical apparatus of the *Art of War*.* The five ranks of pikes, the four-square formation, the camp plan, the ordinance of the battle with its extraordinary velites, the artillery doctrine. It was contestable in 1521 and it is not metaphorically available now. Reading these books for tactics is reading them for the part that has expired.

*The founder doctrine of Discourses I.9.* It will be offered to you, in some form, as licence: one man must do it alone, extraordinary action is excused by the outcome. The conditions Machiavelli attaches are the reverse of how it is used — the case is constitutional founding, the intention must be the common good and not the founder's line, and the authority must not be made hereditary. As it circulates, all three have been removed.

*The dedication strategy.* Machiavelli's own application of his book was to write it for a patron who could restore him. It failed. He got no office. Treat the *Prince* as evidence about what a brilliant analysis of power is worth to a man with no leverage: nothing, unless someone with leverage wants it that week.

**The net.** For a man entering with nothing, this pair of books yields a short and unromantic list. Build a capability before you build anything else, because capability finds money and money does not find capability. Train rather than wait to be gifted. Go where things are broken, because that is the only place an unbacked person is allowed to act, and convert the opening into something durable before the trouble passes. Expect opposition proportional to how much you would change, not to how wrong you are. Refuse plans that depend on other people's enthusiasm holding, and refuse plans that require several weak parties not to defect. Take a side in fights you are in; refuse recruitment into fights that are not yours. Depend on nothing that depends on someone else's goodwill. Keep two or three people who are allowed to contradict you, and ask them. And understand that the method that gets you your first result is the thing most likely to kill you later, which is Machiavelli's own conclusion about fortune and the reason he thought half of everything was outside our hands.

Everything else in these books — the cruelty, the fox, the fear, the injuries at one stroke — belongs to a man who already has a state. Reaching for it before you have one is the error Machiavelli diagnosed in the Italian princes: preparing yourself to be a prey to whoever should assault you, while performing the manners of power you cannot back.
