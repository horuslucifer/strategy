# 10. Frederick II of Prussia — Instructions for His Generals (*Les Principes généraux de la guerre* / *Die General-Principia vom Kriege*)

## 1. Identity

This is a working handbook written by a reigning king, in his own hand, for the fifty or so men who would command his army when he was not there. It is not a treatise for the public, not a bid for literary reputation, and not — despite the way it is now sold — a book of aphorisms. It is a private technical memorandum on how to run a Prussian campaign: how to stop the men deserting, where to put the flour, how to read ground, when to fight, and how a smaller army beats a bigger one. It was kept secret for seven years, was distributed under oath in locked cases, and reached the world only because it was captured off a prisoner.

**The manuscripts.** J. D. E. Preuss, editing volume 28 of the *Œuvres de Frédéric le Grand* (Berlin: Decker, 1856), describes three manuscripts in the royal archives: two different French redactions in the author's own hand, and an official German translation made by a secretary.

- **First redaction** (State Archives, K.365.G), dated by Preuss to 1746 or 1747, titled *Instruction pour les généraux qui auront à commander des détachements, des ailes, des secondes lignes et des armées prussiennes*. Forty-seven quarto pages on gilt-edged paper, with seven plans drawn "rapidly and without art" by Frederick himself. Undated and unsigned; it ends with the single word *Dixit*. Preuss dates it by content: the examples are from the Second Silesian War and Hohenfriedeberg, Soor and Kesselsdorf are named, so it cannot be earlier than 1746. Some articles are very short, others almost entirely crossed out. The quires are headed *Système de guerre, cahier 2, 3*.
- **Second redaction** (Royal House Archives, K.148.G), entirely in the King's hand, titled *Les Principes généraux de la guerre, appliqués à la tactique et à la discipline des troupes prussiennes*. The same work revised: thirty-one articles and ten coloured plans, thirty-nine closely written quarto pages in five quires, the first headed with the title, the other four *Réflexions et maximes militaires*. It closes *Dixit / Frédéric. (Potsdam) 2 avril 1748.* Preuss records that after several months' polishing Frederick sent it to his brother, the Prince of Prussia, on 19 June 1748, dedicated to him, and that the Prince was the only person to whom he showed it.
- **German translation** (State Archives, K.148.E), 150 folio pages, written by a secretary, heavily reworked in the King's own cabinet with the privy cabinet councillor Eichel acting as his secretary for the job.

**The secrecy, and the leak.** In his (then unpublished) Political Testament of 27 August 1752 Frederick writes, at p. 50, that he is attaching to it a treatise on the military art composed "four years ago," which he had not wanted to give to anyone "for fear of indiscretion." When war looked certain he had the German translation printed *as manuscript*: *Die General-Principia vom Kriege, applicirt auf die Tactique und auf die Disciplin derer Preussischen Truppen*, no place of printing, 1753, 214 large octavo pages, signed *Friderich*, with thirteen plates. He sent it to Field Marshal Count von Schwerin by cabinet order of 30 January 1753. Every distributed copy carried a handwritten preface dated Berlin, 23 January 1753, enjoining the deepest secrecy; the copies were issued and returned in tightly closed cases of sheet metal or leather.

On **20 February 1760**, near Cossdorf on the Elbe, Major-General von Czettritz was taken prisoner by the Austrian Feldmarschall-Leutnant Baron Beck. His copy was in his baggage. Preuss explicitly corrects his own earlier date of 21 February — which he had taken from the *Berlinische Nachrichten*, 1760, no. 26 — on the strength of Czettritz's own report to the King, dated Grossenhayn, 20 February, which says it happened that morning between six and seven o'clock. **Thomas R. Phillips's 1940 introduction gives 21 February 1760; Preuss, working from the prisoner's own dispatch, gives 20 February. Preuss is the better authority here.**

**The transmission chain, which is the single most important fact about the English text.** The captured book was German. In 1761 it was reprinted abroad, inaccurately, as *Des Königs von Preussen Majestät Unterricht von der Kriegskunst an seine Generals* (Frankfurt and Leipzig, 152 small octavo pages, thirteen copperplates). The same year it was turned into French — not back to Frederick's French, but freshly translated from the German — as *Instruction militaire du roi de Prusse pour ses généraux, traduite de l'allemand par M. Faesch, lieutenant-colonel dans les troupes saxonnes* (Frankfurt and Leipzig, 1761, 160 small octavo pages). That French was re-Germanised in 1762, and Englished in 1762 as *Military Instructions, written by the King of Prussia, for the Generals of his Army*, "Translated by an Officer" (London: Becket and De Hondt). Major Thomas Foster's translation of 1797 was also made from Faesch's French.

So the standard English Frederick runs: **Frederick's French (1748) → a German translation with additions (1753) → a pirated and inaccurate German reprint (1761) → Faesch's French (1761) → English (1762, Foster 1797)**. Four removes. Preuss further notes that the German translation silently added explanatory sentences and corrected place-names, and that all the pirated editions lack the "belle introduction" printed at pp. 1–4 of the 1753 German original.

**Three whole articles never reached the English reader.** Preuss states that Articles II (*Des projets de campagne*), XII (*Des talents qu'il faut à un général*) and XXIV (*De l'attaque et de la défense des places*) exist in the German translation manuscript but were not printed with the rest; a footnote at Article II adds *Tout cet article est omis dans la traduction de 1753*, and one at Article XII, *Cet article est omis dans la traduction*. I verified this independently rather than taking it on Preuss's word: the French has thirty-one articles; Foster's English has twenty-eight; grepping both Foster's text and the 1762 for the distinctive contents of Articles II and XII ("Norman," "distrust," "sentinel of his army," "father… executioner," "chess") returns nothing. **The English-language Frederick has therefore never contained his chapter on planning a campaign, or his chapter on the character of a general.**

**Foster's own warning.** In his Preface to the First Edition, Foster says he began because he could not obtain a copy of the 1762 translation, and then writes: "It is not pretended (for my slender acquaintance with the language forbad such exactness) that every phrase is construed in perfect conformity to the French idiom… ignorance (I hope not inattention) may have occasioned some deviation from the true meaning." The most-cited English Frederick is a dragoon major's admittedly loose rendering of a Saxon officer's re-translation of a pirated German version of a French original.

**Which texts I used.** My primary is Preuss's French, *Œuvres de Frédéric le Grand*, vol. 28 (Berlin, 1856), pp. 1–96, printed from the autograph of 2 April 1748; public domain, and the only text here that is Frederick's own words. For public-domain English I used Foster's translation (the British Library copy digitised by archive.org as `bim_eighteenth-century_military-instruction-fro_frederick-ii-king-of-pr_1797`, a "third edition," Sherborne: Cruttwell, priced 7s 6d, with Foster's dedication dated March 1797) and the anonymous 1762 first English translation. I cross-read a second Foster scan dated 1818 on its title page (`militaryinstruc00prusgoog`) and found the two agree in every passage I compared. For orientation I read Major Thomas R. Phillips's 1940 English in *Roots of Strategy* in full — but that is a translation of the **1747 first redaction** from Richard Fester's Berlin edition of 1936, a different text from the one the English tradition descends from, and it is in copyright; I quote it once, briefly, and name him.

**Corrections to the blog entry.** The blog (Yskander Arquimides Saucedo, dogmadoctrineblog.org, 10 February 2021, entries 19–20) says:

> "Frederick II the Great of Prussia (1740 – 86). Frederick became King in 1740 after succeeding Marshal de Saxon as Commander in the War of Polish Succession."

Four errors in one sentence.

1. *The blog gives (1740 – 86) in the same parenthetical form it uses for every other author's life dates — Vauban (1633–1707), Clausewitz (1780–1831). The evidence says these are reign dates, not life dates.* Frederick was born 24 January 1712 and died 17 August 1786. The reign is right — he was proclaimed king on 31 May 1740, the day his father Frederick William I died — but the format misleads, and it makes the 1747–48 author thirty-five, not seven.
2. *The blog says he became King "after succeeding" someone as a commander. The evidence says he became King by hereditary succession on his father's death*, and not as a consequence of any command.
3. *The blog says the man he succeeded was "Marshal de Saxon" in the War of the Polish Succession. Frederick never commanded in that war.* He attended the 1734 Rhine campaign as Crown Prince, as a volunteer with Prince Eugene of Savoy's Imperial relief army, and was frustrated that Eugene would not attack. The war (1733–38) was over before he reigned.
4. *And they were on opposite sides.* Maurice de Saxe — "de Saxon" is the blog's own garble, repeated from its entry 17 — was in the French besieging army at Philippsburg in 1734 under Berwick, and was made lieutenant-general for his conduct there. Frederick was with the Imperial force trying to relieve it. There is no sense in which one succeeded the other.

The blog's entry on the text — "In 1747, Frederick wrote his military treatise… The advice in the book is in context with Prussia and how to best deal with its enemies" — is true but thin, and it omits the only thing about the book's history that matters: that it was secret, oath-bound, captured in 1760, and reaches English through four translations.

**One correction to the brief that commissioned this file.** Pairing the German title *Die General-Principia vom Kriege* with the date 1747 conflates three documents: the untitled first draft of 1746/47, the French *Principes généraux* signed 2 April 1748, and the German *General-Principia* printed in 1753. "Instructions for His Generals, 1747" is Phillips's title for the first draft, not the name of the work the English tradition transmits.

## 2. The world the text assumes

Frederick assumes **an instrument he did not build**. The Prussian army of 1748 was twenty-seven years of his father's work: the drill, the cantonal recruiting system, the officer corps, the full treasury. Frederick's opening article is not about how to create such a force but about how to avoid wasting it. This is the largest single thing standing between the book and any reader who begins with nothing.

He assumes **an army half of which does not want to be there**. "Nos régiments sont composés la moitié de citoyens, et l'autre moitié de mercenaires" — half citizens, half mercenaries, and the mercenaries, bound to the state by nothing, desert at the first opportunity. Fourteen numbered rules against desertion are the first substantive content in the book, before campaign plans, before battle. Camp away from woods; patrol the flanks and rear; never march at night without necessity; place officers at the mouths of defiles; hide retreats from the men and dress them in some pretext that flatters them; and — the honest one — when a regiment starts bleeding men, check whether the captain has been stealing their pay.

He assumes **a small state with long frontiers and larger neighbours**. The introduction says it flatly: he writes only for his own officers, speaks only of what applies to Prussians, and reckons as enemies only his neighbours, which he calls (in a phrase the scan mangles) unfortunately a synonym. Austria, Saxony, Russia. Every geographic example is Silesia, Bohemia, Moravia, Saxony, Brandenburg. The book is not a general theory; it is a regional operations manual that happens to contain general theory.

He assumes **magazine warfare**. An army eats from fortified depots in its rear, moved forward in echelon by wagon, converted to bread in iron field ovens. Campaigns begin when the green forage grows and stop when it does not. Three weeks' flour is the unit of operational reach. This single assumption governs more of the book than any other and is the reason so much of it is about roads, rivers, convoys and mills.

He assumes **linear tactics and close order**. Three ranks, contiguous squadrons, the attack at the quick step with the musket on the shoulder, fire withheld. He assumes Prussian troops load and manoeuvre faster than anyone else's, and he is explicit that this speed is the whole margin: "Un général audacieux chez les autres peuples n'est que dans les règles chez nous" — a general who would be reckless among other peoples is merely acting by the rules with us. That margin was temporary, and he knew it later; see §6.

He assumes **a literate, centralised administration**: commissaries, contracts, fixed relays of horses so that news from the most distant post reaches him in ninety minutes, four written reports a day at daybreak, ten, four and ten.

He assumes, finally, **that the reader is himself**. The addressee is his brother and heir, "à qui un demi-mot doit expliquer mes pensées." The book presupposes the drill regulations: "Je fonde tous mes raisonnements sur mes *Institutions militaires*; et comme c'est le catéchisme des officiers, je ne traite dans cet écrit que ce qui tient à la partie du général." It is the second layer. It assumes the first layer is already installed.

## 3. Core doctrine

Twenty principles. Loci are given by the article numbering of the French autograph as Preuss prints it, with Foster's English article number in brackets where it differs, and page numbers where I could read a running head in the scan. Foster's numbering runs three behind from Article III onward because three articles were never printed in the English line.

**1. Preserving the instrument is the first duty, before any plan.**
*Locus:* Art. I, pp. 4–6.
*His reasoning:* Other armies can replace a clumsy deserter with a clod and lose nothing. Prussia cannot: a soldier drilled two years to the required degree of skill, leaving and badly replaced or not replaced at all, "tire à conséquence à la longue." He has seen whole regiments melt away by desertion. And are there in his states many men of the height his soldiers are, and if there were, would they be trained tomorrow?
*Limits:* The whole argument is specific to a long-service drilled army. He does not claim it of armies in general; he says the opposite of them.

**2. Attrition is administrative, and so is its cure.**
*Locus:* Art. I, pp. 5–6.
*His reasoning:* The fourteen rules are all dull — camp siting, patrols, march discipline, officers posted at defiles, punishing marauding, checking the captain's books. None is inspirational. That is the point: men are lost by routine negligence, so they are kept by routine attention.
*Limits:* He concedes the officers will not do it on their own. "La plus grande partie d'une armée est composée de gens indolents; si le général n'est sans cesse à leurs trousses, toute cette machine si ingénieuse et si parfaite se détraquera bien vite."

**3. Your neighbours are your enemies; build the plan on that, not on wishes.**
*Locus:* Introduction, p. 3; Art. II, p. 8.
*His reasoning:* "Pour l'ordinaire, les voisins d'un prince sont ses ennemis." Politics and the military art must work together in a campaign plan: know the strength of the prince you are fighting, his allies, and the country that is to become the theatre of your shame or your glory.
*Limits:* He fixes a ratio and refuses to go past it. Seventy-five thousand against a hundred thousand is enough; beyond a third's inferiority he offers nothing.

**4. Wars that take you far from your own frontiers are worthless.**
*Locus:* Art. II, p. 8.
*His reasoning:* "Toutes les guerres qui nous éloignent trop de nos frontières ne valent rien," and every other nation's attempt has ended badly: Charles XII's glory eclipsed in the wastes of Poltava, Charles VI unable to hold Spain, the French unable to hold Bohemia. Plans that aim at the far point are to be rejected as bad.
*Limits:* This is a statement about logistics reach under the magazine system, not a moral principle, and he says so by immediately talking about flour.

**5. Absolute defence is the worst of all plans.**
*Locus:* Art. II, pp. 8–9.
*His reasoning:* Strong camps invite envelopment; you dare not fight, so you withdraw; he turns you again; and on the reckoning "vous perdez plus de terrain par votre retraite qu'après la perte d'une bataille, et que votre armée se fondra plus considérablement par la désertion qu'après l'action la plus sanglante." Pure defence has everything to lose and nothing to hope. He would prefer the boldness of a general who risks a battle *à propos*, because that man has everything to hope, and in his very misfortune still has the defensive left.
*Limits:* *À propos.* The whole weight is on the timing, which Art. XXVI supplies.

**6. Against several strong enemies: split them politically, concede a province deliberately, destroy them one at a time.**
*Locus:* Art. II, p. 16.
*His reasoning:* The hardest plans are those against many powerful enemies. Then you use politics to set them against each other and buy one off. Militarily, "il faut alors savoir perdre à propos (qui veut défendre tout ne défendra rien), sacrifier une province à un ennemi, et marcher, en attendant, avec toutes vos forces contre les autres et les obliger à une bataille."
*Limits:* He says immediately what it costs: wars of this kind ruin armies by the marching, and if they last, they end badly anyway.

**7. Provisions are the foundation, and hunger is the real weapon.**
*Locus:* Art. III, p. 17; Art. II, pp. 9, 12.
*His reasoning:* "Quand on veut bâtir une armée, dit un grand général, il faut commencer par le ventre; c'en est le fondement." And: a general who does not provide himself with enough food, were he superior to Caesar, will not be a hero for long. In the 1747 draft he puts the corollary plainly: the great secret of war is to starve your enemy, which exhausts men more surely than courage and risks less than fighting.
*Limits:* He immediately concedes that starvation rarely ends a war and that matters are decided by great battles, so the starving is a means to the battle, not a substitute for it.

**8. Magazines in the rear, in echelon, inside fortresses.**
*Locus:* Art. III, p. 17.
*His reasoning:* A principal magazine in front of the army is lost at the first check and you are without resource; placed in echelon, "on ne fait pas la guerre en désespéré," and one small disgrace does not bring total loss.
*Limits:* He names the commissaries as the weak point — if they are crooks the state loses enormously — and prescribes honest supervisors rather than trusting the system.

**9. Know the country as a chess player knows the board, and acquire the knowledge physically.**
*Locus:* Art. II, p. 8; Art. VI, pp. 23–24.
*His reasoning:* Knowing the theatre is as necessary as knowing the chessboard if you mean to play chess. Two ways: first the map — big towns, rivers, the shape of the hills — then local knowledge: where the high roads run, which towns can be defended and from which side attacked, the plans of the fortresses and their strengths and weaknesses, which streams are impassable in spring and dry in summer. Plains are read quickly, "il semble que ce soit une carte géographique que l'on a couchée devant soi"; wooded and mountainous country is hard because the view is always bounded. For that you go into the mountains on horseback, map in hand, with the mayors of the nearby villages, hunters, shepherds, "et même des bouchers," and you climb the highest hill you can find.
*Limits:* Seasonal. He is explicit that the same ground is a different problem in April and August.

**10. *Coup d'œil* is two learnable things, and its foundation is fortification.**
*Locus:* Art. VII, pp. 25–26.
*His reasoning:* The first is judging on the spot how many troops a piece of ground will hold — acquired by practice, by laying out camps yourself until the eye stops being wrong about dimensions. The second, "tout à fait supérieur," is seeing at once every advantage the ground offers. "Le fondement de cette espèce de coup d'œil est sans contredit la fortification": you apply the rules of fortification to the positions of armies. In a square league there may be two hundred positions; the trained eye seizes the best, and by the same rules reads the weak place in the enemy's line.
*Limits:* He says the second talent can be acquired and perfected only "pourvu que l'on soit né avec un génie heureux pour la guerre," and that the judgement of an enemy's capacity at the start of a battle comes only from experience.

**11. Place each arm where it can act; convention is not a reason.**
*Locus:* Art. VIII, p. 26.
*His reasoning:* Modern orders of battle are defective because they are all cast in the same mould, infantry in the centre and cavalry on the wings. Cavalry near woods you do not control, by impassable marshes, or in ravines parallel to the camp is cavalry that cannot act, and a clever enemy will oppose it with infantry and guns and shoot it into flight. Put all the cavalry on one wing, or all in the second line, or divide it — according to the ground.
*Limits:* Applies to camps and orders of battle chosen at leisure; he allows that an army caught on the march takes what it can get.

**12. Do not detach. Separate your forces and you will be beaten in detail.**
*Locus:* Art. XI [Foster X], pp. 36–37.
*His reasoning:* "C'est une ancienne règle de guerre, et je ne fais que la répéter: si vous séparez vos forces, vous serez battu en détail; si vous voulez livrer bataille, rassemblez le plus de troupes que vous pourrez." He then lists the bill: Albemarle's detachment beaten at Denain, which lost Eugene the whole campaign; Starhemberg separated from the English and beaten at Villaviciosa; the Saxons beaten at Kesselsdorf for not calling in Prince Charles; Hildburghausen at Banjaluka. And: "J'aurais mérité d'être battu à Soor" — I should have deserved to be beaten at Soor, had my generals' skill and the troops' valour not saved me.
*Limits:* He does not forbid it absolutely. Detach for convoys; detach in mountainous countries like Bohemia and Moravia to hold the gorges your supplies come through, until you have a magazine in a fortified city. Then take two precautions: strong camps, and positions the detachment can hold alone until you can reach it.

**13. Who wants to keep everything keeps nothing. The essential thing is the enemy's main body.**
*Locus:* Art. XI [Foster X], pp. 37–38.
*His reasoning:* Defensive war naturally invites detachments. "Les petits génies veulent tout conserver, les hommes sensés ne voient que l'objet principal; ils parent les grands coups, et souffrent un petit mal pour en éviter un de plus grande conséquence; qui conserve tout ne conserve rien." Then the rule: the essential part to fasten on is the enemy's army — divine his real design and oppose it with all your force. His example is his own: in 1745 he abandoned Upper Silesia to be pillaged by the Hungarians so as to oppose Prince Charles with more force, and detached only after beating him, whereupon Nassau cleared Upper Silesia in a fortnight.
*Limits:* The example is the limit. The province was abandoned *temporarily*, with a beaten enemy and a fortnight's mopping-up in view — not written off.

**14. The general's first professional skill is dissimulation, because he is always on stage.**
*Locus:* Art. XII [not in Foster], pp. 39–41.
*His reasoning:* A perfect captain is a creature of reason — Plato's republic, the philosophers' centre of gravity, the chemists' potable gold. Perfection is incompatible with humanity. But the necessary qualities can still be listed, and the first is hiding one's thought, because "toute l'armée lit son sort sur son visage." Thoughtful, and the officers say he is hatching something; sad, and they say affairs go badly, and imagine worse than the truth; and these rumours run through the camp and pass into the enemy's. So the general must be like an actor who sets his face to the part, and if he cannot master himself he must feign illness or invent a pretext.
*Limits:* He puts a floor under it before he starts: "Je suppose, devant toutes choses, qu'il soit honnête homme et bon citoyen, qualités sans lesquelles l'habileté et l'art de la guerre sont plus pernicieux qu'utiles."

**15. Distrust everything; distrust most after your greatest successes; reason from the enemy's chair.**
*Locus:* Art. XII [not in Foster], p. 41.
*His reasoning:* "Les Normands donnent une règle à leurs enfants: Défie-toi. — De qui? — De tout le monde." Only fools trust their enemies. But security lulls you, and it is after the greatest advantages that one must be most suspicious: you think the enemy discouraged and fall into lethargy over all his enterprises; or a skilful enemy amuses you with feigned peace proposals. The working method: "Il faut toujours raisonner sur la situation où l'on se trouve, et dire: Quel dessein formerais-je, si j'étais de l'ennemi?" Imagine several, then think how to make them fail, and correct on the spot what is defective in your position, camp, depots or detachments.
*Limits:* He puts the brake on himself: "Que tout ceci ne vous rende pas timide, car la hardiesse veut être jointe à la circonspection; et comme on ne peut jamais démontrer la sûreté d'une entreprise, il suffit de la bien disposer. L'événement doit se remettre à la fortune."

**16. Intelligence converts inferiority into superiority, and is worth paying for extravagantly.**
*Locus:* Art. XIV [Foster XII], pp. 46–48.
*His reasoning:* "Si l'on savait toujours les desseins des ennemis d'avance, avec une armée inférieure on leur serait supérieur." Everyone tries; few succeed. Four classes: small people who take up the trade, double spies, spies of consequence, and those forced by violence into the wretched employment. The small sort — townsmen, peasants, priests — can only tell you where the enemy is camped, and their reports are so confused that they leave you less certain than complete ignorance would. Deserters know their own regiment and no more. So you write depositions down, which is the only way to get anything out of them. Double spies are for feeding the enemy false news. The best are close to the enemy general.
*Limits:* The method is ugly and he does not hide it. When nothing else works, seize a substantial citizen with a wife, children and a house, force him to take your disguised man into the enemy camp as his servant, and threaten to have his wife and children cut down and his house burned if he does not bring your man back. "J'ai été obligé de me servir de ce moyen lorsque nous étions au camp de Chlum, et cela me réussit."

**17. Population, country and religion are instruments.**
*Locus:* Art. XVI [Foster XIV], pp. 49–51.
*His reasoning:* Three kinds of country — your own, neutral, enemy — and within enemy country, Catholic and Protestant. In your own, detachments can be bold and are always successful, and failure is unpardonable. In a neutral country you make friends: enforce good discipline, paint your enemies as barbarous, and if the people are Protestant make them believe a shared zeal attaches you to them. "Le fanatisme en tient lieu, et lorsqu'on peut animer le peuple pour la liberté de sa conscience, qu'il est agité par des prêtres et des dévots, on peut entièrement compter sur lui; c'est intéresser à votre cause Dieu et les enfers."
*Limits:* He gives the counter-case immediately: in an enemy Catholic country like Bohemia or Moravia, raise no partisans — they will all be lost, the country being hostile to them — and keep to a close-fisted war with light troops on the defensive.

**18. Battles decide, and being forced into one is evidence of your own earlier mistake.**
*Locus:* Art. XXVI [Foster XXIII], pp. 83–84.
*His reasoning:* "Les batailles décident du sort des États." A reasonable man makes no move without a good motive, and a general still less should fight without an important object; "et, s'il est forcé de se battre, c'est toujours parce qu'il a commis quelque faute qui l'a réduit à recevoir cette fière loi de son ennemi." He then convicts himself: of five battles his troops have given, only three were premeditated; he was forced at Mollwitz, because the Austrians got between his army and Ohlau where his guns and food were, and at Soor, because they cut the Trautenau road. "Ainsi, en donnant des préceptes ici que je n'ai pas suivis moi-même, par imprudence, c'est pour que mes officiers profitent de mes fautes." The best battles are those into which you force the enemy, because your interests are diametrically opposed and you must therefore want everything he does not want.
*Limits:* He lists the legitimate motives and no others: to raise a siege of one of your places, to drive him from a province, to penetrate his, to lay a siege, to overcome his obstinacy against peace. And wars must be short: a long one insensibly relaxes discipline, depopulates the country and exhausts its resources.

**19. The oblique order: how the weaker beats the stronger.**
*Locus:* Art. XXV.7 [inside Foster XXII], pp. 74–75.
*His reasoning:* When Prussian numbers are inferior, do not despair; the general's disposition must supply the numbers. Weak armies should seek close, hilly country, because the ground is narrow everywhere, so that the enemy's numbers, unable to overlap you, become useless and sometimes a burden — "ainsi le terrain remit une sorte d'égalité entre les deux armées," which is why Soor was won at half the Austrian strength. Then the disposition: refuse one wing to the enemy and reinforce the one that is to attack, and with it throw everything against one enemy wing, taking it in flank. "Une armée de cent mille hommes, prise en flanc, peut être battue par trente mille hommes, car l'affaire se décide alors bien vite." The left is not to advance until the enemy's left is wholly beaten.
*Limits:* He states the three advantages precisely, and the third is a limit as much as an advantage: a small number can measure itself against a superior body; part of your army attacks on a decisive side; and if you are beaten, only part of your army has been beaten, and three quarters of fresh troops remain to make the retreat.

**20. Councils of war are the machinery by which a general avoids doing anything.**
*Locus:* Art. XXVIII [Foster XXV], p. 88.
*His reasoning:* "Le prince Eugène disait qu'il n'y avait rien de tel que de tenir un conseil de guerre toutes les fois qu'un général avait envie de ne rien entreprendre. Cela est si vrai, que la plupart des voix sont toujours pour la négative." A general to whom the prince entrusts his troops must act by himself; and secrecy, so necessary in war, is never kept in a council.
*Limits:* He immediately concedes the opposite case: a general to whom a subordinate opens a good opinion should profit by it, because a true citizen forgets himself where the state's business is concerned and does not care whose idea it was. In Art. XII he gives the technique — take the idea without remarking on it, and after it succeeds name the man in front of many officers.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

The text speaks to this **directly and at length, and means something narrower by it than a modern reader will.**

Directly addressed. Article XXV, section 7 is literally headed *Comment on peut battre l'ennemi à forces inégales* — how one can beat the enemy with unequal forces — and its first sentence is that when Prussian numbers are inferior to the enemy's, one must not despair of beating them, but the general's disposition must then supply the numbers. That is the clearest statement of the weak actor's problem anywhere in the thirteen-text list, and Frederick's answer has three parts, all concrete: choose close and hilly ground so that the enemy's surplus cannot be brought to bear and becomes a burden; refuse one wing; mass everything against one flank. Article XIV adds the fourth part and the most important: superior information makes an inferior army superior.

Article II adds a fifth, for the case of several enemies at once: split them by politics, buy one off with advantages, deliberately sacrifice a province, and beat the rest one at a time — "savoir perdre à propos."

Also directly addressed, and usually forgotten: **absolute defence is worse than fighting.** His argument is arithmetic, not morale. A pure defensive loses ground at every turning movement and men by desertion, "plus considérablement qu'après l'action la plus sanglante," with nothing to hope for. A risked battle at the right moment has everything to hope, and even in defeat leaves you the defensive you would otherwise have chosen.

**But be exact about the kind of weakness he means.** Frederick's weak man is a king with seventy-five thousand men, a full treasury, fortresses, magazines and the best-drilled infantry in Europe, facing a hundred thousand. He sets the limit himself: do not embarrass yourself about the enemy's numbers "provided they do not exceed you more than a third." Beyond that ratio the book has nothing. He is weak the way a small state is weak among large ones — relatively, and with an intact instrument. He is never poor, never unknown, never without position. There is no passage anywhere in the thirty-one articles addressed to a man with no resources at all.

**Inference, clearly labelled as mine.** What can be extracted for a reader who really has nothing is not Frederick's situation but his arithmetic. Three of his moves do not depend on being a king: (a) choosing terrain that makes the opponent's surplus unusable rather than trying to match the surplus; (b) accepting a named, chosen loss in order to be strong at one point; (c) buying information at prices that look absurd relative to your means, because information is the only input that multiplies. The fourth — refusing one wing — depends on having a wing to refuse, and is the point at which the analogy starts to cost more than it pays.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Central, and structurally so. The book is explicitly the second storey on a building whose ground floor is the drill regulations: "Je fonde tous mes raisonnements sur mes *Institutions militaires*; et comme c'est le catéchisme des officiers, je ne traite dans cet écrit que ce qui tient à la partie du général." The introduction sets the standard: the Roman discipline survives nowhere but with us, and following their example, "il faut de même que la guerre nous soit une méditation, et la paix un exercice." Preuss's footnote attaches this to Vegetius, *De re militari*, book I, chapter 1.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** The most developed subject in the book after supply, and the one Frederick most nearly reduces to a formula: perfect foreknowledge of the enemy's designs makes an inferior army superior. He classifies sources by reliability rather than by romance, rates most of them low, writes down deserters' statements because that is the only way to extract value from them, and pays without limit. He is equally frank about his own counter-intelligence: communicate the general plan of campaign at most to the officer responsible for supplies, encode letters to detached generals, and when preparations cannot be hidden, deceive your own officers so that their indiscretion carries the false version.

**Timing.** Not treated as a separate topic, but it is the hinge of the argument about battle: fight *à propos*, and being forced to fight proves you missed your moment earlier. Elsewhere: "les heures décident de beaucoup à la guerre, et c'est là que l'on apprend à connaître le prix des moments," with the instruction to correct a defective position *sur-le-champ*.

**Terrain and position.** Treated at greater length than anything except supply — Articles VI to X are all ground. The two governing claims: ground is read through the rules of fortification, not by instinct; and ground is what equalises unequal numbers, "le terrain remit une sorte d'égalité entre les deux armées." Two standing warnings: never occupy a camp you can only leave through a defile, because a vigilant enemy seizes the defile and takes you without fighting; and never take a position "qu'un honnête homme ne saurait soutenir sans risquer sa réputation."

**Deception and information.** Article XIII is entirely stratagem. The principle: feign the contrary of what you intend. March and counter-march so the enemy cannot learn where you are assembling; camp so he fears for two or three cities at once and go to the other side when he hurries to one; to force a battle, pretend to fear it, because his self-confidence becomes your accomplice. To avoid a battle, do the opposite — make apparent war with all the attitudes of the offensive, do not withdraw easily, and then steal away by a night march planned long before. He names the masters of this: "Cette sorte de guerre est le chef-d'œuvre des Autrichiens, et c'est d'eux qu'il faut l'apprendre."

**Alliances.** Brief but not silent. In Article II, against several enemies, recourse to politics "pour les brouiller entre eux et pour en détacher l'un ou l'autre par des avantages qu'on leur procure." In the plan against Bohemia, the frank admission that the country cannot be held by making war in it, and that taking it permanently from Austria requires an allied army moving up the Danube while yours crosses Moravia, both arriving at Vienna together. In Article XI, the standing lesson that allies who fail to join are the reason detachments get destroyed. He also records the dependency without embarrassment: the winter of 1741–42 in Bohemia was survivable only because the French were occupying the Austrians.

**Logistics and economy.** The largest subject in the book. Belly first. Magazines in the rear, in echelon, in fortresses, on rivers where possible. Grain drawn from peasants and gentlemen against their tax, with contractors only where that is physically impossible, "car ces gens pillent sans miséricorde." Three weeks' flour with the army; bread for ten days and biscuit for five on an expedition; hand mills issued to each company so the soldiers can grind what they find. Convoys built around infantry, not cavalry. Ovens moving with the commissariat. The economy of the state enters as a limit on the duration of war: a long war relaxes discipline, depopulates the country, exhausts its resources.

**Morale and discipline.** Discipline is the foundation of both the glory and the preservation of the state, and the whole first article is its maintenance. On morale the instruction is behavioural, not rhetorical: the general speaks to the men on the march, visits them while they cook, asks whether they are cared for, praises inexperienced officers and grants them small favours, and punishes the negligent one. "Si vous voulez gagner l'amitié du soldat, ne le fatiguez ni ne l'exposez sans qu'il voie que cela est nécessaire. Soyez leur père, et non pas leur bourreau." And the mechanism of confidence: shorten the fight, because the more vigorous the attack the less it costs, and a soldier led that way trusts the general and exposes himself cheerfully.

**The decisive point.** Explicit. An army in position is to be attacked "à l'endroit où il y a le moins de résistance à craindre," against the school that says attack the centre. Whoever has the best *coup d'œil* perceives the weak spot at first glance. And in the article on detachments the decisive point is named at the strategic level too: the essential thing is the enemy's army, and beat that and the detachments run away by themselves.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Present as warning rather than theory. After victory, pursue to the first defile, do everything possible to the enemy, but do not get drunk on success; camp by the rules; remember that armies have lost the fruit of their heroism through false security. And: "il ne faut jamais s'imaginer d'avoir tout fait, lorsqu'il reste encore quelque chose à faire." He also states the stopping condition at the strategic level — wars should be short — and mocks Luxembourg, who when his son suggested they could still take another town replied, hold your tongue, would you have us go home and plant cabbages.

**When not to fight.** Not when you have no important object; not when a council of war would be needed to decide it; not against villages, because the attacks are so murderous that he has made it a law to avoid them, since you can lose the flower of your infantry there and no man's lifetime will produce a better one; not against a standing battery position whose flank cannot be turned, because case shot at three hundred and fifty paces shoots a battalion full of holes before it arrives; not from inside an entrenchment, which he says he would never put himself into except after a terrible misfortune.

**Defence versus offence.** He is one-sidedly offensive and knows it. Pure defence is worthless; the first of two commanders to adopt an offensive attitude almost always reduces the other to the defensive and makes him conform; "la puissance des Prussiens consiste dans l'attaque." His own preferred form is "la défensive qui se tourne en offensive" — appear timid, entrench so as to look fearful, let him pass, get behind him, cut him from his supplies, and fight on ground you chose. The rule he says he practises himself: to have rest oneself it is necessary to keep the enemy occupied.

**The character required of the commander.** Article XII, the one the English tradition lost. Honest man and good citizen first, "qualités sans lesquelles l'habileté et l'art de la guerre sont plus pernicieux qu'utiles." Then the list of contradictory virtues: dissembling while appearing natural, mild and severe, ceaselessly suspicious and always tranquil, sparing of his soldiers' blood out of humanity and sometimes prodigal of it, working with the head and acting with the body, discreet, deep, informed of everything, not forgetting one thing to do another, and not despising as beneath him "ces petits détails qui tiennent si fort aux grandes choses." And at the close of the whole book, the quality he puts last: he asks his officers to tell him where he is wrong, so that he can explain his reasons more fully "ou pour me ranger de votre avis, si je suis tombé en défaut."

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The quotation nobody has read.** "He who defends everything defends nothing" is the single most-circulated line attributed to Frederick, a staple of management writing and of information-security writing in particular. It is not a fabrication — but almost everything said about it is wrong. I grepped all three public-domain English editions in my cache (1762, Foster's third edition digitised as the 1797 item, and the 1818 Foster) for every plausible English form. **The phrase does not occur in any of them.** What Frederick wrote is in French, in two places, with two different verbs. In Article XI, on detachments: *qui conserve tout ne conserve rien.* In Article II, on campaign plans against several enemies: *qui veut défendre tout ne défendra rien* — in a parenthesis, in the future tense, attached to the instruction to sacrifice a province. **And Article II was never printed in the German translation of 1753, and therefore never reached the English tradition at all.** The English editions most often cited as the source of the quotation are the ones that could not possibly contain it. Foster, who does translate Article XI, drops the *conserver* sentence entirely; his text reads only "Generals of little experience are anxious to preserve every thing, whilst the man of intelligence and enterprize regards only the grand point." Phillips's 1940 English, translating the earlier 1747 draft, has it as an "ancient apothegm" — an attribution to antiquity which does not appear in Preuss's French of the 1748 text.

The substantive misreading follows from the textual one. In context the line is not a general injunction to focus. It is advice for a specific, desperate configuration — several strong enemies at once — and it prescribes a specific, painful act: name a province, give it up, and accept the political cost. Frederick's own illustration is that he let the Hungarians pillage Upper Silesia for a season. Quoted as a slogan it says "prioritise." Read in place it says "choose what you will lose, and lose it on purpose."

**Leuthen.** The brief that commissioned this file assumes the Leuthen example is in the text. It cannot be. Leuthen was fought on 5 December 1757; the work was signed 2 April 1748 and printed in German in 1753. The oblique order *is* in the text, at Article XXV.7, with a plan, three named advantages, and the claim that a hundred thousand taken in flank can be beaten by thirty thousand — but the battle everyone associates with it postdates the book by nearly a decade, and any account that has Frederick writing up Leuthen for his generals has the chronology backwards.

**Imitating the forms instead of the reasoning.** Napoleon's complaint, quoted by Phillips from Colonel E. M. Lloyd's *A Review of the History of Infantry*, is that Frederick laughed in his sleeve at the Potsdam parades when he saw young French, English and Austrian officers so infatuated with the manoeuvre of the oblique order that it was fit for nothing but to make a few adjutant-majors a reputation. This is the standard failure mode with this text: the drill, the uniform and the geometric diagram are copyable; the supply system, the intelligence expenditure and the judgement of the moment are not.

**Frederick's own retraction.** The most damaging critic of the *Instructions* is Frederick. In the Political Testament of 7 November 1768, quoted by Preuss in his editorial preface, he tells his successor: "Je joins à cet ouvrage l'*Instruction* que j'ai donnée à mes généraux. Il ne faut pas s'étonner si vous trouvez quelque contradiction dans ce que j'écris à présent et ce que contient cette *Instruction*. La raison en est que j'avais minuté cette *Instruction* après la paix de 1748, et que, dans les guerres précédentes, l'ennemi ne connaissait ni le terrain ni la tactique, que son artillerie était pitoyable, et que son infanterie ne valait pas mieux." Twenty years of being copied had cancelled the margin the book was built on. Phillips, in his introduction, notes the corresponding reversal on fire: the 1747 text has the infantry attack with the musket on the shoulder and fire as little as possible, while by 1758 Frederick was writing that attacking without at least equal fire is to fight an armed troop with clubs, and in the Military Testament that battles are won by superiority of fire.

**The Delbrück controversy (*Strategiestreit*).** The serious scholarly fight over Frederick, and the one any reader of this text should know about. Hans Delbrück argued — in *Die Strategie des Perikles erläutert durch die Strategie Friedrichs des Grossen* (1890) and in volume IV of the *Geschichte der Kriegskunst* — that Clausewitz had meant to distinguish a strategy of annihilation (*Niederwerfungs-* or *Vernichtungsstrategie*) from a strategy of exhaustion (*Ermattungsstrategie*) with two poles, battle and manoeuvre, and that Frederick belonged to the second. The German General Staff's historians and their allies — Friedrich von Bernhardi, Rudolf von Caemmerer, Max Jähns, Reinhold Koser — took this as an attack on the army's doctrine and replied, with increasing heat, that Frederick had not shunned decisive battle, and pointed to Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757. The two sides were substantially arguing about different levels of war: Delbrück about strategy in its political frame, the staff historians about operations. The quarrel ran on into the 1920s and 1930s. The text itself supports Delbrück more than his opponents on one point and them more on another: Frederick says battles decide the fate of states, *and* that being forced to fight one is proof of a prior error.

**The Prussian legend.** Frederick was made into a founding ancestor by the Nazi regime, which drew the parallel between his territorial expansion and its own; the blog entry notes this correctly. It is a reception fact, not a fact about the book, and the book gives it no purchase: the *Principes généraux* contain no racial theory, no theory of the nation, and no theory of conquest beyond a flat statement that wars taking you far from your frontiers are worthless.

**The indecisiveness critique.** Russell F. Weigley, *The Age of Battles: The Quest for Decisive Warfare from Breitenfeld to Waterloo* (1991), argues that the battles of this era, Frederick's included, bought strategic results out of all proportion to their cost. Christopher Duffy (*The Army of Frederick the Great*, 1974; *The Military Life of Frederick the Great*, 1985) and Dennis Showalter (*The Wars of Frederick the Great*, 1996) are the standard modern military treatments; Robert M. Citino (*The German Way of War*, 2005) reads Frederick as the founding figure of a Prussian-German preference for short wars of movement — which is, note, exactly what Article XXVI prescribes.

**The translation problem, which is a critique in its own right.** Beyond the four-stage chain and the three missing articles, Foster's own preface concedes his French was slender and that ignorance may have caused "some deviation from the true meaning." Anyone quoting the *Instructions* from a free English text is quoting a translation of a translation of a pirated translation, made by a man who said he might have got it wrong. Jay Luvaas, *Frederick the Great on the Art of War* (New York: The Free Press, 1966), is the standard modern English selection and translates much of Frederick's military writing into English for the first time; it is in copyright and I have not read it, so I do not quote it.

**Popular attributions that do not belong to this text.**

- *"Hunde, wollt ihr ewig leben?"* / "Rascals, would you live forever?" — placed variously at Kolin (18 June 1757) and Kunersdorf (12 August 1759), with several competing wordings, and doubted by Carlyle, Frederick's most exhaustive biographer. Not in the *Principes généraux*. Treat as legend.
- *"God is always on the side of the big battalions."* Routinely attached to Frederick. I grepped Preuss's French of the whole *Principes généraux* for *gros bataillons* and *gros escadrons*: no occurrence. The saying has older French antecedents usually credited to Bussy-Rabutin and to Voltaire; I have not opened either source, so I record only that it is not in this text.
- *"He who defends everything defends nothing"* in that exact English wording: see above. The idea is his; the sentence, in any English edition I could open, is not.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

**Start with what does not transfer, because it is most of the book and pretending otherwise would falsify everything after it.**

Frederick inherited a working machine. His father spent twenty-seven years building the drilled infantry, the cantonal recruiting system, the officer corps and the treasury that made the son's audacity affordable. The *Principes généraux* is explicitly the second volume: it says so, and refers the reader to the drill regulations as "le catéchisme des officiers" for everything below the level of generalship. Nothing in this text tells you how to build an instrument. It tells you how to spend one. A man with nothing is at the stage Frederick's father was at, not the stage Frederick was at, and the book has no advice for that stage at all. Read it as a manual for the situation you are in and it will make you spend capital you do not have.

Second, the entire apparatus is sovereign. Frederick commands the treasury, the magistrates, the recruiting, the law and the church. When ordinary intelligence-gathering fails, his remedy is to seize a prosperous citizen and threaten to kill his wife and children — and he records that he did it, at Chlum, and that it worked. When he needs a neutral population, his remedy is to manufacture religious feeling and "interest God and hell in your cause." These are not incidental ugliness; they are the methods the book's logic produces when it is held by someone with coercive power and no one above him. A reader without power cannot use them, and a reader who acquires a little power and does use them has misunderstood what he was reading. Say it plainly: much of Frederick's toolkit is available only to a king, and the parts of it that are available to anybody are the parts that are criminal.

Third, his "weakness" is not yours. He sets the boundary himself: do not worry about the enemy's numbers provided they do not exceed you by more than a third. Seventy-five thousand against a hundred thousand. He is a small state among large ones, not a man among institutions. The oblique order works because he has an army with two wings and can afford to refuse one. If you have no wing, there is nothing to refuse, and the geometry does not apply. This is the point at which the analogy stops paying and starts costing.

Fourth, his own verdict on the book's shelf life. In 1768 he told his heir not to be surprised at the contradictions between what he was writing then and what the *Instruction* contained, because when he drafted it the enemy knew neither the ground nor the tactics, his artillery was pitiful and his infantry no better. The margin the book is built on had been copied away in twenty years. Any specific technique in it should be read as a technique with an expiry date that has passed.

**Now the parts that do transfer, with the reasoning.**

*The first article is about attrition, not victory, and that ordering is the book's best single idea.* Frederick opens not with how to win but with how to stop losing the men he already has — fourteen dull administrative rules, including checking whether the captain has been stealing the soldiers' pay. His argument is that a trained man badly replaced "tire à conséquence à la longue." A man starting with nothing has almost no capital except the few capacities he has actually built and the few people who will actually take his call, and he loses those the same way Frederick lost soldiers: not to defeat but to quiet, unattended leakage. The transferable move is to put the leak audit first, before the plan, and to accept that the fix is boring.

*Absolute defence loses more than a lost battle.* Frederick's case is arithmetic: a pure defensive gives up more ground by retreating than you lose by losing, and melts more men by desertion than the bloodiest action, and has nothing to hope for at the end. For a man with nothing, the equivalent of absolute defence is not committing — staying unexposed, keeping options, never putting the thing in front of anyone who can reject it. It feels safe and it is the one posture with a guaranteed negative expectation. But note the condition Frederick attaches, because it is the whole of the advice: the general risks a battle *à propos*, at the right moment, having chosen the ground and fed his army. Not any battle. The alternative to absolute defence is not recklessness; it is one prepared commitment.

*Being forced to act proves you missed your moment earlier.* "S'il est forcé de se battre, c'est toujours parce qu'il a commis quelque faute." This is the most usable diagnostic in the book because it is retrospective and cheap. Whenever you found yourself with no choice — had to take the work, had to accept the terms, had to answer that day — the error is not in the forced move; it is somewhere behind it, in a position taken or a supply not laid in. Frederick applies it to himself in print, names Mollwitz and Soor, and says he publishes his own faults so his officers can use them. Two of his five battles failed his own test.

*Deliberate, named loss.* "Qui veut défendre tout ne défendra rien" is advice for the man facing several stronger opponents, and what it actually prescribes is to pick a province and let it be pillaged. Not to prioritise — to concede, specifically, and to say which. A man with nothing is by definition short on every front at once, and the instinct is to keep every front barely alive. Frederick's instruction is to write down what you are abandoning, abandon it properly, and put everything into one place. His own example is honest about the cost: Upper Silesia was pillaged for a season.

*Ground before force.* Weak armies seek close, hilly country, because there the enemy's surplus cannot be brought to bear and becomes a burden. The general form is: do not try to match the larger opponent's quantity; move to a domain where his quantity has nowhere to deploy. Soor was won at half the Austrian strength because the ground would not let them overlap. This is the one strategic move in the book that costs nothing but judgement, which is why it is the most transferable thing in it.

*The eye is a body of rules, not a gift.* Frederick demystifies *coup d'œil* in a way almost no other writer on this list does: the first half is acquired by laying out camps yourself until you stop misjudging dimensions, and the second half rests "sans contredit" on the rules of fortification — an existing technical discipline you can learn and then apply to a new object. What looks like instinct in an experienced man is a learned rule set running fast. The practical consequence for someone starting: find the boring technical layer under the judgement you admire, and learn that.

*Information is the one input that converts inferiority into superiority, and it should be overpaid for.* Frederick states the conversion directly and then behaves accordingly: pay spies with "une libéralité prodigue," because a man who risks the rope deserves it. But pair that with his ruthless rating of sources — townspeople and priests can tell you only where the enemy is camped, and their reports leave you less certain than ignorance; deserters know their own regiment and no more; so write the depositions down, which is the only way to get value from them. The transferable discipline is two-sided: spend disproportionately to find out what is actually happening, and grade every source by what it is structurally in a position to know.

*Reason from the other chair, and be most suspicious after you win.* "Quel dessein formerais-je, si j'étais de l'ennemi?" Imagine several of his plans, then work out how to make each fail, and fix the defect in your own position immediately, because "les heures décident de beaucoup." And: it is after the greatest advantages that one must be most distrustful, because you believe the other side is finished and fall into lethargy over everything he does. The first good month is where this most often costs people.

*Committees are how you avoid deciding.* Prince Eugene's line, that a general who wants to undertake nothing need only call a council of war, with Frederick's gloss that most of the votes are always for the negative. But take his qualification too, because it is the useful half: take the good idea from whoever offers it, say nothing at the time, and name him publicly after it works. Frederick's stated reason is unsentimental — "votre modestie, au lieu de vous attirer des envieux, vous gagne des amis."

*Supply before ambition.* Belly first; a general who has not provisioned himself, though he were superior to Caesar, will not be a hero for long; magazines behind you, not in front, so that one reverse does not take everything; and plans that carry you far from your own frontiers are worthless. For a man with nothing this converts almost mechanically into: know how many weeks you can run, keep the reserve behind you rather than committed forward, and refuse the plan whose logistics you cannot describe — however good it looks.

*Last, the thing that makes the book usable at all.* Frederick ends by asking his officers to tell him where he is wrong so that he can either explain himself better "ou pour me ranger de votre avis, si je suis tombé en défaut," and says his small experience has convinced him the art is inexhaustible. This from an absolute monarch writing for subordinates who could be broken at his word, in a document they were sworn not to carry into the field. It is also the one instruction in the entire text that a man with nothing can execute today at no cost.
