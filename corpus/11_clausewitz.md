# 11. Carl von Clausewitz — On War (Vom Kriege)

## 1. Identity

*Vom Kriege* is an unfinished manuscript published after its author's death. That single fact governs how everything else in it must be read, and it is the fact most often dropped.

Carl von Clausewitz (born Burg, near Magdeburg, 1780; entered the Prussian army as a Fahnenjunker in 1792) drafted the work from 1816, when he was posted at Coblenz, through the spring of 1830. His widow, Marie von Clausewitz, née Countess von Brühl, published it in Berlin with Ferdinand Dümmler as Parts 1–3 of the ten-volume *Hinterlassene Werke über Krieg und Kriegführung*: Part 1 in 1832, Part 2 in 1833, Part 3 in 1834. Her preface is dated 30 June 1832 from the Marble Palace, Potsdam. She states that the papers were published "exactly in the condition in which they were found, without a word being added or erased."

The work is in eight books. This translation's table of contents carries 128 chapter entries; the nominal count is 125 chapters, because Book VI ch. XX and Book VIII chs. III and VI are each split into an A and a B part. The distribution is: I (8), II (6), III (18), IV (14), V (18), VI (30), VII (22), VIII (9).

Two authorial notes govern the whole. The **Notice of 10 July 1827** announces a revision that was never carried out: the first six books are "only a mass which is still in a manner without form," to be reworked around two ideas — the two kinds of war (overthrow of the enemy vs. limited conquest for bargaining) and the principle that "War is only a continuation of State policy by other means." A second, **undated memorandum** found among his papers — Marie says it "appears of very recent date" — contains the line that every serious reader has to hold onto: "The first chapter of the first book is the only one which I consider as completed."

**Translation used.** J. J. Graham (Colonel, 1808–83), *On War*, London: N. Trübner, 1873 — the first complete English translation, public domain. The local file is Project Gutenberg ebook #1946, which reproduces the 1909 London three-volume reprint "new and revised edition with an introduction and notes by Colonel F. N. Maude." Two cautions follow from that. First, the Gutenberg front matter states "1874 was 1st edition of this translation"; the translation was published in 1873. Second, and more important for anyone quoting from this file: **the footnotes marked (EDITOR) are Maude's, not Clausewitz's**, and Maude uses them to argue his own Edwardian positions — e.g. the note at Book III ch. VIII complaining that England has "not yet... arrived at a correct appreciation of the value of superior numbers," and the note at Book VI ch. I reminding the reader that "all this antedates by some years the introduction of long-range weapons." Do not cite Maude as Clausewitz. Howard and Paret (Princeton, 1976) is the standard modern English text and is under copyright; it is quoted here only sparingly and by name.

One transcription defect in this file: in the body text, Book VI ch. XXII ("The Cordon") is headed "CHAPTER XX." The front table of contents has the numbering right. Cite Book VI ch. XXII.

**Correcting the blog's commentary (entries 21 and 22).**

- *The blog says* Clausewitz "served as President of the Prussian War College, where he wrote his seminal work, On War, until his death of Cholera in 1831." *The evidence says* the title and the timeline are both wrong. On the title: Graham's own memoir in this edition records that "In 1818, he became Major-General, and Director of the Military School at which he had been previously educated." Marie's preface is more damaging still to the word "President": she notes that the post "was not satisfactory to him, as, according to the existing organisation of the Academy, the scientific part of the course is not under the Director, but conducted by a Board of Studies." He was the administrative head of an institution whose curriculum he did not control. The image of Clausewitz teaching strategy from a chair of authority is false.
- *The blog says* he wrote the book at that post "until his death." *The evidence says* he stopped in the spring of 1830, two clear years before his death. Marie: "in the spring of 1830, he was appointed to the artillery... He then put his papers in order, sealed up the separate packets, labelled them, and took sorrowful leave of this employment which he loved so much." He went to Breslau as Chief of the Second Artillery District, then in December 1830 became Chief of Staff to Gneisenau on the Polish frontier. He returned to Breslau on 7 November 1831 and died on the 16th. Graham's memoir gives the cause as cholera, "the seeds of which he must have brought with him from the army on the Polish frontier." So the cholera and the year are right; "until his death" is not. This matters because the two years of silence are exactly why the book is unfinished.
- *The blog omits* the single most consequential fact about the text: it is posthumous, unrevised, and internally inconsistent by the author's own admission, and it exists because his widow published it. A reader who does not know this will read Clausewitz's superseded positions as his settled doctrine. This omission is the blog's worst error on this entry.
- *The blog says* the work focuses "on conventional warfare between states." *The evidence says* this is materially incomplete. Book VI ch. XXVI, "Arming the Nation," is a full chapter on people's war, sets out five conditions for its effectiveness, and treats the *levée en masse* as a real instrument that will spread by imitation. The book also devotes Book VI ch. XXV to strategic retreat into the interior and treats the Spanish and Russian cases at length.
- *The blog says* it "was written between 1816 and 1830." *This is correct*, and matches Marie's preface precisely; she dates the scientific labour from Coblenz in 1816 and the cessation to spring 1830. Credit where it is due.
- *The blog says* the 1984 Weinberger Doctrine "was modeled on Clausewitzian concepts." *The evidence says* the transmission is real but runs through an intermediary the blog does not name, and that intermediary got Clausewitz wrong. The Vietnam-era reading that fed Weinberger's six conditions came through Col. Harry Summers's *On Strategy* (Strategic Studies Institute 1981; trade edition 1982), whose "people / army / government" trinity is not Clausewitz's trinity (see §6). Saying the doctrine was modelled on Clausewitz, without saying it was modelled on Summers's Clausewitz, makes a misreading look like a lineage.
- Two smaller points. The blog's "Brigadier General" is a reasonable English gloss on Prussian Generalmajor (1818). And the blog's "liaison officer between Prussian and Russian armies from 1813–1814" drops 1812, which is the year that matters: Clausewitz entered Russian service in 1812 and carried the letters that produced York's Convention of Tauroggen — an episode he described himself and which Graham reproduces at length in the memoir.

## 2. The world the text assumes

Clausewitz writes from inside a specific and short-lived condition, and he knows it. Book VIII ch. III.B is an explicit historical survey whose purpose is to show "how each period has had its own peculiar forms of war, its own restrictive conditions, and its own prejudices."

**The state as the unit, and a small number of states.** "Europe was divided into a dozen kingdoms and two republics; it was now conceivable that two of these powers might fight with each other without ten times as many others being mixed up in the quarrel." War is between governments that can conclude a peace, and peace is what ends it. There is no concept here of a war without a terminus.

**Two regimes of war, one of which had just replaced the other.** Before 1793, armies were "an instrument of the cabinet, their principal basis being the treasury or the revenue of the government," the people "absolutely nothing directly," and the limits of the enemy's means "approximately known." This made war calculable and moderate, and it made sieges and manoeuvre more normal than battle. Then: "War had suddenly become again an affair of the people, and that of a people numbering thirty millions, every one of whom regarded himself as a citizen of the State." After that the means "had no longer any definite limits." Clausewitz's whole theoretical problem is that he has watched the ceiling come off, and he does not know whether it stays off. He declines to predict, and only says that "bounds, which to a certain extent existed only in an unconsciousness of what is possible, when once thrown down, are not easily built up again."

**The technology of the smoothbore and the marching column.** Combat is decided at short range; the commander can physically see part of his battle; movement is at the speed of feet and horses; supply is by magazine, requisition and living off the country. Maude's footnotes mark precisely where this dates: the superiority of the defensive "antedates by some years the introduction of long-range weapons," and "Railways, steamships, and telegraphs have... enormously modified the relative importance and practicability of surprise."

**Information is slow, scarce and corrupt.** No telegraph on the field. Reports arrive by rider, late and contradictory. This is not incidental colour; it is one of the four elements that make up friction.

**Psychologically, he assumes a man under fear and fatigue.** The commander is in physical danger, sleep-deprived, and subject to a stream of alarming reports. Half the book is about what that does to judgment.

**What this determines about transfer.** The *structural* claims survive the technology, because they are claims about any two-sided, reactive contest in which effort has a price and the object has a value: the reciprocal escalation, the subordination of means to object, the economics of effort against value, friction, the asymmetry between preserving and acquiring, the culminating point. The *material* claims do not: numbers of troops, subsistence by magazine, fortress sieges, cavalry, the tactical chapters of Books IV, V and VII. The book's own doctrine tells you to expect this, since it insists that events "must be judged of in connection with the peculiarities of the time."

## 3. Core doctrine

**1. War is an act of force to compel the opponent to our will; its immediate object is to disarm him.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §2–4.
*His reasoning:* he refuses abstract definitions and starts from a duel, two wrestlers. The ultimate object is submission of the enemy's will; since we cannot reach his will directly, the practical object becomes disarming him, because a disarmed enemy has no prospect of improvement and must yield.
*Limits he attaches:* the disarming of the enemy "is rarely attained in practice and is not a condition necessary to peace. Therefore it can in no wise be set up in theory as a law." He states the absolute and then immediately withdraws it.

**2. The abstract conception of war escalates to the extreme; reality damps it.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §3–10.
*His reasoning:* three reciprocal actions — in the use of force, in the aim to disarm, in the exertion of powers — each of which logically drives both sides to the maximum, because whatever one side omits becomes the other's licence to omit too.
*Limits:* he calls the pure result "a play of ideas produced by an almost invisible train of logical subtleties" and "a mere paper law." War is never isolated, never a single blow, and its results are never absolute; therefore "the probabilities of real life take the place of the conceptions of the extreme."

**3. Real war is a calculation of probabilities, and chance makes it a game.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §10, §19–22.
*His reasoning:* once the belligerents are actual states rather than concepts, each infers the other's intentions by the law of probability from what he is and does. Add chance — with which no human activity is in such constant contact — and war becomes "of all branches of human activity the most like a gambling game."
*Limits:* he insists this is not licence for recklessness. Theory "must accord a place to courage, to boldness, even to rashness," but the margin for the accidental is proportional to the courage and self-reliance available to fill it.

**4. The political object is the measure of both the military aim and the price.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §11; Book I ch. II; Book VIII ch. III.B.
*His reasoning:* the political object is the original motive, so it must be an essential factor in the product. "As War is no act of blind passion, but is dominated by the political object, therefore the value of that object determines the measure of the sacrifices by which it is to be purchased. This will be the case, not only as regards extent, but also as regards duration."
*Limits:* the political object is "no despotic lawgiver"; it must accommodate itself to the nature of the means, and it works only through the masses it has to move, so the same object produces different effects on different peoples and at different times.

**5. War is a continuation of political intercourse with a mixture of other means.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §24 (heading and text); Book VIII ch. VI.B; Notice of 1827.
*His reasoning:* political intercourse does not stop when war starts — it continues by another instrument. "Is not war merely another kind of writing and language for political thoughts? It has certainly a grammar of its own, but its logic is not peculiar to itself."
*Limits:* the political element "does not sink deep into the details of war. Vedettes are not planted, patrols do not make their rounds from political considerations." The principle governs plans, not tactics.

**6. The military point of view is subordinate to the political, not the reverse.**
*Locus:* Book VIII ch. VI.B.
*His reasoning:* policy declared the war; "it is the intelligent faculty, war only the instrument, and not the reverse." Therefore it is "an irrational proceeding" to ask soldiers for a purely military plan of a war.
*Limits:* he does not mean policy may ask the impossible. If policy demands what the instrument cannot do, the fault is in the policy, not in the interference — "It is not this influence but the policy itself which should be found fault with." And knowledge of war is *not* the chief qualification for a war minister; "elevation, superiority of mind, strength of character" are.

**7. War is a trinity of violence, chance, and reason.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. I §28.
*His reasoning:* war is "a wonderful trinity, composed of the original violence of its elements, hatred and animosity, which may be looked upon as blind instinct; of the play of probabilities and chance, which make it a free activity of the soul; and of the subordinate nature of a political instrument, by which it belongs purely to the reason." A theory that omits or fixes the ratio of any one of the three is destroyed by reality at once.
*Limits and a caution:* the people / army / government mapping appears in the *next* paragraph as an illustration of which social element each tendency principally concerns, not as the definition. See §6 below.

**8. Friction is what separates real war from war on paper.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. VII; Book I ch. VIII.
*His reasoning:* "Everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult." The army is not one piece but a mass of individuals each generating its own friction; danger and exertion multiply it; the weather and a thousand petty circumstances intervene. "Activity in War is movement in a resistant medium."
*Limits:* the general who is most impressed by friction is not the best general — that class of over-anxious commanders is common among the experienced. And the cure is expensive: "A powerful iron will overcomes this friction; it crushes the obstacles, but certainly the machine along with them." The only real lubricant is habituation to war, which no commander can grant his army at will.

**9. Most information in war is false, and fear multiplies the falsehood.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. VI.
*His reasoning:* "Great part of the information obtained in War is contradictory, a still greater part is false, and by far the greatest part is of a doubtful character." Worse than contradiction is mutual confirmation of error, which forces a resolution that turns out folly. "In a few words, most reports are false, and the timidity of men acts as a multiplier of lies and untruths."
*Limits:* his prescription is not stoicism but a deliberate correction of bias — the commander who is not naturally buoyant should "do violence to his own natural conviction by inclining from the side of fear to that of hope." He dismisses the textbook maxim of trusting only certain information as "miserable book comfort."

**10. Uncertainty is structural, not remediable.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. III; Book II ch. II §24.
*His reasoning:* "War is the province of uncertainty: three-fourths of those things upon which action in War must be calculated, are hidden more or less in the clouds of great uncertainty." And: "all action must, to a certain extent, be planned in a mere twilight, which in addition not unfrequently—like the effect of a fog or moonshine—gives to things exaggerated dimensions and an unnatural appearance."
*Limits:* what the feeble light leaves indistinct "talent must discover, or must be left to chance." He does not offer a method; he says there is none, and that this is why positive theory is impossible.

**11. Genius in war is coup d'œil plus resolution plus character.**
*Locus:* Book I ch. III.
*His reasoning:* two qualities are indispensable against the unexpected — an intellect that retains "some traces of inner light" in intense obscurity, and the courage to follow that faint light. *Coup d'œil* is not literal eyesight but "the rapid discovery of a truth which to the ordinary mind is either not visible at all or only becomes so after long examination." Resolution is courage before responsibility, an act of feeling awakened by the intellect. Character is constancy of conviction under a stream of contrary impressions, with the working maxim "in all doubtful cases to adhere to the first opinion, and not to give it up until a clear conviction forces us to do so."
*Limits:* he distinguishes character from obstinacy, and notes that men resolute in a subordinate rank often lose it in high command, because the dangers of a wrong decision become visible to them.

**12. Theory educates judgment; it does not supply rules for use.**
*Locus:* Book II ch. II §27–28; Book II ch. V.
*His reasoning:* theory's job is to separate what seems amalgamated, explain the properties of the means, show their probable effects. "It should educate the mind of the future leader in War, or rather guide him in his self-instruction, but not accompany him to the field of battle."
*Limits:* he permits maxims to crystallise if they arise naturally, but "not in order to form out of it an algebraical formula for use upon the battle-field." This principle is what makes every checklist extracted from this book a violation of it.

**13. Moral forces are the greater half of the account and resist measurement.**
*Locus:* Book III chs. III–V.
*His reasoning:* the physical and moral effects are "completely fused, and are not to be decomposed like a metal alloy by a chemical process." "The physical are almost no more than the wooden handle, whilst the moral are the noble metal, the real bright-polished weapon." The chief moral powers are the talents of the commander, the military virtue of the army, and its national feeling.
*Limits:* he admits they "will escape from all book-analysis," refuses to systematise them, and says he will "remain here more than usually incomplete and rhapsodical."

**14. The first principle of strategy is to be numerically superior at the decisive point.**
*Locus:* Book III ch. VIII.
*His reasoning:* strip the combat of everything modifiable and only numbers remain. "The direct result of this is, that the greatest possible number of troops should be brought into action at the decisive point." He tests this against history: Leuthen and Rosbach are the only modern European victories against double numbers; Frederick failed at Kollin with 30,000 against 50,000; Napoleon failed at Leipzig with 160,000 against 280,000. Hence "we find no example of a Marathon."
*Limits:* superiority in numbers is "only one of the factors"; to regard it as a necessary condition of victory "would be a complete misconception." Where absolute superiority is unattainable, the task is relative superiority at the decisive point — and that rests less on calculating time and space than on "the right appreciation of those points" and the resolution "to sacrifice the unimportant to the advantage of the important."

**15. Keep the forces concentrated; let no part be idle.**
*Locus:* Book III ch. XI; Book III ch. XIV.
*His reasoning:* "The best Strategy is always to be very strong, first generally then at the decisive point. Therefore... there is no more imperative and no simpler law for Strategy than to keep the forces concentrated. No portion is to be separated from the main body unless called away by some urgent necessity." Economy of forces is the same truth from a wider angle: troops that are idle while the enemy fights are worse than wasted, because they are "for the moment quite neutralised."
*Limits:* concentration can overshoot. Book VI ch. XXVII warns that force against a centre of gravity "may easily be greater than the resistance requires, and then there is a blow in the air, a waste of force."

**16. Boldness is a creative force, because it profits from the weakness of others.**
*Locus:* Book III ch. VI.
*His reasoning:* over and above the calculation of space, time and quantity, boldness earns "a certain percentage which boldness derives from the weakness of others." Against hesitation the probability is necessarily in its favour, because hesitation is already a loss of equilibrium. He claims that at equal discernment, "a thousand times as many" cases end in disaster through over-anxiety as through boldness.
*Limits:* boldness becomes rarer and more dangerous as rank rises, and at the top "individual boldness in action may easily become a fault"; it must be joined to a reflective mind. And it must never strike at obedience, "for there is nothing in War which is of greater importance than obedience."

**17. Surprise and stratagem promise far more than they deliver.**
*Locus:* Book III chs. IX–X.
*His reasoning:* surprise underlies all undertakings and has real moral effect, but "in idea it promises a great deal; in the execution it generally sticks fast by the friction of the whole machine." It is easier in tactics, where times and spaces are small, and nearly impossible at the level of policy, where preparations take months. Stratagem fares worse: strategy deals in the movement of real forces, not in words, and demonstrations large enough to deceive cost so much force that they risk the decisive point. "A correct and penetrating eye is a more necessary and more useful quality for a General than craftiness."
*Limits — and this is the crucial exception:* "the weaker the forces become which are under the command of Strategy, so much the more they become adapted for stratagem." See §4.

**18. Defence is the stronger form of war, but it has a negative object.**
*Locus:* Book VI ch. I; Book I ch. II.
*His reasoning:* the object of defence is to preserve, and "To preserve is easier than to acquire." All time not turned to account falls to the defender: "He reaps where he has not sowed." Add the use of ground, and "the defensive form of war is in itself stronger than the offensive." His proof is behavioural: nobody has ever attacked on one theatre with his weaker army and defended on another with his stronger, which shows what generals actually believe.
*Limits:* the negative object means you use it only "so long as our weakness compels us to do so," and give it up as soon as you are strong enough for the positive object. Defence is "no mere shield but a shield formed of blows delivered with skill." Pure passivity is not war. The natural course is to begin defensive and end offensive.

**19. The attack weakens as it advances, and has a culminating point.**
*Locus:* Book VII chs. IV–V; Book VII ch. XXII.
*His reasoning:* he lists seven causes of the attack's decay — occupation duties, protection of communications, losses and sickness, distance from depots, sieges, relaxation of effort, secession of allies — and in Book VII ch. XXII balances them against seven causes of increase. The assailant "buys up prospective advantages... but, in the meantime, he has to pay down on the spot for them a certain amount of his military force." Beyond the point where the balance turns, "there is a reaction; the violence of such a reaction is commonly much greater than the force of the blow. This we call the culminating point of the attack."
*Limits:* the point cannot be computed; it must be found "by the fine tact of judgment," and the commander must "guess" at half a dozen unknowables. Worse, the defensive you fall back into after an overrun is a weakened defensive — it keeps the ground but loses the prepared theatre, the support of the population, and much of the advantage of waiting. And the psychology runs against stopping: "like a horse drawing a load uphill, [the assailant] finds it less difficult to advance than to stop."

**20. Strike the centre of gravity; reduce the enemy's power to as few of them as possible.**
*Locus:* Book VI ch. XXVII; Book VIII ch. IV; Book VIII ch. IX.
*His reasoning:* borrowed from mechanics — a blow at the centre of gravity produces the greatest effect, and the most effective blow is struck with the centre of gravity of one's own power. "Out of them a certain centre of gravity, a centre of power and movement, will form itself, on which everything depends; and against this centre of gravity of the enemy, the concentrated blow of all the forces must be directed." He locates it case by case: in Alexander's, Gustavus's, Charles XII's and Frederick's armies; in the capital, in states torn by dissension; in the protecting ally, for small dependent states; in the unity of interests, in a confederacy; in the leader and public opinion, in a national insurrection. Book VIII ch. IX reduces the whole plan of war to two principles: "to act concentrated as much as possible" and "to act as swiftly as possible."
*Limits:* the reduction to a single centre is not always possible; where the enemies are genuinely independent, the war is really two wars and overthrow is generally out of the question. And the whole apparatus rests on "a supreme act of strategic judgment," not on a procedure.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

This text speaks to the weaker party **directly and at length** — more directly than its reputation suggests — and it also tells him, without softening, what he cannot have.

**What it says directly.**

*The weak win by duration and cost, not by decision.* Book I ch. II builds the case explicitly. Two things can substitute for the ability to continue the fight: "the improbability" and "the excessive price, of success." Since "the value of that object determines the measure of the sacrifices by which it is to be purchased," the weaker party's route is to raise the price above the value: "as soon... as the required outlay becomes so great that the political object is no longer equal in value, the object must be given up, and peace will be the result." He names the method — "the *wearing out* of the enemy," which he defines as "a gradual exhaustion of the physical powers and of the will by the long continuance of exertion" — and then states the conclusion flatly: "We see then that this class of means, the wearing out of the enemy, includes the great number of cases in which the weaker resists the stronger." His case study is Frederick in the Seven Years' War, who "was never strong enough to overthrow the Austrian monarchy; and if he had tried to do so after the fashion of Charles the Twelfth, he would inevitably have had to succumb himself."

*Take the smallest object you can.* The same passage: "if we want to overcome the enemy by the duration of the contest, we must content ourselves with as small objects as possible, for it is in the nature of the thing that a great end requires a greater expenditure of force than a small one." The limit case is "simple passive resistance," at which "our means attain their greatest relative value."

*Use the stronger form because you are weak.* Book VI ch. I says the defensive is the stronger form and that you use it "so long as our weakness compels us to do so." This is the weak actor's structural gift: the asymmetry between preserving and acquiring is free, and it accrues with time.

*Undertake nothing beyond your powers.* Book III ch. I praises Frederick not for brilliance but for proportion: "while pursuing a great object with very limited means, he undertook nothing beyond his powers, and *just enough* to gain his object." And the close of Book VII ch. XXII is the text's plainest sentence on the subject: "Only he that does great things with small means has made a successful hit."

*Stratagem is the last resource of the feeble.* Book III ch. X, after dismissing cunning for anyone with real forces: "to the quite feeble and little, for whom no prudence, no sagacity is any longer sufficient at the point where all art seems to forsake him, stratagem offers itself as a last resource. The more helpless his situation, the more everything presses towards one single, desperate blow, the more readily stratagem comes to the aid of his boldness." Note the register. He is not recommending cleverness; he is describing what is left when everything else is gone.

*People's war is dispersal, not confrontation.* Book VI ch. XXVI is a manual for the materially outmatched. Its operating rule: "National levies and armed peasantry cannot and should not be employed against the main body of the enemy's army, or even against any considerable corps of the same, they must not attempt to crack the nut, they must only gnaw on the surface and the borders." Its survival rule: such a force "should, like a kind of nebulous vapoury essence, never condense into a solid body; otherwise the enemy sends an adequate force against this core, crushes it." Its mechanism is attrition at the edges — the fire spreads "as it does in heather," reaches the enemy's lines of communication, and "preys upon the vital thread by which his existence is supported." He attaches five conditions: the war is fought in the heart of the country; it cannot be decided by a single catastrophe; the theatre is large; the national character suits it; and the ground is broken. He adds that "a poor population accustomed to hard work and privations usually shows itself more vigorous and better suited for war," and that people's war alone is a phantom — it must be combined with a regular army under one plan.

**What it says against the weak, directly.**

Book III ch. VIII is cold water. Superiority of numbers is "the most general principle of victory," the first rule is "to enter the field with an Army as strong as possible," and the historical record shows that "it is very difficult in the present state of Europe, for the most talented General to gain a victory over an enemy double his strength." Book I ch. II goes further: in pure theory "a War between two States of very unequal military strength would appear an absurdity; therefore impossible," and such wars occur only because real war diverges from the concept. And Book III ch. VIII ends by conceding that after doing everything possible, "a review of the total relations must then decide whether or not the combat is to be avoided for want of sufficient force."

**Where I am inferring rather than reporting.** Clausewitz never generalises any of this to an individual person; his weak actor is always a small state, a defending nation, or an outnumbered army. The application of "wearing out," "smallest object," and "the defensive form" to a single man with no resources is my extrapolation, made in §8 and labelled there. What is *not* extrapolation is the logic those principles rest on — that effort has a price, that the object has a value, and that a contest ends when one side's price exceeds its value. That logic is stated by Clausewitz in general terms and is indifferent to the size of the actor.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Book I ch. VIII. The only thing that reduces friction is habituation to war, and it cannot be granted at will; peacetime manoeuvres are a weak substitute but far better than "mere mechanical exercises of routine." His specific prescription is to build friction *into* the exercises, so that judgment and resolution are exercised, and so that "the soldier, high or low... should not have to encounter in War those things which, when seen for the first time, set him in astonishment and perplexity; if he has only met with them one single time before, even by that he is half acquainted with them." He extends this to physical fatigue, which should be practised "less to accustom the body to them than the mind." His second-best measure is importing men who have seen real war elsewhere.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Book I ch. VI. Deeply sceptical; see principle 9. The foundation of all our ideas and actions is untrustworthy, changeful, and multiplied by fear. There is no collection method offered, only a discipline of judgment: the law of probability as guide, a deliberate correction against the pessimistic bias, and firmness against the impression of the senses, which "is stronger than the force of the ideas resulting from methodical reflection."

**Timing.** Book I ch. I §13–19; Book III ch. XII; Book VII ch. V. The only motive that can suspend action is that one side "waits for a more favourable moment for action," which normally means the other side should strike now. Periods of inaction, he argues, are explained chiefly by the superiority of the defensive absorbing the impulse of opposed interests, and secondarily by imperfect knowledge. On the other side of the ledger, the culminating point is a timing problem the commander must guess at, and most generals err by stopping short while the enterprising err by going past.

**Terrain and position.** Book III ch. II lists geography as one of five elements of strategy but warns against analysing the elements separately. Book VI ch. I gives the defender "a preferential use" of ground; Book VII ch. XXII lists use of ground as the first of the four sources of defensive superiority and the only one that survives intact when the defensive is improvised on conquered territory. Book III ch. IV sets out "elective affinities": national spirit tells most in mountains, trained discipline in open country, the talent of a general in closely intersected, undulating country — because in mountains he has too little command over the parts and in plains the problem does not exceed his powers.

**Deception and information.** Book III chs. IX–X. See principle 17. His position is unusual among the classics and worth stating plainly: he thinks deception is overrated by writers and rarely productive in practice, because strategy moves real forces at real cost and cannot bluff cheaply. Words, declarations and false reports, which are the cheap instruments of deceit in ordinary life, have little purchase in the strategic field.

**Alliances.** Book VIII ch. VI.A is scathing and specific. No state takes up another's cause with the same earnestness as its own; the contingent is fixed in advance without regard to the scale of the war; the auxiliary commander answers to his own government. "The business is often settled like a commercial transaction; each, according to the amount of the risk he incurs or the advantage to be expected, takes shares in the concern to the extent of 30,000 or 40,000 men, and acts as if he could not lose more than the amount of his investment." Against that, Book VIII ch. IV notes that for a small state dependent on a greater one, the centre of gravity lies in the ally's army; and in a confederacy it lies "in the unity of interests," which is where to aim.

**Logistics and economy.** Book V ch. XIV ("Subsistence") and Book VII ch. IV. Subsistence became a commanding problem in modern war for two reasons: armies got larger and permanent, and wars became continuously connected rather than a sequence of separated enterprises, so armies must be ready at all times. He traces the burden to the state's assumption of responsibility once feudal obligation was commuted into money, making supply "an affair belonging to government." On the operational side, distance from depots is the fourth of his seven causes of the attack's decay.

**Morale and discipline.** Book III chs. III–V. See principle 13. Military virtue is distinguished from mere bravery and from enthusiasm; it is bravery disciplined into "obedience, order, rule, and method." The three chief moral powers are the commander's talents, the army's military virtue, and its national feeling, and he refuses to rank them: "The best plan is not to undervalue any of them."

**The decisive point.** Book III ch. VIII; Book VIII chs. IV and IX. Relative superiority at the decisive point is the whole of strategy's positive contribution, and identifying the point is harder than the arithmetic of concentrating on it. "The right appreciation of those points" is what he credits to Frederick and Napoleon, not a talent for calculating time and space, which he says is "neither the most difficult, nor the most decisive."

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Book VII chs. IV, V, XXII. See principle 19. The essential and least comfortable finding is that the moment of greatest danger is the moment the offensive ends: the defensive you inherit is weaker than the defensive you left, and the psychological momentum makes advancing feel easier than halting.

**When not to fight.** Addressed, but obliquely. Book III ch. VIII: after making your force as strong as possible, "a review of the total relations must then decide whether or not the combat is to be avoided for want of sufficient force." Book I ch. I §17: the weaker party who cannot dispense with the advantage of the defensive "must put up with the unfavourable prospects which the future holds out; for it may still be better to fight a defensive battle in the unpromising future than to assume the offensive or make peace at present." Book I ch. II: "passive expectation of the enemy's blow" is a legitimate means. What he does not offer is a rule of avoidance comparable to Sun Tzu's; his framing is always that not fighting is itself a strategic act with its own price.

**Defence versus offence.** Book VI ch. I; Book I ch. II. See principle 18. The fullest statement in the classical canon, and the one most often inverted by readers who remember Clausewitz as an apostle of attack.

**The character required of the commander.** Book I ch. III. Courage of two kinds — indifference to danger, which is steadier, and courage from positive motive, which carries further. Then intellect fine enough to find truth by tact of judgment; *coup d'œil*; resolution, which is courage before responsibility and requires intelligence, so that "men who have little intelligence can never be resolute"; presence of mind; strength of character, which is constancy of conviction, distinguished carefully from obstinacy. Book VIII ch. VI.B adds, for the man above the commander, that "elevation, superiority of mind, strength of character" matter more than technical knowledge of war.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The slogan.** The line everyone knows — "war is the continuation of politics by other means" — is a compression that loses two things. Clausewitz's developed statement, at Book VIII ch. VI.B, is: "war is nothing but a continuation of political intercourse, with a mixture of other means," and he immediately explains the qualifier: "We say, mixed with other means, in order thereby to maintain at the same time that this political intercourse does not cease by the war itself." The slogan's "by" replaces a mixture with a substitution, which is the opposite of his point. Second, the German *Politik* covers both "policy" (purposive, unilateral) and "politics" (contested, multilateral). Christopher Bassford has made this the centre of his argument that critics attack a rationalist Clausewitz who does not exist. It is worth noting that Graham's own §-heading at Book I ch. I §24 — "WAR IS A MERE CONTINUATION OF POLICY BY OTHER MEANS" — is itself the slogan form, so the flattening is partly an artefact of the first English translation.

**"The fog of war."** The phrase does not occur in Graham. Grepping the whole file for "fog" returns exactly three hits, and none of them is the slogan: literal weather in the friction chapter (Book I ch. VII, "the fog prevents the enemy from being discovered in time"); literal weather again in Book III ch. I §33, "Weather" ("it is mostly only by fogs that it plays a part"); and one simile, in Book II ch. II §24. What Clausewitz actually wrote is that action must be planned "in a mere twilight, which in addition not unfrequently—like the effect of a fog or moonshine—gives to things exaggerated dimensions and an unnatural appearance." Two things follow. The figure is *twilight*, and the pathology he names is not blindness but *distortion* — things look bigger and stranger than they are. That is a more specific and more useful claim than the slogan, and the slogan buries it. "Fog of war" as a fixed English phrase is a later coinage, not a translation of a phrase in this book.

**"No plan survives contact with the enemy."** Not Clausewitz. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, 1871, in the form that no operational plan can be relied on beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main body. Quote Investigator traces the misattribution to Clausewitz to Anthony Farrar-Hockley's *Airborne Carpet* (1969). It belongs in this section, not in §7.

**The trinity misread as people / army / government.** The most consequential modern misreading. Clausewitz's trinity, at Book I ch. I §28, consists of three *tendencies*: primordial violence and hatred; the play of probability and chance; and subordination as an instrument of policy. The words people, army and government appear in the *following* paragraph, where he says each tendency "concerns more" one of them. Col. Harry Summers's *On Strategy* (1981/1982) elevated the illustration into the doctrine and made "people, army, government" the American army's Clausewitz for a generation; Martin van Creveld then built the "non-trinitarian warfare" critique of *The Transformation of War* (1991) on that same substitution, attacking a proposition Clausewitz did not make. Edward Villacres and Christopher Bassford set this out in "Reclaiming the Clausewitzian Trinity," *Parameters* 25:1 (1995). Bassford notes that the PAG terms "appear nowhere at all in the paragraph that lists the Trinity's components." The error matters because it converts a descriptive model of three forces in any war into a prescriptive rule about keeping three state institutions in harmony.

**B. H. Liddell Hart.** The most famous hostile critic. In *The Ghost of Napoleon* (1933) he called Clausewitz "the Mahdi of mass and mutual massacre," held him responsible for the doctrine of absolute war and so for the frontal assaults of 1914–18, and argued that "Clausewitz's principle of force without limit and without calculation of cost fits, and is only fit for, a hate-maddened mob." The charge is weakest precisely where Liddell Hart pressed it hardest: "without calculation of cost" is the reverse of Book I ch. II, which makes the calculation of cost against the value of the object the governing rule.

**John Keegan.** In *A History of Warfare* (1993) Keegan argued that war is an expression of culture rather than of policy, and presented Clausewitz as a narrow Prussian whose politicisation of war corrupted the West. Bassford's rebuttal is that Keegan conflates *policy* with *politics*, assumes politics means rational action, and so misses that Clausewitz's model already contains the irrational and the accidental — the very material Keegan's cultural cases supply.

**Reading the unfinished as the settled.** The commonest scholarly failure mode, and the one this corpus file most needs to guard against. The 1827 Notice announces that the first six books must be reworked around the two kinds of war and the primacy of policy, and the undated memorandum says only Book I ch. I is finished. So passages in Books II–VII that read as though absolute war were the standard are, on Clausewitz's own account, unrevised material. The scholarship is genuinely divided on how far the revision got. Werner Hahlweg dated the undated memorandum to 1827 rather than 1830 (Howard and Paret's dating), which would give Clausewitz years rather than months to revise; Azar Gat has argued, against the mainstream, that the 1827 Note records an intellectual crisis rather than a maturing insight. The honest position for a book built on this text is that the *direction* of Clausewitz's final thought is documented in his own hand, and the *extent* of its execution in the surviving text is disputed.

**Business and self-help misuse.** Three specific corruptions recur. First, "centre of gravity" and "friction" are lifted as vocabulary while the two-sided, reactive structure that gives them meaning is dropped — Clausewitz's centre of gravity is the point where an *opposing will* coheres, not a company's core competence. Second, the culminating point is quoted as a caution about overextension while its actual content — that the defensive you fall back into is degraded, and that stopping feels harder than continuing — is omitted, which is the whole of its practical value. Third, and most directly self-contradicting: the book is repeatedly mined for rules, when Book II ch. II states that theory must not furnish "positive doctrines and systems which he can use like mental appliances," and must not "accompany him to the field of battle." A list of Clausewitz's rules for anything is a violation of the text it claims to summarise.

**Edition-specific cautions.** Maude's 1909 footnotes in this file are not Clausewitz and sometimes argue against him; they are marked (EDITOR) and should never be quoted as the author. Graham's Victorian diction ("Buonaparte," "Rosbach," "Leignitz") differs from modern spellings and from Howard/Paret's phrasing, so searches based on the modern translation will fail against this file.

**Marie's role.** Vanya Eftimova Bellinger, *Marie von Clausewitz: The Woman Behind the Making of On War* (Oxford, 2015), working from correspondence acquired by the Prussian Privy State Archives in December 2012, argues that Marie's part in the formation and publication of the book was substantially larger than the modest self-description in her own preface, and larger than historians had allowed. Treat "exactly in the condition in which they were found" as her claim rather than as an established fact about the text.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

This is the most dangerous text in the thirteen for this purpose, and the most useful, for the same reason: it is the only one that treats a contest as a two-sided reactive system with an economy attached. Everything that transfers comes from that economy. Everything that fails to transfer fails because a man's life is not a duel, and Clausewitz's definition of war is precisely a duel.

**What transfers.**

*The object governs the effort, and the value of the object caps the price.* This is Book I ch. II and it is the single most valuable idea here for a man with no reserves. A rich man can absorb an overrun; a man with nothing cannot, so for him the discipline of setting the object before setting the effort is not prudence but survival. The operational form is unglamorous: write down what the thing is for and what it is worth before starting, and treat the figure as binding. Clausewitz's rule is that when the outlay exceeds the value of the object, you give up the object. Most people cannot do this because the sunk cost has become the reason for continuing — which is exactly the pathology he describes at the culminating point, where the assailant "finds it less difficult to advance than to stop."

*Preserving is cheaper than acquiring, and time accrues to whoever holds.* "He reaps where he has not sowed." The man with nothing has no position to preserve, which is the whole of his problem, so the first move is to acquire a minimum position — income, housing, a skill that is already paid for — and then let the defensive form do its free work while the positive move is prepared. Clausewitz's sequence is explicit: begin with the defensive, end with the offensive, and give up the defensive "as soon as we feel strong enough to aim at the positive object." A man who tries to run the positive object first, from nothing, is choosing the weaker form for the harder task.

*Duration is the weapon of the weaker party.* "The wearing out of the enemy... includes the great number of cases in which the weaker resists the stronger." The mechanism transfers exactly, because it is an economic mechanism: in any sustained contest, the stronger party pays more per unit of time for the same object and will eventually find the price above the value. The practical translation is not "persist" as an attitude but "structure your position so that continuing is cheap for you and expensive for them" — low fixed costs, no debt, no dependence on a single counterparty, the capacity to still be there in year five. Frederick is the case, and Clausewitz's praise of him is instructive: not that he was brilliant, but that he "undertook nothing beyond his powers, and just enough to gain his object."

*Concentration, because dispersal is fatal to small forces.* "No portion is to be separated from the main body unless called away by some urgent necessity." With few resources the cost of running three projects is not a third each; it is the loss of the decisive weight on any of them. Clausewitz's companion rule — economy of forces — is that nothing may be idle, and he says explicitly that idle force is worse than force employed to no purpose, because it is "for the moment quite neutralised." Both rules point the same way for a man with nothing: one thing, at full weight, at the point that decides.

*The centre of gravity, as an instruction to stop accumulating unimportant wins.* Find the thing on which everything else in the situation depends and put the weight there, rather than "conquering one of the enemy's provinces, with little trouble and superior numbers, and preferring the more secure possession of this unimportant conquest to great results." The translation is unflattering: most of what a man does to feel he is progressing is the unimportant province. Certificates, follower counts, small optimisations. The question Clausewitz forces is which single point, if it moved, would move everything else.

*Friction, and the only known remedy.* "Everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult." The plan is not the work; the resistant medium is the work. The remedy in the text is not willpower — he warns that iron will crushes the obstacles "but certainly the machine along with them" — it is habituation, and specifically rehearsal that *includes* the friction, so that nothing is met for the first time when it matters. That is a direct, testable instruction: practise under the bad conditions, not the clean ones.

*Information discipline.* Most of what you are told is false, and fear multiplies it. Clausewitz's correction is asymmetric and worth copying exactly: because men lean toward the alarming report, deliberately lean the other way to stay level. This is not optimism; it is a bias correction applied by a man who knew he had the bias.

*Boldness, with his own caveat attached.* He claims boldness earns "a certain percentage... from the weakness of others," that hesitation is already a loss of equilibrium, and that failures from over-anxiety outnumber failures from boldness by a large factor. For a man with nothing, who has less to lose by acting than a man with a position, this is a real structural advantage and one of the few he has. His caveat is equally real: as responsibility rises, boldness must be joined to reflection or it becomes "a mere blind outburst of passion to no purpose."

**What does not transfer, and why.**

*The first principle of strategy is unavailable to him.* Clausewitz's central positive rule is to be numerically superior at the decisive point, and his evidence is that even Frederick and Napoleon usually lost when outnumbered two to one. He says in pure theory that a war between states of very unequal strength "would appear an absurdity." A man entering with nothing is in that position against nearly everyone. There is no way to read this text honestly and come away with a method for the outmatched to win by force. What is left to him is the residue: the defensive form, duration, raising the other side's price, and concentration of the little there is. That is a smaller inheritance than the self-help literature pretends, and it should be stated as smaller.

*The separation of policy from instrument.* Clausewitz assumes a cabinet that sets the object and an army that executes it, and he identifies incoherence — "confusion, unsteadiness of purpose, and inconsistency" — as what happens when the two are not aligned. A man alone is both. He has no external body holding him to a stated object, which means the condition Clausewitz treats as a failure state is his default. The only substitute is a written, dated, revisited statement of the object, and the honesty to notice when the effort has silently drifted away from it. Nothing in the book supplies that discipline; it assumes it.

*The enemy.* This is the point at which the book becomes actively harmful if applied literally. War is defined here as "an act of violence intended to compel our opponent to fulfil our will," and the whole architecture — destruction of forces, centre of gravity, the culminating point — presupposes an adversary whose defeat is the object. Most of a man's situation on entering the world is not adversarial and not zero-sum: employers, landlords, colleagues, institutions, and the people whose help he will need are not an opposing army, and a man who models them as one will read every situation wrongly and be correctly avoided. The parts of Clausewitz that transfer are the parts about *cost, time, and one's own limits*; the parts about the enemy transfer only into genuinely adversarial settings — litigation, a contested negotiation, a competitor with a fixed market to divide — and should be left there.

*The engagement as the means.* "Strategy is the employment of the battle to gain the end of the War." Remove the battle and the architecture has no means. Much of the book is about how to fight; a man with nothing should, most of the time, not be fighting. Book I ch. II's "passive expectation of the enemy's blow" and Book VI's defensive are the parts written for him; Books IV and VII largely are not.

*People's war.* The most seductive analogy and the one that fails hardest. Clausewitz's five conditions require a population, a large country, broken ground, a favourable national character, and combination with a regular army; and he says flatly that a people's war on its own is "a phantom." There is no one-man version. Anyone selling asymmetric insurgency as a career strategy is selling something this chapter does not contain.

*Stratagem, read correctly, is a warning.* It is true that Clausewitz says stratagem "offers itself as a last resource" to the feeble. It is also true that he classes it with the situation of a man "for whom no prudence, no sagacity is any longer sufficient at the point where all art seems to forsake him," and that his considered verdict is that "a correct and penetrating eye is a more necessary and more useful quality for a General than craftiness." The man with nothing who reaches for cleverness first is doing the thing this text names as the mark of helplessness. Cunning is what is left when judgment has run out, not a substitute for acquiring judgment.

*And the book itself refuses to be a manual.* "It should educate the mind of the future leader in War, or rather guide him in his self-instruction, but not accompany him to the field of battle." Any chapter of our book that turns Clausewitz into a procedure is contradicting him. What he offers is a set of relations to hold in the head — effort against value, preserving against acquiring, advance against exhaustion — that sharpen judgment without replacing it. That is less than a reader wants and more than most of the canon gives.
