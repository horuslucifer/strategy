# X1. The grammar of strategy

A shared vocabulary for a book built on thirteen texts written across roughly twenty-three centuries in nine languages. The texts do not agree, and some of the words this book uses did not exist when most of them were written. This file fixes the terms, says who coined them, and marks where the canon splits.

Every attribution below was checked against a text I opened: Giles's Sun Tzu, Shamasastry's Arthashastra, the Loeb Aeneas Tacticus, Vegetius in Latin (Books I–III) and in Clarke's 1767 English, Whitehorne's *Art of War* with Marriott's *Prince* and the *Discourses*, Graham's *On War*, Mahan, Corbett, Harris's *Book of Five Rings*, Phillips's *Roots of Strategy* (de Saxe and Frederick in English), Frederick's *Principes généraux* in French, and the French Vauban treatises. ## 1. The levels, and the older vocabulary that preceded them

The modern ladder runs: **policy** (what the state wants), **strategy** (how armed force is used to get it), **operations** (how campaigns link battles into a design), **tactics** (how a fight is conducted). This ladder is young. Only the bottom two rungs are ancient, and the word "strategy" in its current sense is younger than the American republic.

**The Greek.** In classical Greek the technical word for the science of war was not *strategia* but *taktikē*. Aeneas Tacticus, writing in the fourth century BC, defined tactics as ἐπιστήμη πολεμικῶν κινήσεων, "the science of military movements" — Aelian quotes it at *Tactica* III.4, and the 1928 Loeb editors reproduce it in their introduction, along with Aelian's remark (I.2) that Aeneas was the first writer after Homer to compose στρατηγικὰ βιβλία, which means not "books of strategy" but "generals' books": military manuals. Στρατηγία meant the office of a general, generalship, command — a job, not a level of war. The genres carried their own names: *taktika* for handling troops, *poliorkētika* for siegecraft, and the subscription of the sole authoritative manuscript of Aeneas reads Αἰνείου πολιορκητικά, "Aeneas on Siege Operations." A Greek or a Byzantine could write a whole book about what we would call strategy without having a word for it; the *Strategikon* is, in effect, "the general's handbook."

**The eighteenth-century coinage.** The modern sense enters the European vernaculars in the 1770s, and it enters through Byzantium. Paul-Gédéon Joly de Maizeroy (1719–1780) translated the *Taktika* of the Emperor Leo VI as *Institutions militaires de l'empereur Léon le philosophe* (Paris, 1771) and used the new abstraction in his commentary; his *Théorie de la guerre* (1777) carries in its own subtitle the phrase *démonstrations sur la stratégique*. Beatrice Heuser credits him with "the introduction of the term 'stratégie' to French and thus into the Western vernacular languages." The sources I could open disagree on the year — 1770, or the 1771 commentary, or a memoir read to the Académie in 1775; the parallel German coinage of *Strategie* is dated to a 1777 translation of Leo (Heuser 2010, 4–5). The safe statement, and the one this book will use: **the word is a product of the 1770s, it was coined by scholars reading a Byzantine manual, and Joly de Maizeroy is its father.**

**Bülow.** Adam Heinrich Dietrich von Bülow (1757–1807) gave the new word its first rigorous — and first ridiculous — definition in *Geist des neueren Kriegssystems* (Hamburg, 1799; revised 1805). Heuser quotes him: "The *science* of military movements of two armies at war outside our range of view, or, if you prefer, out of the range of the shot of the big guns etc. is strategy." Strategy is what happens beyond eyeshot, tactics what happens within it, and war reduces to angles drawn between an army's base and its objective. The 1911 *Britannica* is fair to the result: he tried to reduce eighteenth-century practice "to mathematical rules... manœuvring his armies *in vacuo*."

**Jomini and Clausewitz fix it.** Jomini defines the three working branches (*Art of War*, ch. I): "Strategy decides where to act; logistics brings the troops to this point; grand tactics decides the manner of execution and the employment of the troops." Clausewitz cuts differently and more durably (*On War* II.1): "tactics *is the theory of the use of military forces in combat*. Strategy *is the theory of the use of combats for the object of the War*." He is explicit that he is codifying usage, not inventing it: "This division into tactics and strategy is now in almost general use." Both men reject Bülow's geometry, and Clausewitz buries rival and target together, naming "the *Breadth of the Base*, in Bulow, and the *Position on Interior Lines*, in Jomini" as the two "fundamental principles of the earliest theories of strategy published in our times," neither of which has "in no instance shown themselves absolute and effective" (VI.30).

**The missing rung.** Neither man has an operational level. Clausewitz's "strategy" covers the campaign, Jomini's "grand tactics" the battlefield, and between them there is no separate stratum. The intermediate level is a twentieth-century Soviet addition — *operativnoe iskusstvo*, operational art — associated with A. A. Svechin, whose *Strategiia* was published in Moscow by Voennyi vestnik in 1926 (English trans., East View, 1992); Western doctrine absorbed it in the 1980s. When this book says "operational" it means the design that links engagements into a campaign, and the word should be read as a modern convenience imposed on texts that did not have it.

**Policy above strategy** is Clausewitz's contribution, treated under *war as a continuation of policy* below. It has one older cousin: Kautilya's Arthashastra is a book of statecraft in which war is one of six instruments, and its author never needed the distinction because he never separated them.

## 2. Ends, ways, means, and risk

The formulation taught in most staff colleges since the 1980s is **Strategy = Ends + Ways + Means**, with **Risk** the gap between what the means buy and what the ends demand. It is associated with Arthur F. Lykke Jr. of the US Army War College, whose three-legged stool — the seat tilting when one leg is short — became doctrinal shorthand. **UNVERIFIED as to locus:** I could not open Lykke's article ("Defining Military Strategy," *Military Review*, May 1989, commonly cited as 69, no. 5, 2–8); DTIC and the Army War College press both refused access. The critical literature is verifiable: Jeffrey W. Meiser, "Ends+Ways+Means=(Bad) Strategy," *Parameters* 46, no. 4 (2016), with a symposium reply by Gregory D. Miller, Chris Rogers, Francis J. H. Park, William F. Owen and Meiser, "On Strategy as Ends, Ways, and Means," *Parameters* 47, no. 1 (2017), doi 10.55540/0031-1723.2843; and Beatrice Heuser, "Ends, Ways, Means: Clausewitz and Other Prophets," *Military Strategy Magazine* (2012). The core objection is that the formula is a budgeting checklist, not a theory: it balances a column of resources against a column of aims and says nothing about the adversary, who is trying to unbalance both.

How the canon parses it:

**Sun Tzu** puts ends first and makes them economic. The end is an intact asset (III.1: "the best thing of all is to take the enemy's country whole and intact"); the means are finite and audited before commitment (I.3–14); risk is measured as duration, because "there is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare" (II.6). Ways are chosen to minimise the drawdown of means — hence deception, hence foraging on the enemy.

**Kautilya** has the most literal ends–ways–means machinery in the canon: seven means (*saptanga*), six ways (*sadgunya*), and a political end stated as the conqueror's expansion through the circle of states. Risk he treats as *calamity* — Book VIII enumerates the troubles that can strike each constituent, ranked by severity.

**Aeneas Tacticus** inverts the frame: the end is survival, the means are the citizens themselves, the risk is internal — the faction inside the walls, not the army outside.

**Vegetius** collapses ways into means: a properly selected, drilled and paid legion generates the ways automatically, and a state that neglects the means has no strategy, only hope.

**Maurice's *Strategikon*** treats means as irreplaceable and risk as the master variable: an empire that cannot buy a new army must not lose the one it has, so ways are chosen for cheapness — ambush, raiding, subversion — over decisiveness.

**Machiavelli** denies that means are fungible: money is not the sinews of war, good soldiers are (*Discourses* II.10) — a direct attack on the "means" column as accountants understand it.

**Vauban** is the canon's only pure means-and-ways specialist: given a fortress and a season he can tell you the days, men and shovels. The end is set by the king.

**De Saxe and Frederick** split precisely over ways. See §4.

**Clausewitz** rejects the formula's independence of variables: "the smaller our political object, the less value shall we set upon it and the more easily we shall be induced to abandon it" (quoted by Corbett from Book I). The object determines the means, the means react back on the object, and the whole hangs in the trinity. Risk is not a residual but the medium — friction, chance, and the enemy's reciprocal will.

**Mahan and Corbett** agree on means (a battle fleet) and split on ways (§4). Corbett adds the canon's sharpest correction to the formula: in limited war the end itself can be seized directly, and the enemy's armed force need not be the objective at all.

## 3. Glossary

Forty-five terms. Each gives a short definition, the text that originates or best defines it, and the locus, chapter-and-paragraph in the edition named.

**the object.** The political thing a war is for, as distinct from the military aim adopted to get it. Clausewitz insists the first governs: "the political view is the object, War is the means, and the means must always include the object in our conception" (*On War* I.1 §24). Corbett adds a third, the *ulterior object* — the territory, trade or expedition for whose sake sea command is wanted at all (*Some Principles*, Pt. II ch. I).

**the decisive point.** Any point whose possession "would exercise a marked influence either upon the result of the campaign or upon a single enterprise." Jomini, *Art of War*, Art. XIX; his fundamental maxim is "to throw by strategic movements the mass of an army, successively, upon the decisive points of a theater of war" (Art. XVII). He concedes the obvious objection himself: the difficulty is recognising them.

**centre of gravity.** "A certain centre of power and movement... on which everything depends," against which the concentrated blow is directed. Clausewitz develops it twice: as mechanics in VI.27, where finding the *centra gravitatis* is "a supreme act of strategic judgment," and as politics in VIII.4, where the centre lies in the army, the capital, the allied army, the unity of interests, or "in a national insurrection, in the person of the chief leader, and in public opinion."

**culminating point of the attack.** The moment at which the attacker's superiority is exhausted and the scale turns, after which "the violence of such a reaction is commonly much greater than the force of the blow." Clausewitz, *On War* VII.5: "everything then depends on discovering the culminating point by the fine tact of judgment."

**culminating point of victory.** The same idea applied to a won campaign: the point beyond which further success converts into loss. Clausewitz, *On War* VII.22; the chapter exists because commanders routinely pass the point "without being aware of it."

**friction.** The accumulation of small unforeseeable resistances that separates war on paper from war. Clausewitz, *On War* I.7: "Everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult" — friction is the only concept "which in a general way corresponds to that which distinguishes real War from War on paper."

**fog.** The uncertainty of all data in war. **Note the correction:** "the fog of war" is not a phrase of Clausewitz's. In Graham's translation "fog" appears twice — as literal weather among the causes of friction (I.7), and in a simile: action must be planned "in a mere twilight, which in addition not unfrequently — like the effect of a fog or moonshine — gives to things exaggerated dimensions and an unnatural appearance" (II.2 §24). The governing image is twilight; the popular phrase is a later compression.

**the trinity.** War as a composite of three tendencies — blind instinct (hatred and enmity), the play of chance and probability, and subordination to reason as a political instrument — belonging respectively to the people, the general and his army, and the government. Clausewitz, *On War* I.1 §28: "a wonderful trinity." Any theory leaving one out "would immediately become involved in such a contradiction with the reality, that it might be regarded as destroyed at once by that alone."

**economy of force.** Keeping every part of the force employed, idle troops being worse than misused ones. Clausewitz, *On War* III.14: "Whoever has forces where the enemy does not give them sufficient employment... he is a bad manager of his forces." Vegetius states the same idea negatively in the *Regulae*: "It is better to have several bodies of reserves than to extend your front too much" (III.26, Clarke).

**concentration.** Massing force at the point of decision, which Clausewitz makes the one simple law of strategy. *On War* III.11: "The best Strategy is *always to be very strong*, first generally then at the decisive point... there is no more imperative and no simpler law for Strategy than to *keep the forces concentrated*."

**interior and exterior lines.** Interior lines are those on which one force can concentrate against several separated enemies faster than they can combine; exterior lines are those of a force operating on both flanks of the enemy. Jomini, *Art of War*, Art. XXI: "*simple and interior lines enable a general to bring into action... upon the important point, a stronger force than the enemy*." Clausewitz concedes it "rests on a sound foundation" but calls it "nothing but another case of one-sided theory" (II.2 §11).

**the indirect approach.** Attacking along the line of least expectation rather than least resistance. **The phrase is B. H. Liddell Hart's, from *The Decisive Wars of History: A Study in Strategy* (London: G. Bell & Sons, 1929), later expanded as *Strategy*** — it is routinely misattributed to Sun Tzu, and the words "indirect approach" occur nowhere in Giles's Sun Tzu, in either the 1910 text or the commentary volume; I searched both. What Sun Tzu has is *ch'i*, which Giles renders "indirect," a narrower idea (next entry). **UNVERIFIED:** Liddell Hart's text was not available to me; the 1929 imprint was confirmed from Open Library's catalogue record.

**cheng and ch'i (orthodox and extraordinary).** The pair of forces in every engagement: *cheng* is the force the enemy is looking at, *ch'i* the force that arrives from where he is not looking. Sun Tzu V.3–6 (Giles): "In all fighting, the direct method may be used for joining battle, but indirect methods will be needed in order to secure victory." The commentators insist these are not fixed roles but a rotation — Ho Shih: "*cheng* may also be *ch'i*, and *ch'i* may also be *cheng*"; whichever the enemy is watching is by definition the orthodox one.

**shih (strategic configuration, potential, "energy").** The accumulated positional advantage that makes the eventual blow almost effortless. Giles translates it "energy" and gives Sun Tzu's own image at V.23: "the energy developed by good fighting men is as the momentum of a round stone rolled down a mountain thousands of feet in height" — the force is in the height and the roundness, not in the push.

**the void (weak points, *hsü*).** The place where the enemy is not. Sun Tzu VI, "Weak Points and Strong" in Giles; the rule is VI.30, "in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak," on the water analogy — water "runs away from high places and hastens downwards." Musashi's Void is a different concept and should never be conflated with it: the ground of unobstructed perception, "where there is nothing... not included in man's knowledge" (Harris trans.).

**mandala (the circle of states).** The geopolitical model in which your neighbour is your enemy and your neighbour's neighbour is your friend, extended outward through twelve kings. Kautilya, *Arthashastra* VI.2 (Shamasastry): the conqueror, his friend and his friend's friend form one circle, the enemy, the *madhyama* (mediatory) and *udasina* (neutral) kings three more — "four primary Circles of States, twelve kings, sixty elements of sovereignty, and seventy-two elements of states," with the conqueror as the nave and the other kings as spokes. His three modes of conflict sit inside the same scheme: open battle "in daylight and in some locality," treacherous fight (feints, bribery, striking a careless enemy) and silent battle by intrigue with the enemy's officers (VII.6), the last two preferred where circumstances allow (X.3).

**the six measures (sadgunya).** The six postures available to a state: peace (*sandhi*), war (*vigraha*), neutrality or staying put (*asana*), marching (*yana*), alliance or seeking shelter (*samsraya*), and the double policy of peace with one and war with another. Kautilya, *Arthashastra* VII.1: "The Circle of States is the source of the six-fold policy," and the choice among them is mechanical — "Whoever is inferior to another shall make peace with him; whoever is superior in power shall wage war."

**the seven constituents (saptanga).** The seven limbs of a state, each of which can be strong, weak or in calamity. Kautilya, *Arthashastra* VI.1: "The king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army and the friend are the elements of sovereignty." Power is broken into three kinds — intellectual (counsel), sovereign (treasury and army) and physical (valour) — at VI.2.

**command of the sea.** Not possession of the ocean but control of its communications. Corbett, *Some Principles*, Pt. II ch. I: "Command of the sea, therefore, means nothing but the control of maritime communications." His corrective: command is usually in dispute, since "the normal position is not a commanded sea, but an uncommanded sea." Mahan is more absolute — "that overbearing power on the sea which drives the enemy's flag from it" (*Influence of Sea Power*, ch. I).

**the fleet in being.** A fleet kept in active existence to deny the enemy command without risking a decision on his terms. Corbett, *Some Principles*, Pt. III ch. III.1, on Torrington in 1690: "A temporary defensive he considered was the only way to win the command, while to hazard a decision in inferior strength was the best way to lose it." Corbett insists on the active sense — "not merely in existence, but in active and vigorous life" — and says the phrase has been wrongly narrowed to anti-invasion defence.

**lines of communication.** The roads connecting an army to its base, which are simultaneously its supply arteries and its line of retreat. Clausewitz, *On War* V.16: "they form the connection between the army and its base, and are to be considered as so many great vital arteries... By their second purpose, that is as lines of retreat, they constitute in a real sense the strategic rear of the army." At sea the communications at stake are the nation's, not the fleet's (Corbett, Pt. II ch. I).

**the pré carré.** The doctrine of a rationalised, doubled belt of fortresses along a straightened frontier, replacing a scatter of isolated posts. Vauban's phrase, from a letter to Louvois of January 1673: "le roi devrait un peu songer à faire son pré carré. Cette confusion de places amies et ennemies ne me plaît point." **Quoted from the French Wikipedia article on the phrase, citing Bernard Pujo, *Vauban*, 63; I have not opened the letter.** The resulting northern frontier ran two lines deep, the first including Dunkirk, Lille and Valenciennes.

**the parallel.** A trench dug parallel to the defending wall, linking the zigzag approach saps and serving as a covered place of arms from which the next bound is launched. Three were standard; Vauban's French calls them *parallèles* or *places d'armes*, distinguishing the *première* from the *seconde* (Œuvres, *De l'attaque des places*). The 1911 *Britannica* dates the method to the siege of Maestricht (1673) and reports, with a hedge worth preserving, that it was "said to have been suggested by the practice of the Turks at Candia in 1668."

**siege by regular approaches.** Reducing a fortress on a calculable schedule by trench, parallel, battery and mine rather than by assault. The 1911 *Britannica* says the method "in principle remains to this day the standard method of attacking a fortress," and that Vauban's renown "rests far more" on the attack than on his systems of fortification. The caution belongs in the definition: Jamel Ostwald's *Vauban under Siege* (Brill, 2007), reviewed by Michael Wolfe in *H-France Review* 8 (2008), no. 10, argues from the archives of the War of the Spanish Succession that "few generals ever actually implemented what he so fervently advocated" — a doctrine more praised than executed, because martial vigour kept overriding engineering efficiency.

**magazines and depots.** Pre-stocked supply bases that free an army from living off the country and simultaneously chain it to a radius. Vegetius makes the strategic case: "Famine makes greater havoc in an army than the enemy, and is more terrible than the sword... The main and principal point in war is to secure plenty of provisions and to destroy the enemy by famine" (III.3, Clarke). Frederick builds his whole defensive scheme around them and states the corresponding doctrine in the *Principes généraux*: "Le plus grand art du général dans la défensive, c'est d'affamer son ennemi."

**cadenced step.** Marching in time to a beat, so that a column can change speed without concertinaing and arrive in order. Vegetius I.9 fixes the Roman rate: *militari gradu XX milia passuum horis quinque dumtaxat aestivis conficienda sunt. Pleno autem gradu, qui citatior est, totidem horis XXIIII milia peragenda sunt* — twenty Roman miles in five summer hours at the military step, twenty-four at the full step. De Saxe rediscovered it and thought it the whole secret: "Have them march in cadence. There is the whole secret, and it is the military step of the Romans" (*Reveries*, in Phillips, *Roots of Strategy*, 203).

**the legion.** A self-contained, permanently embodied, uniformly drilled formation of citizens, as against hired contingents of allies. Vegetius II.1–2 (Clarke): auxiliaries are "hired corps of foreigners... without knowledge of one another or any tie of affection," whereas "the peculiar strength of the Romans always consisted in the excellent organization of their legions"; he derives the name *ab eligendo*, from the care used in choosing the men. De Saxe's proposed "legion" is a conscious revival, and Phillips records the Prince de Ligne's remark that if a god inspired the legion, "a God inspired Vegetius."

**the oblique order.** Refusing one wing and massing the other against a single enemy flank, so that a part of your army defeats the whole of his. Vegetius has it as the second and third of his seven dispositions: "He who judges himself inferior should advance his right wing obliquely against the enemy's left" (III.26, Clarke). Frederick gives the modern rationale in the *Instructions*: "All weak armies attacking stronger ones should use the oblique order... in setting yourself to defeat a wing and in taking a whole army in the flank, the battle is settled at the start."

**defence as the stronger form.** The proposition that defence is intrinsically the stronger form of war, but with a negative object, so that it must be abandoned as soon as strength permits. Clausewitz, *On War* VI.1: "the defensive form of war is in itself stronger than the offensive... we must only make use of it so long as our weakness compels us to do so." The corollary is often forgotten: "it is therefore, also, the natural course in war to begin with the defensive, and to end with the offensive."

**fixed and mobile defence.** Defence anchored to positions and works, against defence that trades ground for the chance to counterstrike. Clausewitz's Book VI is the canonical treatment, and his Frederick of the last three Seven Years' War campaigns is the type case: a king "not thinking of an offensive," acting offensively only "as the best means of defending himself," a counterstroke against Austria implied at the bottom of the design (VI.1). The *Strategikon* is the canon's purest mobile-defence text; the Vauban belt the purest fixed one.

**limited and unlimited war.** Wars distinguished not by size but by whether the object is worth the whole war-power of either side. Corbett draws it out of Clausewitz's late chapters (*Some Principles*, Pt. I ch. III): "In the case of a limited object... the complete destruction of the enemy's armed force was beyond what was necessary. Clearly you could achieve your end if you could seize the object, and... could set up such a situation that it would cost the enemy more to turn you out than the object was worth to him." Jomini, he notes, reached almost the same place by the concrete route.

**absolute and real war.** Absolute war is the logical extreme towards which the concept tends; real war is what the "non-conducting medium" of interests, forces and circumstances permits. Clausewitz, *On War* VIII.2: the philosophical conception requires that "there can be no suspension in the Military Act, and peace cannot take place until one or other of the parties concerned is overthrown." The gap between that and the record is what Book VIII explains.

**war as a continuation of policy.** War is not merely a political act but a political instrument. Clausewitz, *On War* I.1 §24 (Graham): "War is not merely a political act, but also a real political instrument, a continuation of political commerce, a carrying out of the same by other means." It is usually quoted without the companion sentence that limits it: "the political object is no despotic lawgiver on that account; it must accommodate itself to the nature of the means."

**the coup d'œil.** Seeing at once, in the middle of obscurity, the truth others reach slowly or not at all. Clausewitz, *On War* I.3 pairs it with *resolution*, the courage to follow the faint light; stripped of the over-figurative, "it amounts simply to the rapid discovery of a truth which to the ordinary mind is either not visible at all or only becomes so after long examination and reflection."

**virtù and fortuna.** Fortune is the half of events outside our control; *virtù* is the capacity that prepares against her. Machiavelli, *Prince* XXV: "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but... she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less," followed by the flood image — fortune "shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her." *Virtù* is therefore dykes built in fair weather, not boldness in the storm.

**necessità (necessity).** The constraint that produces action where choice would produce sloth. Machiavelli, *Discourses* I.1: "men act either of necessity or from choice, and merit may seem greater where choice is more restricted," which is why a sterile site makes a better city — the inhabitants are "constrained to industry, and less corrupted by ease." Frederick states the operational form: the best battles are those in which the enemy is forced by necessity to fight (*Principes généraux*, art. XXVI).

**the citizen militia (one's own arms).** Troops raised from the political community they fight for, as opposed to mercenaries and auxiliaries. Machiavelli, *Prince* XII–XIII: mixed forces "are much better than mercenaries alone or auxiliaries alone, but much inferior to one's own forces," and Rome's decline "commenced only with the enlisting of the Goths." The corollary is *Discourses* II.10, "That contrary to the vulgar opinion, Money is not the sinews of War" — in every case he cites, "the victory remained with him who held the sinews of war to consist, not in money, but in good soldiers."

**tempo.** The rate at which decisions and actions are imposed relative to the enemy's ability to answer. No canonical text uses the word; the idea is carried by Sun Tzu's "Rapidity is the essence of war" (XI.19, Giles) and by Musashi's *timing*, the canon's nearest thing to an explicit theory of rate: "There is timing in everything. Timing in strategy cannot be mastered without a great deal of practice," including the enemy's collapse — "Everything can collapse... when their rhythm becomes deranged" (*Book of Five Rings*, Harris trans.)."

**initiative.** Holding the power to set the terms of the next engagement. Frederick states it as a rule of contrariety (*Principes généraux*, art. XXVI): "il faut toujours obliger l'ennemi à ce qui lui répugne... il faut que vous vouliez tout ce qu'il ne veut pas" — always compel the enemy to what he dislikes, because you must want everything he does not. Vegetius states the same rule first: "it is therefore a maxim never to do, or to omit doing, anything as a consequence of his actions, but to consult invariably your own interest only" (III.26, Clarke).

**surprise.** Gaining relative superiority by acting before the enemy has adjusted. Clausewitz, *On War* III.9, ranks it twice: "the means to the attainment of numerical superiority," and "also to be regarded as a substantive principle in itself, on account of its moral effect." Vegetius: "Novelty and surprise throw an enemy into consternation; but common incidents have no effect" (III.26).

**deception.** Managing the enemy's picture of you as a weapon in its own right. Sun Tzu I.18–25: "All warfare is based on deception" — seem unable when able, inactive when moving; irritate the choleric, flatter the arrogant, exhaust the resting. The list closes with the clause that makes the doctrine self-limiting: these devices "must not be divulged beforehand."

**security.** Denying the enemy surprise, and protecting the force from disruption you did not initiate. This is Aeneas Tacticus's whole subject: guards and patrols, panics, watchwords and countersigns (*On the Defence of Fortified Positions* XXII–XXVII). His watchword chapter (XXIV) is the canon's best short account of how a security procedure fails through ordinary human ambiguity — Ilium was nearly retaken because one party's countersign was Tyndaridae and the other's Dioscuri, two words for the same gods.

**unity of command.** One will directing one design. Vegetius states it classically while explaining why auxiliaries are inferior: "it is one of the most essential points in military undertakings that the whole army should be put in motion and governed by one and the same order" (II.2, Clarke). Sun Tzu's version is the general's independence from a distant sovereign (III.12–16; X.23).

**the principles of war.** A short codified list of maxims meant to organise instruction and judgement. The genre is ancient — Vegetius's *Regulae bellorum generales* (III.26) lists about thirty — but the modern canonical list is Fuller's, in "The Principles of War, with Reference to the Campaigns of 1914–1915," *Royal United Services Institution Journal* 61, no. 441 (Feb 1916), 1–40, and developed in *The Foundations of the Science of War* (London: Hutchinson, 1926). Taken into the British *Field Service Regulations* after the First World War, the lists multiplied: the British ten (selection and maintenance of the aim; maintenance of morale; offensive action; security; surprise; concentration of force; economy of effort; flexibility; cooperation; sustainability) and the American nine (objective, offensive, mass, economy of force, manoeuvre, unity of command, security, surprise, simplicity), plus restraint, perseverance and legitimacy in 2011. **Both lists are taken from the Wikipedia article "Principles of war," citing the 2011 British Defence Doctrine and Joint Publication 3-0; I could open neither doctrinal publication, both hosts having refused access.** The standing criticism is Clausewitz's, written before the lists existed: such attempts are progress "in their analytical part," but "in their synthetical part, in their precepts and rules, they are quite unserviceable," because "they strive after determinate quantities, whilst in War all is undetermined" (II.2 §12).

**the Way (heihō).** Strategy as a lifelong discipline of one person rather than a science of managing others. Musashi, *Book of Five Rings*, Ground Book, where the five books are named and the Void announced as "that which has no beginning and no end"; the treatise's characteristic move is to insist that what is true of one swordsman is true of ten thousand men.

## 4. Where they actually disagree

The canon is not a consensus with local variations. Four of its disagreements are load-bearing, and a book that papers over them produces advice that cancels itself.

**Mahan against Corbett: is the enemy's fleet always the objective?**

Mahan says yes, and states it as a conscious choice between two incompatible policies. Reviewing the Anglo-French wars he describes French practice as having "subordinated the control of the sea by the destruction of the enemy's fleets... to the success of particular operations, the retention of particular points, the carrying out of particular ulterior strategic ends," and delivers his verdict: "such a policy, however applicable as an exception, is faulty as a rule." The reader should at least "recognize that the two lines of policy, in direct contradiction to each other, do exist" (*Influence of Sea Power upon History*, in the chapters on the War of American Independence). His image is unambiguous: the enemy's navy "may be accurately called the army in the field," and islands are "but rich towns."

Corbett says no. "Seek out the enemy's fleet" fails when contact is not certain, because the search itself opens your own communications; he notes Mahan's later amendment, "Seek out the enemy's fleet, if you are sure of getting contact," and offers his own replacement: "Seek contact with the enemy's fleet in the most certain and favourable manner that is open to you" — of which seeking it out "is only one way of doing this, and not always the best way" (*Some Principles*, Appendix, the "Green Pamphlet," RN War College, January 1909). Behind the tactical argument sits the theoretical one: since command "means nothing but the control of maritime communications," the fleet is a means, not an end, and in limited war the ulterior object can sometimes be seized directly (Pt. I ch. III; Pt. II ch. I).

The disagreement is narrower than it looks and sharper where it is real. Both want a superior battle fleet; both accept a decision on favourable terms. They divide on the default. Mahan's is to go and find the fleet; Corbett's is to sit on the communications and make it come to you — "It is far better to make it come to you, and this has often been done by merely sitting on the common communications."

**Clausewitz against Sun Tzu: can a war be won without blood?**

Sun Tzu: "to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting" (III.2, Giles), the argument being economic — a shattered state is worth less than an intact one (III.1).

Clausewitz denies not the possibility but the prudence of building a theory on it, in language chosen to give no ground: "philanthropists may easily imagine there is a skilful method of disarming and overcoming an enemy without great bloodshed... still it is an error which must be extirpated; for in such dangerous things as War, the errors which proceed from a spirit of benevolence are the worst" (I.1 §3). The reason is reciprocal, not moral: "he who uses force unsparingly, without reference to the bloodshed involved, must obtain a superiority if his adversary uses less vigour in its application." He returns to it in Book IV: "Let us not hear of Generals who conquer without bloodshed... not for making the sword we wear blunter and blunter by degrees from feelings of humanity, until some one steps in with one that is sharp and lops off the arm from our body" (IV.11).

They are not quite arguing about the same thing. Sun Tzu describes an ideal outcome and the methods that raise its probability; Clausewitz describes the danger of adopting that ideal as a planning assumption against an opponent who has not. The book should say so rather than choose. But the practical divergence is real: Sun Tzu's doctrine licenses a general to avoid battle indefinitely, and Clausewitz's does not.

**Machiavelli against Vegetius: cavalry.**

Vegetius ends his *Regulae* by declining to write about cavalry at all, because it has improved past the ancients: "this branch of the service has been brought to perfection since the ancient writers... nothing can be collected from their works. Our present mode of discipline is sufficient" (III.26, Clarke). From a writer whose every other sentence tells the moderns to imitate the ancients, this is the one place he treats his own age as the advanced party.

Machiavelli reverses it. Book II of the *Art of War* (Whitehorne's 1560 English) asks "What order, or what vertue maketh, that footemen overcum horsemen" and answers physically: horses cannot go everywhere infantry can; they are "slower then the footemen to obeie, when it is requisite to alter the order"; disordered horse cannot re-form under violence as foot can; and above all "a horse is a beaste, that hath sence, and knoweth the perilles, and with an ill will, will enter in them" — against the pike, the spur driving him forward is weaker than the point holding him back. His test: "prove to run a horse against a walle: you shall finde fewe, with what so ever furie he come withall, will strike against it." The conclusion is flat: "a bande of footemen to bee moste safe, ye, invinsible for horses."

Machiavelli does not name Vegetius here, and the honest statement is that they diverge on where an army's strength lies, not that one answers the other. But Vegetius is the source Machiavelli works from everywhere else, which is what makes the divergence worth marking: the pupil takes the master's method — imitate the Roman infantry — and uses it to overturn the master's one modern concession.

**De Saxe against Frederick: should you give battle?**

De Saxe: "I do not favor pitched battles, especially at the beginning of a war, and I am convinced that a skillful general could make war all his life without being forced into one... Frequent small engagements will dissipate the enemy until he is forced to hide from you." He guards the claim at once — an opportunity to crush the enemy should not be refused — but restates it as the summit of the art: "war can be made without leaving anything to chance. And this is the highest point of perfection and skill in a general" (*Reveries*, in Phillips, *Roots of Strategy*, 298).

Frederick opens his article on battle with the opposite premise: "Les batailles décident du sort des États" — battles decide the fate of states (*Principes généraux*, art. XXVI; Foster's 1797 English numbers it XXIII). War produces problems manoeuvre cannot close: "il faut bien en venir à des moments décisifs... ou pour terminer des querelles qui ne finiraient jamais." He is not reckless — no battle "sans un but important," and he confesses in the same article that of five battles his troops had fought only three were premeditated, the other two forced on him by his own errors, offered "pour que mes officiers profitent de mes fautes." But he closes the door de Saxe leaves open. He grants de Saxe's whole case for the defensive — "Le plus grand art du général dans la défensive, c'est d'affamer son ennemi; c'est un moyen où, sans rien hasarder, il y a tout à gagner" — then refuses the conclusion, because starvation does not end wars: "comme l'enlèvement d'un convoi ou la perte d'un magasin ne finit pas la guerre, et qu'il faut des batailles pour décider, il est nécessaire d'employer l'un et l'autre de ces moyens pour réussir."

That last sentence is the exact point of division, and it is worth quoting in full in any chapter that touches the question, because it shows a first-rate commander conceding the opponent's premise and rejecting his conclusion on a single evidentiary ground: manoeuvre exhausts, but it does not conclude.

**Two smaller splits worth marking.** *Clausewitz against Jomini on interior lines:* Jomini makes the position on interior lines a fundamental principle (Art. XXI); Clausewitz calls it "nothing but another case of one-sided theory which can never gain ascendency in the real world" (II.2 §11) and reports that neither Bülow's base nor Jomini's interior lines "have in no instance shown themselves absolute and effective" when tested against the defence of a theatre of war (VI.30). The dispute is whether geometry is a principle or a convenience. *Kautilya against most of the canon on treachery:* where Vegetius, Frederick and Clausewitz treat subversion as an auxiliary, Kautilya ranks *kutayuddha* above open battle when circumstances permit and builds a bureaucracy of spies to supply it (*Arthashastra* VII.6, X.3). He is closer to the *Strategikon* — which spreads false rumours and writes letters designed to fall into enemy hands — than to anyone in the European line.
