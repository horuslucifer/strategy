# X5. How the canon is misread

## 0. What this file is for, and how it was checked

The thirteen texts of the foundation list were written for commanders, kings, magistrates and admirals. This book is for a man with nothing. That gap is the central technical problem of the project, not a detail to paper over with analogy. This file catalogues the ways it has already been bridged badly, so the book's own bridges can be built in the open.

Method. Every quotation was checked against a text I opened: the cached Giles *Sun Tzu* (1910), Shamasastry's *Arthashastra*, Marriott's *Prince* and Thomson's *Discourses* with their Italian originals, Graham's *On War* (1873), the Latin *Epitoma rei militaris*, the Loeb *Aeneas Tacticus*, Harris's *Book of Five Rings*, Mahan and Corbett. The material in §5 was verified against publisher pages, arXiv, Crossref and encyclopaedia articles I opened; where I confirmed a citation but not its findings, the finding is marked **UNVERIFIED** and no number given. Nine items are flagged, eight marked inline; §6 lists them.

The test applied throughout is narrow: does the appropriation make a claim the source text contradicts on the page? Where it does, I quote both. Where it is thin rather than wrong, I say so.

## 1. The genre

### 1.1 What the appropriations have in common

Four moves recur, whichever text is mined.

**Extraction of the aphorism from its cost argument.** Each of these texts is structurally an accounting document. Sun Tzu's Chapter II prices an army of a hundred thousand and states what fraction of state revenue a campaign dissipates; Vegetius's rules come after two books on recruitment and drill; Kautilya's sixfold policy sits inside fifteen books of tax administration. The appropriations keep the conclusions and drop the ledger, and what survives is a set of maxims that appear to be free — the exact reading the source texts were written to prevent.

**Substitution of a symmetrical rival for an asymmetric one.** The canonical author faces an enemy with comparable means and an explicit intention to destroy him; the reader faces a colleague, a counterparty, a market, or an indifferent hiring process. The doctrines are relation-specific and the relation is not the same.

**Promotion of the reader to the command seat.** Every appropriation seats the individual in a chair built for someone holding an army, without asking whether he has one. §2.7 documents this.

**Loss of the negative case.** All of these texts contain extensive material on when not to act, and the appropriations reliably drop it. §3 collects it.

### 1.2 Sun Tzu for executives

The business appropriation of Sun Tzu is the oldest and largest. The Wikipedia article on *The Art of War*, which I opened, records that "Entrepreneurs and corporate executives have turned to it for inspiration and advice on how to succeed in competitive business situations," that some Japanese corporations have made it required reading, that Greene's *The 48 Laws of Power* extensively quotes it, and the popular-culture vector from Gordon Gekko in *Wall Street* (1987) to Scolari sliding passages under his players' doors in 2002. I did not open the executive titles (Krause, McNeilly and the rest) and make no claims about their arguments — **UNVERIFIED**.

It keeps deception, speed, knowing the enemy, the hierarchy of attack. It drops the cost frame: Chapter II establishes that war is ruinous and the first question is whether to fight at all, and Ts'ao Kung's note at its head states this without ornament — "He who wishes to fight must first count the cost."

What survives translation into business is "all warfare is based on deception" (I.18), treated as the thesis. It is one line of a book whose own ranking of methods puts fighting last:

> "to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting." (III.2)

> "the highest form of generalship is to baulk the enemy's plans; the next best is to prevent the junction of the enemy's forces; the next in order is to attack the enemy's army in the field; and the worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities." (III.3)

A doctrine that ranks physical engagement last on cost grounds — III.4–7 prices the siege at six months of preparation and a third of the men — is not a doctrine of aggression but of avoidance for the strong. Sun Tzu also caps the deception doctrine himself at I.25: the devices "must not be divulged beforehand." Deception is a function of asymmetric knowledge, and a book of deceptions read by everyone in an industry is not a weapon but a shared vocabulary.

The second dropped element is the epistemic honesty of Chapter XIII. Foreknowledge, Sun Tzu writes, "cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation" (XIII.5); it "can only be obtained from other men" (XIII.6). The book's central input cannot be derived from the book. It has to be bought, at cost, from people — an unglamorous conclusion that does not survive transfer into management prose.

### 1.3 Musashi for traders

Musashi's *Gorin no Sho* has been read as a manual of market timing and solitary discipline. The vector is real; the specific trading titles I did not open — **UNVERIFIED**.

The hook is a single sentence. In the Fire Book's section on timing, Musashi writes (Harris's translation): "there is timing in the Way of the merchant, in the rise and fall of capital." Read alone it looks like an invitation; read in place it is the opposite. The Ground Book sets out four Ways — farmer, merchant, warrior, artisan — as distinct occupations, and defines the merchant's as living by taking profit. Musashi is not collapsing the warrior's Way into the merchant's; he is using the merchant as one instance in a list of domains that exhibit rhythm, alongside dancing, music, bow and gun. The line illustrates a general claim about timing, and is not a bridge between two trades the same book has just separated.

It keeps solitude, discipline, timing, the undefeated duellist, the *Dokkōdō*'s renunciations. It drops that this is a craft manual with a stated scope: the analogy for strategy is the carpenter — plans, materials, foremen, timber graded by quality and position — and the claim for the warrior is "to master the virtue of his weapons." The instruction most hostile to the trading reading is in the Water Book: "You should not have a favourite weapon." Over-attachment to one instrument is the named failure mode, so a trader reading Musashi as licence for a signature strategy has read him backwards.

The structural problem is worse than the textual one. Musashi's authority is an unreplicated sample of one man's survival in more than sixty duels — the shape of evidence §5 shows cannot support inference. We have no books by the men who used an identical method and lost the fourth.

### 1.4 Machiavelli as a manipulation manual

The popular Machiavelli is a manipulation manual whose motto is "the ends justify the means." Both halves are wrong, and the second is checkable to the letter.

**The phrase is not in *The Prince*.** I searched Marriott's translation (Gutenberg 1232) for "ends justify," "end justifies," "justify the means" and "justifies the means." No matches.

**The real passage is Prince XVIII**, and it says something different. Marriott:

> "in the actions of all men, and especially of princes, which it is not prudent to challenge, one judges by the result.
>
> For that reason, let a prince have the credit of conquering and holding his state, the means will always be considered honest, and he will be praised by everybody; because the vulgar are always taken by what a thing seems to be and by what comes of it; and in the world there are only the vulgar."

The Italian, checked against the cached Wikisource text of chapter XVIII: "e nelle azioni di tutti gli uomini, e massime de' Principi, dove non è giudizio a chi reclamare, si guarda al fine. Facci adunque un Principe conto di vivere e mantenere lo Stato; i mezzi saranno sempre giudicati onorevoli, e da ciascuno lodati."

The claim is descriptive, not normative. *Si guarda al fine* — one looks to the outcome — reports how the public judges where there is no court of appeal ("dove non è giudizio a chi reclamare"), and the sequel insults the judges: they are taken by appearances and by what comes of things, and the world holds nothing but them. Machiavelli is not authorising the prince's means; he is saying the audience is too stupid and too distant to audit them. That is an observation about information asymmetry in sixteenth-century Italian politics, and a *perishable* one — its truth depends on the means being unobservable, which is the first thing §4 takes away. Marriott also inflates: the Italian reads "let a prince then reckon on living and maintaining the state."

**Where the idea does appear — the *Discourses* — it has been mistranslated into the slogan.** Thomson's English at I.9, on Romulus killing Remus:

> "For although the act condemn the doer, the end may justify him; and when, as in the case of Romulus, the end is good, it will always excuse the means."

The Italian, checked against the cached Wikisource text of *Discorsi* I.9: "Conviene bene, che, accusandolo il fatto, lo effetto lo scusi; e quando sia buono, come quello di Romolo, sempre lo scuserà."

Literally: though the deed accuse him, let the result excuse *him*; and when the result is good, as Romulus's was, it will always excuse *him*. The object of *scuserà* is *lo*, the man. There is no word for "means" in the sentence. The slogan enters English here as a translator's amplification of a sentence about excusing a *person*, and is then read back into *The Prince*, where it was never present at all.

**The phrase has an older and stranger pedigree.** *Exitus acta probat* — the outcome proves the deed — is Ovid, *Heroides* 2.85, checked on the Latin Library text. Phyllis, abandoned by Demophoon, imagines the world excusing him and immediately curses the principle: *careat successibus, opto*, may he be denied success. The proverb enters the tradition as a betrayed woman's sarcasm about how men get away with things, fifteen centuries before Machiavelli, and is attributed to him because of a translator.

The appropriation keeps the lion and the fox, the preference for being feared, the injunction to appear virtuous. It drops Machiavelli's subject. *The Prince* is about the acquisition and retention of states; its operational core is chapters XII–XIV, and the conclusion there is not about deception at all:

> "no principality is secure without having its own forces; on the contrary, it is entirely dependent on good fortune, not having the valour which in adversity would defend it. And it has always been the opinion and judgment of wise men that nothing can be so uncertain or unstable as fame or power not founded on its own strength." (XIII)

That is the Machiavellian doctrine most useful to a man with nothing, and the one the manipulation reading never reaches: borrowed capability is worthless, build your own. It is also the doctrine most hostile to that reading, manipulation being a way of operating with forces that are not your own. Beside it stands the part of *The Prince* that the cult of agency deletes, chapter XXV: "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less."

### 1.5 Robert Greene

Greene is the most consequential node in this genre and a legitimate target, since he is explicit about his sources: the Wikipedia article on *The Art of War* records that *The 48 Laws of Power* extensively quotes it. Publication is verified — Viking, 1998, 452 pages, over 1.2 million US copies, 24 translations — as is *The 33 Strategies of War* (Penguin, 2006, 496 pages), described as applying military-historical strategy to "social conflicts such as family quarrels and business negotiations." I opened neither book, so I quote neither, and the individual law titles are **UNVERIFIED** here.

The reception identifies the method problem precisely. *Kirkus* called the book "simply nonsense," on the ground that Greene supplies no evidence and that the laws contradict each other; Jeffrey Pfeffer of Stanford, unsentimental about organisational power himself, said they are "based on isolated examples, and not on solid research"; Jerry Adler in *Newsweek* wrote that Greene "has actually produced one of the best arguments since the New Testament for humility and obscurity." All three as reported in the Wikipedia article I opened, not in the originals.

Two criticisms matter independent of anyone's opinion of Greene. *Mutual contradiction*: a set of laws invocable in opposite directions after the fact is not a decision procedure but a vocabulary for narrating decisions already made, and a book of strategy is at constant risk of this. The remedy is to state, for each principle, the conditions under which it reverses. *Isolated examples*: Pfeffer's objection is the formal version of §5's whole argument. Reasoning from a curated set of historical winners is sampling on the dependent variable, and Denrell (below) shows what that produces — a systematic bias towards risky, concentrated, uninformed practices.

### 1.6 Clausewitz in management

The influence is real. The Wikipedia article on Clausewitz, which I opened, records that the idea of inherent "friction" distorting all prior arrangements "has become common currency in fields such as business strategy and sport," and that "Some theorists of management look to Clausewitz — just as some look to Sun Tzu — to bolster ideas on the concept of leadership." I could not confirm the BCG volume *Clausewitz on Strategy* — **UNVERIFIED**.

It keeps friction, "the fog of war," the centre of gravity, the culminating point, "war is the continuation of politics by other means." What it drops is the sharpest single finding in this file — *the qualification standing three sections later in the same chapter.*

Graham's Book I, chapter I, §3, "Utmost Use of Force," is the passage business writers quote:

> "he who uses force unsparingly, without reference to the bloodshed involved, must obtain a superiority if his adversary uses less vigour in its application. The former then dictates the law to the latter, and both proceed to extremities... to introduce into the philosophy of War itself a principle of moderation would be an absurdity."

Book I, chapter I, §6, "Modification in the Reality," three sections later:

> "If, adhering closely to the absolute... we insist with logical strictness that in every case the extreme must be the object, and the utmost effort must be exerted in that direction, such a stroke of the pen would be a mere paper law, not by any means adapted to the real world... the human mind would hardly submit itself to this kind of logical chimera. There would be in many cases an unnecessary waste of power..."

The escalation argument is a limit case Clausewitz builds in order to demolish it. §3 is the thesis, §6 the antithesis, the rest of the chapter the synthesis, ending at §24 with war as an instrument of policy. Quoting §3 as advice is not quoting out of context in the loose sense; it is quoting the first move of a dialectic as its conclusion, from inside the one chapter Clausewitz said was finished — a fact in his own hand: "The first chapter of the first book is the only one which I consider as completed." And from the notice of 10 July 1827:

> "Should the work be interrupted by my death, then what is found can only be called a mass of conceptions not brought into form; but as these are open to endless misconceptions, they will doubtless give rise to a number of crude criticisms."

He predicted the misreading in the preface of the book that was misread. Liddell Hart's later charge, as the Wikipedia article reports it, is the same diagnosis: the disciples "grasped at his vivid leading phrases, seeing only their surface meaning, and missing the deeper current of his thought."

Two smaller corrections, since this project should not launder clichés either. The phrase "the fog of war" does not appear in Graham's 1873 English; I searched it, and "fog" occurs twice, neither time as that phrase. The nearest passages are Book I, chapter III ("three-fourths of those things upon which action in War must be calculated, are hidden more or less in the clouds of great uncertainty") and Book II, chapter II, where action is planned "in a mere twilight, which... like the effect of a fog or moonshine — gives to things exaggerated dimensions." Whether the German has *Nebel des Krieges* I did not check; no German text is cached — **UNVERIFIED**. And the trinity is commonly misstated as "people / army / government," words not in his definition: Graham's trinity is violence and hatred, the play of probabilities and chance, and subordination to policy, which Clausewitz then *associates* with those three bodies. The association is his, the identification is not.

## 2. Category errors

Six errors, stated as errors rather than vices, because each has a corrective in the texts.

### 2.1 Treating other people as enemies by default

The canonical texts define the enemy institutionally, not psychologically. For Sun Tzu he is another state in a system of a dozen states. For Kautilya he is a structural position in the Circle of States: the adjacent king is the enemy by geometry, the king beyond him the ally by the same geometry. For Aeneas he is an army outside the wall plus a faction inside it. The relation is defined before any individual occupies it.

The appropriation inverts this, taking "enemy" as a stance the reader adopts towards whoever is in front of him. That is a different concept, and it fails in a way the source texts diagnose: Sun Tzu's method requires the enemy to be enumerable and measurable — the five constants and seven comparisons of I.3–14 are a survey instrument — and a reader who designates an indefinite, shifting population as enemies cannot execute the first step. Kautilya VII.2 supplies the price: war's standing disadvantages are "loss of power and wealth, sojourning, and sin," three of which are incurred by hostility itself, before any fighting.

### 2.2 Confusing tactics with strategy

The canon is consistent that the ranking of methods is a cost ranking and that individual encounters are the cheapest to win and the least decisive. Sun Tzu III.3 puts the enemy's plans first and his walls last. Corbett, two thousand years later, makes the same move about scale: "Since men live upon the land and not upon the sea, great issues between nations at war have always been decided — except in the rarest cases — either by what your army can do against your enemy's territory and national life or else by the fear of what the fleet makes it possible for your army to do." The theatre you are good at may not be the theatre in which the question is settled. Translated: excellence in the encounters you are currently having may be irrelevant to the decision that governs your position.

Mahan makes the same case at the largest scale, and it is the canon's strongest argument against reading strategy as personal conduct. His explanation of sea power is six conditions — geographical position; physical conformation, with natural productions and climate; extent of territory; number of population; character of the people; character of the government — of which four are inherited and cannot be acted on by any individual. The book is largely an argument that outcomes are set by structure. It is quoted by people who want to believe they are set by will.

### 2.3 Confusing aggression with initiative

Initiative in these texts means *choosing the terms of the encounter*, which often means declining it. Sun Tzu:

> "The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy.
>
> To secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself." (IV.1–2)

Vegetius at III.26 is blunter: *Aut inopia aut superuentibus aut terrore melius est hostem domare quam proelio, in quo amplius solet fortuna potestatis habere quam uirtus* — better to subdue the enemy by want, surprise or terror than by battle, in which fortune usually has more power than valour. And *Occasio in bello amplius solet iuuare quam uirtus* — opportunity usually helps more than valour. A doctrine ranking opportunity above valour, and fortune above both inside a battle, is not a doctrine of aggression.

### 2.4–2.6 The three slogan readings

Treated in full at §1.2, §1.4 and §1.6. The residue worth carrying forward is the general form of the third: **mistaking a limit case for a recommendation.** Sun Tzu's III.8–10 force ratios are as idealised as Clausewitz's absolute war, and Chang Yu notes they hold only when other factors are equal. Taking the clean model as the instruction is taking the scaffolding for the building.

### 2.7 The structural point: every canonical author writes for someone who already commands

Not a seventh misreading but the condition producing the others.

- **Sun Tzu** writes for a general holding delegated command at a distance from the sovereign: III.12–16 catalogues the three ways a ruler ruins an army by interfering, X.23 asserts the general's right to disobey. The doctrine presupposes an institution and a chain of command.
- **Kautilya** writes for a king. Book I, chapter XIX divides the king's day into eight day- and eight night-periods, each with an administrative function. The advice requires a treasury, a bureaucracy and an espionage service.
- **Aeneas Tacticus** writes for the magistrates of a besieged city, and the material is a state-security apparatus: informer bounties displayed in the market-place ("For whoever points out anyone conspiring against the city... a reward in money shall be announced"), a ban on private dining except at weddings and funerals with notice to the authorities, a rule that a soothsayer may not sacrifice without a magistrate present, and the removal of popular rivals by invented embassies — Dionysius disposing of his brother Leptines by sending him to Himera and declining to recall him (Loeb X). None of it is available to a private man; all of it requires a state.
- **Vegetius** writes to an emperor about recruiting, drilling and organising legions. His famous line is a policy recommendation to a government: *Igitur qui desiderat pacem, praeparet bellum* (Book III, preface).
- **Machiavelli** writes *The Prince* for a Medici and the *Art of War* as a garden dialogue about how a republic should raise infantry. **Clausewitz** writes for the Prussian officer corps and the state employing it; war is defined at Book I, chapter I, §24 as an instrument of *policy*, a thing only a government has. **Mahan and Corbett** write for naval establishments and the legislatures funding them.

Every transfer to the life of a man with nothing must answer one question in the open: *what plays the role of the army?* If nothing does, the doctrine does not transfer and must be cut rather than softened. Where something does — time, attention, a skill, a reputation, a little money, a body — the transfer must specify what the substitution costs. Machiavelli's Prince XIII states the problem: a power not founded on its own strength is the most unstable thing there is.

## 3. What the canon actually says about restraint, cost, and when not to act

Collected with loci. This is what the appropriations discard, and it is the majority of the useful content.

**Sun Tzu**

- II.6: "There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare." II.19: make victory the object, not lengthy campaigns.
- III.2–4: supreme excellence is winning without fighting; "the rule is, not to besiege walled cities if it can possibly be avoided." III.10: "if quite unequal in every way, we can flee from him."
- IV.1–2: make yourself unbeatable first; the opening is the enemy's to give.
- XII.17: "Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical."
- XII.18: "No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique."
- XII.20–22: "Anger may in time change to gladness; vexation may be succeeded by content. But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution."

XII.20–21 is the most transferable sentence in the book and the least quoted: emotions reverse, destructions do not, so reversible and irreversible decisions should be made by different procedures.

**Kautilya**

- VII.2, the opening sentence of the chapter: "WHEN the advantages derivable from peace and war are of equal character, one should prefer peace; for disadvantages, such as the loss of power and wealth, sojourning, and sin, are ever-attending upon war."
- VII.3: "Whoever goes to wage war with a superior king will be reduced to the same condition as that of a foot-soldier opposing an elephant." And: "Just as the collision of an unbaked mud-vessel with a similar vessel is destructive to both, so war with an equal king brings ruin to both." And: "When, either in peace or war, a king finds neither loss to his enemy nor gain to himself, he should, though superior, observe neutrality."
- VII.1: the king who can neither harass his enemy's works nor defend his own "shall seek protection from a king of superior power," working from deterioration to stagnation to progress.

The tie-break rule — when the calculation is even, take peace, because war carries standing costs the calculation has not priced — is the canon's cleanest decision rule.

**Vegetius**, Book III, chapter 26, the general rules:

- *Numquam ad certamen publicum produxeris militem, nisi cum eum uideris sperare uictoriam* — never bring a soldier to a general engagement unless you see that he expects to win. And: *Namquam* [sic, for *Numquam*] *miles in acie producendus est, cuius antea experimenta non ceperis* — never put a soldier in the line whom you have not tested.
- *Difficile uincitur qui uere potest de suis et de aduersarii copiis iudicare* — he is hard to beat who can judge truly of his own forces and his adversary's.
- *Qui frumentum necessariaque non praeparat, uincitur sine ferro* — he who does not lay in supplies is beaten without a sword. *Exercitus labore proficit, otio consenescit* — an army improves by work and grows old in idleness. *Amplius prodest locus saepe quam uirtus* — position often helps more than valour.

This is the canon's most compressed statement of the logic: prepare, test, measure honestly, supply yourself, keep working, prefer position to courage.

**Machiavelli**

- Prince XIII: no principality is secure without its own forces. Prince XXV: fortune is the arbiter of half our actions, "or perhaps a little less," and in the river image the flood cannot be resisted when it comes, though dykes and channels can be built in fair weather — fortune "shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her." A claim about *when* effort pays: in the interval before the crisis, not during it.

**Clausewitz**

- I.i.§6: insisting the extreme must always be the object is "a mere paper law," producing "an unnecessary waste of power." I.i.§24: war is an instrument of policy, not an independent good.
- I.i.§20–21: war is "of all branches of human activity the most like a gambling game." I.iii: three-fourths of what action must be calculated on lies in "the clouds of great uncertainty." I.vii: "Everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult."

**Corbett**: naval pressure alone works only by exhaustion, its effects "must always be slow, and so galling both to our own commercial community and to neutrals, that the tendency is always to accept terms of peace that are far from conclusive." A slow, indirect method that grinds down those applying it is a real category, and the one most individual "strategies" belong to. **Musashi**: "You should not have a favourite weapon."

The pattern: these were professionals writing for professionals, and professionals are cautious. The reckless reading is an amateur's.

## 4. The moral problem, stated

Much of this canon is amoral counsel for rulers. That is not an insinuation; it is on the page.

**Kautilya is the extreme case, and should be quoted rather than alluded to.** Book V, chapter I, "Concerning the awards of punishments," is an operations manual for secretly killing a king's own inconvenient ministers. A spy incites the minister's brother to claim an inheritance; a *tíkshna* — a "fiery spy," defined at I.XI as a desperado who fights elephants and tigers for money — murders the claimant at the minister's door, and the killing is attributed to the minister, who is punished for it. Or: send the minister against a rebellious tribe with an inadequate escort and have spies disguised as robbers kill him, reporting that he fell in battle.

Book XIV, "Secret means," opens "IN order to protect the institution of the four castes, such measures as are treated of in secret science shall be applied against the wicked," then runs through poison recipes, arson by spies disguised as night-walkers, and blinding smokes, with dosages by body weight: "As much as a *kalá* to men; twice as much to mules and horses; and four times as much to elephants and camels."

**Aeneas is the domestic case**: informer bounties, bans on private assembly, a popular brother disposed of by a fake embassy. **Machiavelli's** notoriety is partly earned: Prince XVIII defends breaking faith when the reasons that produced the promise have lapsed, and Marriott's edition records Burd's note that it "has given greater offence than any other portion of Machiavelli's writings."

A book for one man must decide what it takes and refuses. The options, laid out rather than recommended:

**A: take the analysis, refuse the methods.** Keep the structural instruments — the survey of one's own and the opponent's position (Sun Tzu I.3–14), the sixfold policy as a decision frame, the cost accounting, Vegetius's rules, Clausewitz on friction and chance — and refuse the operational material requiring deception or injury. Coherent, because the apparatus does not depend on the methods: Sun Tzu's Chapter I works whether or not you ever execute Chapter XIII.

**B: take everything and accept the costs**, which are the subject of the argument below. Also coherent, and honest.

**C: take the methods only against declared, symmetrical adversaries.** Closest to what the texts do, since all operate inside a declared war between defined parties. Its weakness is that the reader makes the declaration unilaterally, and §2.1 shows how easily it spreads.

**The practical argument, which is not a moral one.** Set aside whether Kautilya's methods are wrong. They were designed for an actor satisfying three conditions a private man does not.

*The king does not go on living among his targets as an equal.* He operates from above a hierarchy holding the power of life and death. A private man's counterparties are his neighbours, his industry, his future employers and friends. He will meet them again, and they will meet each other.

*The king controls the record.* The force of Prince XVIII depends on "dove non è giudizio a chi reclamare" and on a public judging by appearances because it cannot see the means. That condition has largely been destroyed for private individuals: means are now recorded, forwarded, searchable, durable. The Machiavellian bet is a bet on unobservability, and the odds have moved.

*The king's game has a different repetition structure.* A strategy's payoff depends on whether the interaction recurs and whether the counterparty can learn. In a one-shot interaction with a stranger who cannot report you, defection pays; in a repeated one, or a network where reports circulate, it pays once and then stops, because counterparties condition on your history. A king facing a rival kingdom plays something near the first game; a man building a career plays the second. The asymmetry is Sun Tzu XII.20–21 again: the gain from one defection is bounded and reversible, the loss of a reputation neither.

None of this shows the methods are wrong. It shows they are *mispriced* in transfer, moved from a low-repetition, low-observability setting into a high-repetition, high-observability one without a price tag. A reader who wants to defect anyway should at least do the arithmetic Chapter II would have insisted on.

There is also a textual point for restraint that does not depend on ethics. Kautilya, who supplies the poison recipes, also supplies the tie-break rule at VII.2: when the gains from peace and war are equal, take peace. The man who wrote the canon's most amoral counsel also wrote its most explicit instruction to prefer the cheaper option, and both come from the same habit of costing everything.

## 5. Empirical humility

The question is narrow: is there evidence that deliberately "strategic" behaviour by individuals improves outcomes, and what tells against it?

### 5.1 What supports the practice

**Planning that specifies conditions and responses.** The best-established finding concerns implementation intentions — if-then plans of the form "when situation X arises, I will perform response Y," introduced by Peter Gollwitzer in 1999 and distinguished from general goal intentions by being specific and procedural. The meta-analysis is Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119, doi 10.1016/s0065-2601(06)38002-1 — citation confirmed via Crossref, paper not opened, so no effect size is stated — **UNVERIFIED**. The specific-plan effect is real and heavily replicated, and the direction of the finding is that specificity helps; the magnitude I have not verified and will not assert. For firms the parallel is Miller & Cardinal (1994), *Academy of Management Journal* 37, 1649–1665, doi 10.2307/256804 — citation confirmed, findings not — **UNVERIFIED**, cited so the gap is visible rather than filled from memory.

**Practice.** The encyclopaedia article on practice as a learning method, which I opened, reports that "a meta-analysis found the correlation coefficient between deliberate practice and performance was 0.40," and quotes Ericsson disavowing the 10,000-hour rule on the ground that mere hours of engaging in an activity benefit performance much less than deliberate practice does. The counterweight is Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, "Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A Meta-Analysis," *Psychological Science* (2014), corrigendum 2018, doi 10.1177/0956797618769891 — citation confirmed via Crossref. Its domain-by-domain variance figures are widely quoted; I could not open the paper and therefore give none — **UNVERIFIED**.

### 5.2 What undercuts it

**The advice literature is built on survivors.** The cleanest statement is Denrell, J. (2003), "Vicarious Learning, Undersampling of Failure, and the Myths of Management," *Organization Science* 14(3), 227–243 (Crossref DOI 10.1287/orsc.14.2.227.15164, whose string reads 14.2). From the abstract, which I read in full:

> "The organizations that can be observed at any point in time are the survivors of a selective process that has eliminated a large fraction of the underlying population... the available sample of organizations usually undersamples failure... risky practices, even if they are unrelated to performance in the full population of organizations, may seem to be positively related to performance in a sample of survivors... Observations of existing organizations will show that unreliable, uninformed practices and practices that involve concentrated resource allocation are superior to reliable, informed practices."

That is the formal proof of what this file is about. Denrell's result is not that success literature is unreliable in general but that it is biased *in a specific direction*: towards risk, concentration and acting without information — exactly what the aggressive misreadings recommend, and exactly what the sources themselves (Sun Tzu's calculation, Vegetius's testing and supply, Kautilya's costing) do not. The article on survivorship bias, which I opened, adds David McRaney's line that the "advice business is a monopoly run by survivors," Abraham Wald's wartime analysis of returning aircraft, and Taleb's term for the missing data in *The Black Swan*: "silent evidence."

**The business-strategy literature specifically.** Phil Rosenzweig, *The Halo Effect*, Free Press, 2007, argues that much business writing is "cargo cult science" and targets *In Search of Excellence*, *Built to Last* and *Good to Great* on methodological grounds: examining only successful companies, confusing correlation with causation, treating sustained advantage as reproducible by formula. (As given in the encyclopaedia article I opened; the book itself I did not.)

**Luck is a larger term than intuition allows.** Robert H. Frank, *Success and Luck: Good Fortune and the Myth of Meritocracy*, Princeton University Press, 2016, argues that "trivial initial advantages often translate into much larger ones" in winner-take-all markets, and that "for every big winner, there are scores of people who are as skilled, hard-working and intelligent, but came in just behind." (From the publisher's description, which I opened.)

The modelling case is Pluchino, Biondo & Rapisarda, "Talent vs Luck: the role of randomness in success and failure" (arXiv:1802.07068, 2018). From the abstract, read in full: talent is distributed normally while wealth follows a power law, and that mismatch between a scaled input and a scale-invariant output implies a hidden ingredient, which their agent-based model identifies as randomness. Their result: "if it is true that some degree of talent is necessary to be successful in life, almost never the most talented people reach the highest peaks of success, being overtaken by mediocre but sensibly luckier individuals." It is a simulation, not a measurement of the world, and should be cited as such.

**Base rates move more than behaviour does.** Chetty, Hendren, Grusky, Hell, Manduca & Narang, "The Fading American Dream," *Science* 356(6336), 2017, 398–406. From the summary I opened: roughly 90% of children born in 1940 earned more than their parents at age 30; for the 1980 cohort, about 50%. The decline runs across the income distribution, sharpest in the middle class and the industrial Midwest, and is driven mostly by the unequal distribution of growth rather than slower aggregate growth — restoring 1940s-level mobility would require GDP growth above 6% a year under current distribution patterns. A forty-point swing in the probability of out-earning your parents, set by the birth year on your certificate, is larger than any individual strategy documented anywhere in this file.

**Confidence does not track accuracy.** Tversky and Kahneman's illusion of validity (1973, "On the Psychology of Prediction"): confidence tracks how well the predicted outcome fits the input, "with little or no regard for the factors that limit predictive accuracy." Kahneman's own case is the Israeli army's Leaderless Group Challenge, where his team's officer forecasts "were better than blind guesses, but not by much," and — the part that matters here — "The dismal truth about the quality of our predictions had no effect whatsoever on how we evaluated new candidates and very little effect on the confidence we had in our judgments and predictions." (As given in the encyclopaedia article I opened.)

**Grit.** Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017), "Much ado about grit," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 113(3), 492–511, doi 10.1037/pspp0000102 — citation confirmed via Crossref. Drawing on 88 independent samples and over 66,000 people, they found grit "only moderately correlated with performance," "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness," and that only one of its two components, perseverance of effort, explains variance in academic performance once conscientiousness is accounted for. (Details as given in the encyclopaedia article I opened.)

### 5.3 What this licenses

Nothing in §5.2 shows that deliberate strategy is useless. It shows four narrower things, each of which the canon already knew: that the visible evidence for any strategy comes from survivors and is biased towards risk and concentration (Denrell); that structural position — birth cohort, geography, distribution regime — moves outcomes more than individual method does (Chetty, and Mahan's six conditions at the level of states); that confidence in a plan is not evidence for it (Kahneman; Clausewitz's three-fourths in the clouds); and that outcome is a corrupted signal of decision quality because chance intervenes (Pluchino; Clausewitz's "most like a gambling game"; Vegetius's *fortuna potestatis habere*; Machiavelli's half, or a little less).

The position that follows is neither fatalism nor bravado. Strategy improves the *distribution* of outcomes without determining any particular one; the improvement is smaller than the literature selling it implies; and its largest contribution is avoiding ruin rather than producing success — which is what Sun Tzu IV.1, Kautilya VII.2, Vegetius III.26 and Machiavelli XXV all say in their different vocabularies. Spend more planning on irreversibility than on advantage.

## 7. Rules this file generates for the book

1. **Name the army.** No doctrine transfers until the chapter says what, in this man's life, occupies the place the army held in the source. If nothing does, cut it.
2. **Carry the cost argument with the maxim.** A principle imported without the accounting that produced it is not the principle.
3. **State the reversal condition.** A rule that cannot be stated with the conditions under which it reverses is a vocabulary item, not a rule — the Kirkus objection to Greene, taken seriously. Where a source builds an extreme to demolish it, quote both moves or neither.
4. **Weight irreversibility above advantage**, and say what is inherited. Sun Tzu XII.20–21 governs the first; Chetty's numbers are the receipt for the second. A book promising that method will outrun a birth cohort is lying.
5. **No quotation without a source opened**, including the slogans everyone knows. §1.4 is the standing demonstration of why.
