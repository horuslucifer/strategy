# 2. Kautilya (Chanakya / Vishnugupta) — Arthashastra

## 1. Identity

The Arthashastra is a manual of statecraft: how to acquire a state, hold it, extract revenue from it, staff it, police it, and use it against other states. It is not a war book. War occupies roughly Books VII and IX–XIII; the largest single book (II) is an administrative handbook covering mining, weights and measures, shipping, brothels, slaughterhouses, weaving and the superintendency of horses. The work opens by defining its own subject: "The subsistence of mankind is termed artha, wealth; the earth which contains mankind is also termed artha, wealth; that science which treats of the means of acquiring and maintaining the earth is the Arthasástra, Science of Polity" (Book XV, Chapter I). It is addressed to a king, and more precisely to a king's principal minister — the voice is that of a professional adviser who has done the job.

The text survives complete in fifteen books and one hundred and fifty chapters. I verified this against the cached file directly: fifteen book separators, and the final colophon reads "This is the one hundred and fiftieth chapter from the first chapter of the entire work." The reference literature also counts 180 numbered topics (*prakaraṇas*) running consecutively across the books; I did not independently verify that count.

**Translation used.** R. Shamasastry, *Kautilya's Arthashastra* (Bangalore: Government Press, 1915), complete, public domain, cached locally as `sources/arthashastra_shamasastry.txt` (940 KB, scraped from Wikisource with book separators and chapter headings intact). Every quotation in Section 7 was grepped out of that file. Two cautions about this cache: the Book VI, Chapter II colophon misprints "the seventh Book" where it means the sixth, and the Book VII, Chapter XII colophon misprints "Book VIII" where it means Book VII. Both are errors in the source, not in my reading. Also, the Wikisource presentation merges Book I Chapters I and II under a single heading printed as "CHAPTER I & II". The heading carries both numbers and the next heading is "CHAPTER III", so the numbering is *not* short: Book I still runs to Chapter XXI and the file contains 149 `^CHAPTER` headings for 150 chapters. (An earlier draft of this file said the numbering ran one short. That was wrong and is corrected here.)

Shamasastry's English is Edwardian, sometimes opaque, and has been superseded. R. P. Kangle's critical edition, translation and study (University of Bombay, 3 vols.; the critical edition 1960, the textual study 1965, the translation volume dated 1969 in the Wikipedia article's bibliography and 1963 elsewhere — I have not resolved that) and Patrick Olivelle's *King, Governance, and Law in Ancient India: Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra* (Oxford University Press, 2013) are the scholarly standards; L. N. Rangarajan's Penguin edition (1992) rearranges the material topically and is the most readable but is an editorial reconstruction rather than a translation of the text in its transmitted order. I worked from Shamasastry because it is what exists in the public domain and in the cache; where a phrase is strange, that strangeness is his.

**Rediscovery.** The text was considered lost by colonial-era scholarship. In 1905 a palm-leaf manuscript in Grantha script was presented to the newly opened Mysore Oriental Library, then headed by B. Lewis Rice, by a Tamil Brahmin from Thanjavur (Tanjore); the librarian R. Shamasastry identified it as the Arthashastra. Shamasastry published English instalments in the *Indian Antiquary* and *Mysore Review* during 1905–09, the Sanskrit edition in 1909, and the complete English translation in 1915. (Details and dates from the Wikipedia *Arthashastra* article, which cites Olivelle 2013 pp. 1–2 and Trautmann 1971 p. 1; the surviving manuscript is dated to about the sixteenth century.)

**Corrections to the blog's commentary.**

The blog says Kautilya lived "c.350–c.275 B.C." and was "the key advisor to the Indian King Chandragupta Maurya." The evidence says this is the tradition, not the finding. The identification of the text's author with Chanakya, the Mauryan minister, has been rejected by most of the main modern studies, because the received text is stylistically composite and contains material that cannot be Mauryan. The qualifier matters: McClish (2009) names three "major recent studies" of the composition — Kangle (1965), Scharfe (1968), Trautmann (1971) — and the first of those defends the traditional attribution. So the accurate formulation is that the attribution is rejected by the majority, not by all, of the serious literature. Thomas Trautmann's statistical study (*Kauṭilya and the Arthaśāstra: A Statistical Investigation of the Authorship and Evolution of the Text*, 1971) found multiple authorial hands and, at p. 176, "provisionally" proposed 250 CE as the date of the compilation. Hartmut Scharfe (1968) proposed c. 150 CE. Olivelle (2013) identifies three layers — older sources (c. 150 BCE–50 CE), a "Kauṭilya Recension" (c. 50–125 CE), and a later śāstric redaction (c. 175–300 CE). I checked these layer dates against two accounts: the Wikipedia article's summary citing Olivelle 2013 pp. 7–8, and the passage it quotes from Upinder Singh, *Political Violence in Ancient India* (Harvard, 2017), p. 98 — "the first major redaction was composed between circa 50 and 125 CE, and the second one between circa 175 and 300 CE." Mark McClish (*The History of the Arthaśāstra*, 2019) argues for an anonymous *Daṇḍanīti* redacted later by someone using the name Kautilya. Kangle (1965) defended the traditional attribution and the Mauryan date, and is the serious minority position. The honest formulation is the one in the task brief: traditionally 4th–3rd century BC, with the received text likely compiled and redacted into the early centuries AD. I take Olivelle's layer dates from the standard reference literature; I did not open Olivelle.

The blog dates Chandragupta Maurya to "c. 317–293 B.C.E." The most commonly printed range is c. 321–297 BCE (World History Encyclopedia gives "c. 321 – c. 297 BCE"; the Wikipedia infobox, citing Britannica and Upinder Singh 2016, gives c. 320–297). The scholarly spread is wider than either: Habib and Jha (2004) c. 322–298; Upinder Singh (2016) 324 or 321 to 297; Wiley (2009) 320–293; Jansari (2023), following Cribb, puts the accession as late as c. 311–305. Against that spread the blog's end-date of 293 sits at the late edge of an attested position (Wiley; Roy dates the death between 297 and 293), but its start-date of 317 does not correspond to any accession date I found — 317 is Jansari's date for Chandragupta's *arrival in the Punjab*, not for his taking the throne. My earlier formulation, that the blog's figures were simply outside the usual band, was too strong and is corrected here.

The blog says the Arthashastra was "written long before the birth of Christ" and that "its philosophy makes Machiavelli's The Prince look harmless." Both halves of that sentence are Max Weber's, from *Politik als Beruf* (1919), unattributed. I verified the German original on Wikisource: "Der wirklich radikale 'Macchiavellismus' im populären Sinn dieses Wortes ist in der indischen Literatur im Kautaliya Arthasastra, (lange vorchristlich, angeblich aus Tschandva-guptas Zeit), klassisch vertreten; dagegen ist Macchiavellis 'Principe' harmlos." *Lange vorchristlich* is "long pre-Christian"; *angeblich* is "allegedly." Weber hedged the date the blog states flatly, and the dating is exactly the point the modern scholarship contests.

The blog says "Similar to Thomas Hobbes, Kautilya believed the goal of his political science was power." Half right, half wrong. The Hobbes parallel is real and textually locatable: Kautilya's *matsyanyāya*, the law of fishes, is a state-of-nature argument — "in the absence of a magistrate (dandadharabhave), the strong will swallow the weak; but under his protection, the weak resist the strong" (Book I, Chapter IV). But the goal is not power. The text says so in one sentence: "Kautilya holds that wealth and wealth alone is important, inasmuch as charity and desire depend upon wealth for their realisation" (Book I, Chapter VII). Power is disaggregated into three kinds and treated as an instrument: "Strength is power, and happiness is the end" (Book VI, Chapter II). Reading Kautilya as a power-theorist rather than a wealth-theorist inverts his own stated hierarchy and loses what is most distinctive about him — that his realism is fiscal before it is martial.

The blog says the mandala theory "stipulates that the immediate neighboring country is an enemy, but the neighboring countries' enemies are considered an ally," and calls this "his foreign policy." The compression is not false but it drops the working parts. The mandala is a twelve-king system with four circles, sixty elements of sovereignty and seventy-two elements of state (Book VI, Chapter II), and it includes two positions the slogan version omits: the *madhyama*, a middle king strong enough to tip a contest between you and your enemy, and the *udāsīna*, a neutral king strong enough to tip a contest among all three. Those two are what make it a theory of leverage rather than a proverb about neighbours. And the mandala is not the policy — it is the map. The policy is the *ṣāḍguṇya*, the six measures, in Book VII.

## 2. The world the text assumes

A crowded subcontinent of small and medium kingdoms with shared borders and no empty space between them. Every king has a neighbour; every neighbour is, by position, a threat. Distance is the only structural source of friendship: the king beyond your enemy shares your enemy, therefore shares your interest, therefore is your friend, whether or not you like him. This is the single assumption that generates the mandala, and it is the one that transfers least well to a world of oceans, alliances of ideology and non-contiguous power.

An agrarian economy the state owns large parts of directly. The king runs mines, forests, herds, weaving houses, liquor, gambling, prostitution and trade routes as revenue departments with salaried superintendents (Book II). This is not a night-watchman state taxing private commerce; it is a state that is itself the largest economic actor and whose treasury is filled as much by production as by tax. When Kautilya says a king "should direct his attention towards the strengthening of the safety and security of the elements of his sovereignty; for the country is the source of all those works which are conducive to treasury and army" (Book VII, Chapter XIV), he means: go build irrigation works, mines and trade roads.

Population is the scarce resource, not land. Waste land can be colonised; people cannot be manufactured. "What can a depopulated country like a barren cow be productive of?" (Book VII, Chapter XI). Kings compete by attracting each other's subjects with remissions of tax, cheap land and low labour burdens — the text repeatedly contemplates emptying an enemy's country of its population by making your own more attractive. Conquest that ruins the country conquered is a loss.

A caste order treated as a fact of administration rather than a topic of argument, and a religious apparatus the state manipulates openly. Book XIII, Chapter I instructs the king to manufacture his own reputation for omniscience and divine association by using spies disguised as gods, planted omens, and astrologers on the payroll — and Book IX, Chapter IV tells him not to believe any of it himself: "Wealth will pass away from that childish man who inquires most after the stars." Religion is a lever for the operator and a liability for the credulous. The text is cheerfully instrumental about this in a way that has no European equivalent before Machiavelli.

Espionage as permanent civil infrastructure, not wartime improvisation. Book I, Chapters XI–XII establish five standing "institutes of espionage" and five classes of wandering spy, with orphans raised and trained from childhood for the work, cover identities as ascetics, merchants, cultivators and cooks, cipher writing, and a rule that the institutes and the wandering spies must not know one another. Intelligence is an organ of the state, as much as the treasury.

Low information speed and high seasonal constraint. Marching is timed to crops and weather: Book IX, Chapter I specifies December for destroying rainy crops, March for autumnal crops, May–June against a country whose fodder and water have run down. Because command is slow, the text's answer to distance is delegation plus surveillance of the delegates — every officer is watched, and the watchers are watched.

Psychologically, the text assumes people are motivated by interest and grievance, and that both are readable and purchasable. It does not assume they are wicked. It assumes they are legible. Book I, Chapter XIV's taxonomy of the disaffected — the provoked, the alarmed, the ambitious, the haughty — is a sorting mechanism for finding which lever moves a given man, and it is offered without moral comment.

## 3. Core doctrine

**1. The end of the science is wealth, not glory, virtue or power.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapter VII; Book XV, Chapter I.
*Reasoning:* The three classical pursuits — dharma, artha, kama — are interdependent, and any one pursued to excess damages the other two; but charity and desire both require material means to be realised at all, so wealth is the load-bearing one. The definition of the whole science is "the means of acquiring and maintaining the earth."
*Limits:* Wealth is not to be acquired by violating righteousness or economy ("Not violating righteousness and economy, he shall enjoy his desires", Book I, Chapter VII). And wealth has categories: Book IX, Chapter VII names three kinds of wealth conducive to the enemy's prosperity — "dangerous wealth, provocative wealth, and wealth of doubtful consequences" — dangerous wealth being that "which, when obtained, increases the enemy's prosperity, or which, though obtained, is repayable to the enemy, or which causes loss of men and money." Not all gain is gain.

**2. Order is prior to everything, because without an enforcer the strong eat the weak.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapter IV.
*Reasoning:* When punishment is held in abeyance, the "proverb of fishes" (*matsyanyāya*) takes over — the large fish swallows the small. Under an enforcing authority, the weak can resist the strong. Every other science depends on the one that maintains the enforcer.
*Limits:* This justifies authority; it does not license arbitrary authority. See the next principle.

**3. Punishment must be proportionate; both severity and laxity destroy the punisher.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapter IV.
*Reasoning:* Kautilya explicitly rejects his teacher's advice to keep the sceptre always raised. Severe punishment makes a ruler repulsive; mild punishment makes him contemptible; deserved punishment makes him respectable. Punishment awarded under greed or anger "excites fury even among hermits and ascetics."
*Limits:* Proportionality is about publicly awarded punishment. The same text prescribes secret assassination for specific enemies; the constraint is on the visible administration of justice, which is a reputational asset, not on covert action.

**4. The first enemies are internal: lust, anger, greed, vanity, haughtiness, overjoy.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapters VI–VII.
*Reasoning:* Restraint of the senses is what makes study and discipline possible at all, and the six passions are the standing threat to it. The chapter lists named kings destroyed by each one in turn — Rāvaṇa by vanity, Duryodhana by vanity, Janamejaya by anger, and so on — as a casualty list.
*Limits:* This is training, not asceticism. The same passage says the king "shall never be devoid of happiness" and may enjoy the three pursuits, provided none is taken to excess.

**5. No one operates alone; deliberate with three or four, not one and not many.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapters VII and XV.
*Reasoning:* "Sovereignty (rájatva) is possible only with assistance. A single wheel can never move." One adviser proceeds wilfully and unchecked; two can combine against you or deadlock; three or four produce a usable decision without losing secrecy. More than four and both decision and secrecy become expensive.
*Limits:* Council size scales to need, not to a fixed number, and in emergencies the whole assembly is convened and the majority followed. The strict cap applies to secret deliberation.

**6. Test men before you trust them, and never make yourself the bait.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapter X.
*Reasoning:* Ministers are tested by four staged temptations — religious, monetary, sexual, and fear — and posted according to which they survived; those "proved impure under one or all of these allurements shall be appointed in mines, timber and elephant forests, and manufactories." (The earlier draft of this line said they were sent to the mines for failing *all four*; the text says one or all, and names three destinations, not one.) But Kautilya breaks with his predecessors on method: the king must never use himself or the queen as the object of the test, because the temptation may not wash out. "The mind of the valiant, though naturally kept steadfast, may not, when once vitiated and repelled under the four kinds of allurements, return to and recover its original form."
*Limits:* The test must be run against an external object, through intermediaries. Testing is diagnostic, and diagnosis that damages the subject is malpractice.

**7. Intelligence is a permanent establishment, and a report is believed only when three independent channels agree.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapters XI–XII.
*Reasoning:* Five standing institutes and five classes of wandering spy are maintained continuously, with the institutes and the wanderers kept ignorant of each other. "When the information thus received from these three different sources is exactly of the same version, it shall be held reliable. If they (the three sources) frequently differ, the spies concerned shall either be punished in secret or dismissed."
*Limits:* The rule creates a standing cost and a standing risk — the apparatus is itself penetrable, and Book I, Chapter XII assumes the enemy's spies are inside yours.

**8. Disaffection is the enemy's raw material, and it comes in four sorts, each with its own lever.**
*Locus:* Book I, Chapters XIII–XIV.
*Reasoning:* The provoked, the alarmed, the ambitious (impoverished, greedy, addicted, overextended) and the haughty are separately enumerated, with a scripted approach for each. "Those that are angry, those that are greedy, those that are alarmed, as well as those that despise the king are the instruments of enemies." The same taxonomy is used defensively at home and offensively abroad.
*Limits:* Coercion is the last of the four means and is explicitly withheld against assemblies of influential men, because "it is very difficult to inflict punishment on an assembly of influential men; and if inflicted at all, it may not produce the desired effect" (Book IX, Chapter VI). Against groups, use the other three.

**9. A state is seven constituent elements, not a person; each can be in health or distress independently.**
*Locus:* Book VI, Chapter I; Book VIII, Chapters I–II.
*Reasoning:* "THE king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army and the friend are the elements of sovereignty." Book VIII then runs a long disputation on which element's failure is worst, with Kautilya ruling against each predecessor in turn and finally declaring that the calamity which threatens to destroy the other elements is the serious one, whichever element it began in.
*Limits:* The ranking is contextual, not fixed. "When the calamities of a single element tend to destroy the rest of the elements, those calamities, whether they be of the fundamental or any other element, are verily serious."

**10. Position is relational geometry, not sentiment: the mandala.**
*Locus:* Book VI, Chapter II.
*Reasoning:* Whoever is immediately on your border is the enemy; whoever is beyond him is your friend; beyond that, the enemy's friend, and so on outward. Add the *madhyama* (adjacent to both you and your enemy, strong enough to matter to either) and the *udāsīna* (beyond all of them, strong enough to matter to all). Twelve kings, seventy-two elements. The conqueror makes himself the nave of the wheel and the other states the spokes.
*Limits:* Kautilya distinguishes natural from factitious enemies and natural (inherited) from acquired friends, so the geometry is a default that inheritance and conduct can modify.

**11. There are three kinds of strength, and the subtlest beats the grossest.**
*Locus:* Book VI, Chapter II; Book IX, Chapter I; Book VII, Chapter VI.
*Reasoning:* Strength is intellectual (power of deliberation), sovereign (treasury and army) and physical (martial). Against his teacher, who ranks enthusiasm first and then raw power, Kautilya ranks them in reverse: "skill for intrigue is better; he who has the eye of knowledge and is acquainted with the science of polity can with little effort make use of his skill for intrigue and can succeed... in over-reaching even those kings who are possessed of enthusiasm and power." This is why the text distinguishes open battle, treacherous battle and silent battle — the last being victory by intrigue where there is no talk of battle at all.
*Limits:* The sentence usually cited as the limit is not one. In full (Book IX, Chapter I) it reads: "Thus of the three acquirements, viz., enthusiasm, power and skill for intrigue, he who posesses more of the quality mentioned later than the one mentioned first in the order of enumeration will be successful in over-reaching others" (the spelling "posesses" is the source's). Since intrigue is enumerated last, the sentence *strengthens* the ranking rather than qualifying it. The real limit is elsewhere and is structural: intrigue in this text is executed through spies, bribes, gifts and agents, all of which are bought out of the treasury (Book I, Chapters XI–XII; Book XII, Chapter II). Kautilya nowhere says intrigue is free; he says it converts a small stock of resources into disproportionate effect.

**12. Policy is selected from your relative position, not from your temperament — the six measures.**
*Locus:* Book VII, Chapters I–III.
*Reasoning:* Peace (*sandhi*), war (*vigraha*), neutrality (*āsana*), marching (*yāna*), alliance or shelter (*saṃśraya*) and the double policy (*dvaidhībhāva*). The allocation rule is mechanical: "Whoever is inferior to another shall make peace with him; whoever is superior in power shall wage war; whoever thinks 'no enemy can hurt me, nor am I strong enough to destroy my enemy,' shall observe neutrality; whoever is possessed of necessary means shall march against his enemy; whoever is devoid of necessary strength to defend himself shall seek the protection of another; whoever thinks that help is necessary to work out an end shall make peace with one and wage war with another." When the advantages of peace and war are equal, take peace: "for disadvantages, such as the loss of power and wealth, sojourning, and sin, are ever-attending upon war" (Book VII, Chapter II). (An earlier draft rendered these overheads as "loss of men, money, sojourning and sin"; the text says power and wealth.)
*Limits:* The whole of Book VII, Chapter III then supplies exceptions where a superior king should sue for peace or a weaker one should march, so the rule is a default that a concrete reading of the situation overrides.

**13. Measure every position as deterioration, stagnation or progress, and choose the policy that moves you up one step.**
*Locus:* Book VII, Chapter I.
*Reasoning:* The three states are defined against the enemy's trajectory, not absolutely: "No king shall keep that form of policy, which causes him the loss of profit from his own works, but which entails no such loss on the enemy; for it is deterioration." Temporary loss is acceptable if the differential over time favours you. The stated object of the six measures is to move from deterioration to stagnation and from stagnation to progress.
*Limits:* If two hostile powers are deteriorating or stagnating at the same rate, the prescribed move is peace with each other — the relative frame means a contest neither can win is simply not worth running.

**14. Engage downward only: peace with equals and superiors, attack only inferiors.**
*Locus:* Book VII, Chapter III.
*Reasoning:* Three similes carry the whole argument. Against a superior you are "a foot-soldier opposing an elephant." Against an equal it is two unbaked clay pots colliding: both break. Against an inferior you are a stone striking an earthen pot. And the only thing that produces a durable peace between equals is demonstrated strength: "it is power that brings about peace between any two kings: no piece of iron that is not made red-hot will combine with another piece of iron."
*Limits:* Trouble changes the arithmetic. "When a king finds the troubles of his enemy irremediable, he should, though of inferior power, march against the enemy." Relative power is baseline; relative distress is the modifier.

**15. The weak king's rule: shelter, or a fort, or intrigue — never a small army in the open field.**
*Locus:* Book XII, Chapter I.
*Reasoning:* Kautilya rejects both predecessors. Against Bhāradvāja's total submission and Viśālākṣa's honourable last stand, he says: "he who bows down to all like a crab on the banks (of a river) lives in despair; whoever goes with his small army to fight perishes like a man attempting to cross the sea without a boat. Hence, a weak king should either seek the protection of a powerful king or maintain himself in an impregnable fort."
*Limits:* The choice among the three depends on who is attacking. A just conqueror is satisfied by obeisance, so take shelter with him. A greedy conqueror is satisfied by loot, so pay. A demon-like conqueror wants your life, so keep him at a distance with land and wealth and prepare to fight by other means.

**16. When you have lost everything, rebuild the productive base first — the doctrine of the recruitment of lost power.**
*Locus:* Book VII, Chapter XIV.
*Reasoning:* This is the text's explicit programme for the ruined. Break the coalition against you by showing each member what the coalition's leader will do to him next. Then, deficit by deficit: "Whoever is wanting in the power of deliberation should collect wise men around himself, and associate with old men of considerable learning." "He who is devoid of a good treasury and army should direct his attention towards the strengthening of the safety and security of the elements of his sovereignty; for the country is the source of all those works which are conducive to treasury and army." Then the enumeration of what actually generates revenue: irrigation works give crops, trade roads give access and purchase, mines give war materials, forests give timber and elephants, pastures give draught animals. "If he is destitute of an army, he should, as far as possible, attract to himself the brave men of corporations, of thieves, of wild tribes, of Mlechchhas, and of spies."
*Limits:* If you have none of these sources, "he should acquire them from some one among his relatives and friends," and adopt the posture of a weak king toward a strong one in the meantime. There is no step in this programme that begins with an attack.

**17. Attack the ruler whose subjects are oppressed, not the ruler whose subjects are merely poor.**
*Locus:* Book VII, Chapter V.
*Reasoning:* Kautilya overrules his teacher here, and the reason is the sharpest sentence in the book on the subject: impoverished and greedy subjects are easy to bribe, "for though impoverished and greedy, they are loyal to their master and are ready to stand for his cause and to defeat any intrigue against him; for it is in loyalty that all other good qualities have their strength." Oppressed subjects are the exploitable ones. Likewise a powerful wicked king is a better target than a weak virtuous one, because the virtuous king's people "will help him or die with him."
*Limits:* The same chapter lists in detail the twenty-odd administrative behaviours that manufacture disaffection, as a defensive checklist — the analysis cuts both ways and is meant to.

**18. Internal danger before external, always: the lurking snake.**
*Locus:* Book VIII, Chapter II; Book IX, Chapters III and V.
*Reasoning:* "Internal troubles are more serious than external troubles which are like the danger arising from a lurking snake." The reason is structural: internal actors are inside your decision loop and your defences, and external actors can only reach you through them. Book IX, Chapter V ranks the four combinations of internal and external origin and abetment and rules that the internally-originated, internally-abetted danger is gravest. Book IX, Chapter III goes further: "slight annoyance in the rear is more serious" than large profit in front.
*Limits:* The remedy varies by combination — conciliation and gifts against locals abetting foreigners, dissension and coercion against foreigners.

**19. Take the country intact; the point of the conquest is a working country.**
*Locus:* Book XIII, Chapters IV–V.
*Reasoning:* Fire is forbidden where anything else will serve, because "fire cannot be trusted; it not only offends gods, but also destroys the people, grains, cattle, gold, raw materials and the like. Also the acquisition of a fort with its property all destroyed is a source of further loss." And the definition: "no territory deserves the name of a kingdom or country unless it is full of people." Quarter is given to those of the enemy's army who surrender — the text specifies those "lying prostrate in the field, or as standing with their back turned to the conqueror, or with their hair dishevelled, with their weapons thrown down or with their body disfigured and shivering under fear."
*Limits:* Against a population that actively resists, the text authorises destroying stores, crops and trade and killing the leaders in secret — the restraint is instrumental, not moral, and it lapses when the country cannot be held.

**20. Win the conquered by conforming to them, and by keeping your promises.**
*Locus:* Book XIII, Chapter V.
*Reasoning:* "He should adopt the same mode of life, the same dress, language, and customs as those of the people. He should follow the people in their faith with which they celebrate their national, religious and congregational festivals or amusements." Rewards promised to defectors must be paid and paid again as often as help is rendered, "for whoever fails to fullfil his promises becomes untrustworthy both to his own and his enemy's people" (the misspelling "fullfil" is the source's; an earlier draft silently corrected it). The rule against dispossessing a beaten king is in a different place from the rest of this principle — Book VII, Chapter XVI, not Book XIII: "Whoever covets the lands, things, sons, and wives of the kings whom he has either slain or bound in chains will cause provocation to the Circle of States and make it rise against himself; also his own ministers employed in his own territory will be provoked."
*Limits:* The assimilation is calculated and paired with the quiet relocation of anyone dangerous — officers who conspired, captured renegades, and "whoever has caused excitement to the people" — to remote or hazardous postings.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

This is the one text on the thirteen-item list that addresses the weak party head-on, at length, in its own dedicated books, rather than by implication. Book XII is titled "Concerning a Powerful Enemy" and is written entirely from the weaker king's chair. Book VII, Chapter XIV is titled "Recruitment of Lost Power." Book VII, Chapter III catalogues the forms of submission available to an inferior king. Book VII, Chapter XV covers how to live as a protected vassal, and Book V, Chapters IV–V cover how a man with nothing but competence should approach a patron. None of this requires inference.

What the text says directly:

**Do not fight, and do not simply submit.** The opening move of Book XII (principle 15 above) rejects both. Viśālākṣa's position — that a weak king should fight with everything he has because bravery destroys all troubles and this is a warrior's duty regardless of outcome — is stated fairly and then dismissed, not on moral grounds but arithmetic ones. Universal submission is dismissed in the same breath, because the crab that bows to everything "lives in despair." Two viable postures, not one: shelter under a stronger protector, or a position you can hold.

**Price your submission and pay in the cheapest currency.** Book VII, Chapter III enumerates the forms of peace an inferior king can make — surrendering troops, surrendering hostages, paying money, ceding land — and grades them by what they actually cost. Book XII, Chapter I is more specific and more ruthless: if the enemy demands troops, send the units full of traitors and wild tribes under a trusted officer, so that both the enemy and your own undesirable men are consumed; if he demands treasure, pay in precious articles that have no buyer and raw products useless in war; if he demands land, give him land you can recover, or land another enemy already threatens, or land that will cost him men and money to colonise. And the governing rule: "he should take care more of his person than of his wealth, for of what interest is perishing wealth?" Survival first, assets second, dignity nowhere on the list.

**Substitute intrigue for force, because it is the one input the poor can afford.** Book IX, Chapter I's ranking — intrigue over power over enthusiasm — is the strategic charter for the weak, and Book XII, Chapters II–V is its operational manual: split the coalition, corrupt the commanders, seduce the officers, poison the supply, set the rear enemy in motion, plant a rival claimant. Book VII, Chapter VI names the category: *silent battle*, "attempt to win over the chief officers of the enemy by intrigue," fought where there is no talk of battle at all. A weak actor who cannot win a contest can often prevent one from being held.

**Argue the strong man out of it before you fight him.** Book XII, Chapter II gives the weak king a speech to deliver to the invader, and the speech is entirely composed of the invader's own interests: the people urging you to attack me are your enemies wearing a friend's face; you will spend men and money and hand the profits to third parties; you have already lost your standing with the middle and neutral kings, who are waiting for you to overextend. It is the argument from the other man's balance sheet, and it is offered before any of the sabotage begins.

**Build the base, not the army** (principle 16 above). The army is never step one. If you need men immediately you take the ones nobody else wants — corporations, thieves, wild tribes, foreigners, spies — and you accept that they are dangerous: "In the absence of plunder and under troubles, they prove as dangerous as a lurking snake" (Book IX, Chapter II).

**Serving a patron is a technique with its own rules.** Book V, Chapters IV–V is a manual for the competent man with no position. Choose a patron who is capable of taking advice, even if he is poor and lacks resources — "He may even court the favour of such a king as is poor and destitute of the elements of sovereignty, but never, of such a one as is of a depraved character," because a man with a habitual hatred of the science cannot keep what he has. Get an explicit agreement about your role before you take the post. Then: sit close to the principal and far from your rival; never contradict a colleague slyly; never make a claim you cannot support; never ask openly for money or promotion; never form a permanent bloc. Tell him what is good and pleasing; tell him what is unpleasant and good only in private and only if he will hear it; where the truth is hateful, keep silence. And the sentence that governs it all: "Self-protection shall be the first and constant thought of a wise man; for the life of a man under the service of a king is aptly compared to life in fire." Chapter V then gives a long behavioural checklist for reading whether you are still in favour. Signs of satisfaction: he "remembers (the courtier) while taking luncheon", "consults (the courtier) when in trouble", "honours the followers of the courtier", "reveals the secret". Signs of displeasure: "seeing with one eye", "brow-beating", "failure to acknowledge his good deeds", "enumeration of his bad deeds", "contempt of learning, caste, and country (of the courtier)". (An earlier draft listed "he praises your opponent" among the signs of displeasure. The text's displeasure list has the opposite item — "condemnation of his opponent". The nearest thing to the draft's claim is a separate opinion attributed to the son of Pisuna: "mortification ensues when his opponent is courted.") When the favour and the money both stop, leave, or correct your own defects, or attach to one of his friends. The text treats being fired as a scheduled event to be detected early, not a catastrophe.

**A protected vassal has a script.** Book VII, Chapter XV: behave like a servant, seek permission before major undertakings, have your people pray publicly for the protector's long life, help him where you can, never accept a better country even from a friend — and, in the same breath, watch for the moment his position weakens, because Book VII, Chapter II has already noted that a powerless king should behave as a conquered king "but when he finds that the time of his own ascendancy is at hand... he may get out." The deference is instrumental and time-bounded, and the text says so without embarrassment.

**One asymmetry the weak actually own.** Book VII, Chapter IX rules, against his teacher, that a small friend easy to rouse is better than a large one hard to rouse, because the small one "will not, in virtue of his ready preparations, be behind the opportune moment of work." Speed off a small base is a real advantage and the text names it as one.

Where I am inferring rather than reporting: Kautilya nowhere addresses a person with no political standing at all. His "weak king" still has a treasury, subjects and a fort, however depleted. The extension of his doctrine to a man with literally nothing — Section 8 — is my inference from the structure of his argument, not his statement. What is his statement is the underlying method: assess position relative to the strongest actor who can reach you, choose the policy the position dictates rather than the one your temperament prefers, and spend on judgment and information before you spend on force.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Continuous and largely economic. Book II is a preparation manual disguised as an administrative one. Marching itself is classed as one of the six policies under the name *yāna*, glossed as "making preparations" (Book VII, Chapter I) — the preparation and the move are the same category. Book IX, Chapter I sets the order of operations: know your own and the enemy's strength, then place, then time, then the cost in men and money, then the expected profit, and only then march with full force; otherwise stay still.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** The most developed treatment in the thirteen texts. Standing establishments, trained-from-childhood agents, cover occupations that are also revenue businesses, cipher writing, double agents drawing pay from two states, and a triple-corroboration rule for believing a report (Book I, Chapters XI–XII). Uniquely, the apparatus is turned inward first: Book I, Chapters X and XIII are about surveilling your own ministers and your own population before you surveil anyone else's.

**Timing.** Seasonal, agricultural and opportunistic. Book IX, Chapter I fixes marching months against the enemy's crop and fodder cycle. Book XIII, Chapter IV lists the moments for a general assault: fire "accidental or intentionally kindled", a sacrificial performance or spectacle, a quarrel from drink, an army worn down by daily engagements, men fallen asleep from sleeplessness, "a cloudy day, of floods, or of a thick fog or snow." (An earlier draft located this list at Book X, Chapter III. Book X, Chapter III has its own, shorter set of openings against a tired enemy; the cloud-flood-fog-snow list is Book XIII.) Kautilya overrules his teacher's "almost invariably march against an enemy in troubles" with a caution: an enemy's troubles cannot be reliably recognised from outside, so march when your own resources suffice (Book IX, Chapter I).

**Terrain and position.** Treated economically as well as tactically. Forts are graded — plain, river, mountain, in ascending order of value (Book VII, Chapter XII) — and the fort is the weak king's primary asset. Book VII, Chapter X grades territory by whether it can sustain itself without a garrison, by its distance, and by the character of the neighbour it borders. Book X, Chapter IV matches ground types to arms. The recurring criterion is not defensibility alone but whether a position pays for its own defence.

**Deception and information.** Constitutive, not supplementary. Three forms of battle, of which two are deceptive and the third is fought with a deceived enemy where possible (Book VII, Chapter VI; Book X, Chapter III). Book XIII, Chapter I is a programme for manufacturing the king's reputation for omniscience using planted informants and staged miracles — reputation as an engineered asset. The text's own summary of siege method names intrigue and winning over the enemy's people before siege and assault: "Intrigue, spies, winning over the enemy's people, siege, and assault are the five means to capture a fort" (Book XIII, Chapter IV). The order is the doctrine.

**Alliances.** Analysed harder than in any other text on the list. Alliance (*saṃśraya*) is the policy of the man who cannot defend himself, and it is explicitly the least preferred of the six: "whoever adopts the double policy enriches himself, being ever attentive to his own works, whereas an allied king has to help his ally at his own expense" (Book VII, Chapter II). "…for there can be no greater evil to kings than alliance with a king of considerable power, unless one is actually attacked by one's enemy" (Book VII, Chapter II; the earlier draft truncated the clause and capitalised its first word as though it began a sentence). Allies are graded by longevity, responsiveness, size and resources, with Kautilya ruling in each case for the smaller, quicker, longer-standing partner over the larger, slower, richer one. Book VII, Chapter V: march with two equals rather than one superior, because you can separate two equals and you cannot escape a superior; and after victory, let your allies over-collect on the spoils, because winning the circle of states is worth more than winning the argument about shares.

**Logistics and economy.** The centre of gravity of the whole work. Treasury, mines, irrigation, trade routes, forests and pastures are named as the sources from which army and treasury derive (Book VII, Chapter XIV). Book VII, Chapter XII evaluates undertakings by output against outlay — "it is a loss to undertake a work of less out-put and of a greater outlay" — and applies the same test to the enemy's balance sheet: a work "productive of benefits to the enemy" is a loss, and "when the benefits are equal, the conqueror has to consider that his condition is stagnant." (An earlier draft collapsed those two into one and called equal benefit a loss; equal benefit is stagnation.) Book IX, Chapter IV grades expected profit on twelve named characteristics before any march is authorised. "Acquisition and security (of property) are dependent upon peace and industry" (Book VI, Chapter II — this is the chapter's opening sentence, which Shamasastry sets in full capitals; see the normalisation note in Section 7).

**Morale and discipline.** Handled instrumentally and with unusual candour. Book X, Chapter III scripts the pre-battle address, and the king's line is startling: "I am a paid servant like yourselves; this country is to be enjoyed (by me) together with you." Then the religious machinery — priests promising the warrior's heaven, astrologers proclaiming the array impregnable, bards extolling lineage — followed immediately by a schedule of cash bounties: a hundred thousand panas for the enemy king, fifty thousand for the commander, down to twenty for a head, plus double pay on captured goods. Physicians and women with food and drink stand behind the line "uttering encouraging words." Morale is a compound of ideology, money and visible care, deployed deliberately.

**The decisive point.** Not a doctrine of a single decisive battle. Book XIII, Chapter IV's five means are ranked with intrigue first and assault last, and Book XIII, Chapter IV explicitly says reduction must precede siege. The decisive point, where the text locates one, is the loyalty of the opposing population and the integrity of the opposing command — both attacked before contact.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Addressed, though not named as such. Book VII, Chapter I's framework of deterioration, stagnation and progress is a running assessment of whether you have gone past the point where effort still yields. "When, either in peace or war, a king finds neither loss to his enemy nor gain to himself, he should, though superior, observe neutrality." Book X, Chapter III: "Having broken the whole army (of the enemy), (the invader) should seek for peace" — and, where the enemy's army is inferior, he should destroy it, "but not that which has secured a favourable position and is reckless of life." Book VII, Chapter V: after victory, discharge your allies satisfied rather than push the advantage. Success is closed out deliberately rather than pressed.

**When not to fight.** The most fully specified answer in the thirteen. Do not fight a superior (the foot-soldier and the elephant). Do not fight an equal (the two clay pots). Do not fight when peace and war offer equal returns, because war carries standing overheads. Do not fight when the wealth in prospect is dangerous, provocative or of doubtful consequence (Book IX, Chapter VII); do not take a repayable profit except under the specific calculations Book IX, Chapter IV sets out, since "whoever goes to receive a repayable profit or is enjoying it gets destruction." Do not, as the weak party, fight in the field at all. And: "he should not harass a broken army" of the enemy, because a broken army that resumes its attack is irresistible (Book X, Chapter III).

**Defence versus offence.** Both are tools chosen by position, and the text refuses to privilege either. The fort is the weak actor's instrument and the text is emphatic that a fort holds a treasury and an army safe in a way nothing else does (Book VIII, Chapter I). But Book VI, Chapter I's promise is offensive: "a wise king, trained in politics, will, though he possesses a small territory, conquer the whole earth with the help of the best-fitted elements of his sovereignty." Defence is what you do while your base compounds.

**The character required of the commander.** Book VI, Chapter I gives a long list, and its striking feature is how much of it is cognitive and temperamental rather than martial: inquiry, hearing, perception, retention, reflection, deliberation, inference and "steadfast adherence to conclusions" as the qualities of intellect; valour, determination, quickness and probity as the aspects of enthusiasm; and for self-possession, freedom from "passion, anger, greed, obstinacy, fickleness, haste and back-biting habits," never brow-beating, "talking to others with a smiling face." Book I, Chapter XIX adds the operational virtue: "the root of wealth is activity, and of evil its reverse."

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The Weber echo chamber.** Max Weber's line in *Politik als Beruf* (1919) — that Machiavelli's *Principe* is harmless beside the Arthashastra — is quoted more often than the Arthashastra is read, and it has become the standard framing device. Roger Boesche built a book on it (*The First Great Political Realist: Kautilya and His Arthashastra*, Lexington Books, 2002 — title, publisher and year confirmed in the Wikipedia article's bibliography), arguing that Kautilya is the earlier and more radical realist. The blog's own commentary repeats Weber's sentence, including his date-claim, without attribution. The distortion the comparison produces is specific: it selects the assassination-and-poison material (Books XII–XIV), which is perhaps a tenth of the text, and discards the nine-tenths about revenue, irrigation, weights and measures and the superintendency of ships. It also flattens the one place where Kautilya's realism is most interesting — the fiscal one. He is closer to a treasury official than to a condottiere.

**The composite-text problem, and reading it as one man's mind.** The main scholarly critique is not about content but about attribution. Dieter Schlingloff (1967) argued the traditional attribution to the minister Chanakya is hardly historical. Hartmut Scharfe (1968) read the prose as an expansion of an earlier verse work and dated it around 150 CE. Thomas Trautmann (1971) applied statistical analysis of word frequencies across the books, concluded multiple authorship, and dated the compilation provisionally to c. 250 CE. Patrick Olivelle (2013) proposed three layers with the final śāstric redaction in c. 175–300 CE. Mark McClish (2019) argued for an original anonymous *Daṇḍanīti* recast by a later redactor. R. P. Kangle (1960–65) defended the traditional single-author Mauryan attribution and is still cited; L. N. Rangarajan (1992) questioned Trautmann's analysis while following Kangle. Any reading that treats the Arthashastra as a unified psychological portrait of a single man advising a single king — which is how popular treatments almost always present it — is proceeding against the weight of the scholarship. The "Kautilya says / my teacher says" dialogue form, which is the book's most distinctive device, may itself be a redactional layer added to assert an author.

**Reading it as a description of the Mauryan state.** The text is prescriptive and to some degree idealised, and the dating problem removes most of its evidential value for the Mauryas. The Wikipedia *Maurya Empire* article states the position flatly, citing Stein (2010) and others: the Arthashastra, "previously attributed to a Kautilya, who was mistakenly identified with Chanakya, is now thought to be composed by multiple authors in the first centuries of the common era, has lost its value as a source for Mauryan times, as it describes post-Mauryan customs." Separately, the older picture of the empire as a solid mass under uniform central administration has been replaced in the mainstream literature by a core-and-network model — core regions around Pataliputra, Taxila, Ujjain, Kalinga and the lower Deccan, with peripheral or no control between them, and with the empire's extent outside the core depending on the loyalty of local military commanders (Kulke and Rothermund 2004, pp. xii, 448; Thapar 1990, p. 384, as cited there). Historians who take the Arthashastra's superintendents as a literal organisational chart of the Mauryan administration are using a manual as a census. *An earlier draft of this section attributed two specific arguments to Gerard Fussman and a change of position to Romila Thapar. I could not locate either in any source I opened, and both have been cut — see "Cut on verification" at the end of this file.*

**The business and self-help appropriation.** A large industry converts the Arthashastra into management advice — Radhakrishnan Pillai's *Corporate Chanakya* (Jaico Publishing House, 2010) and its sequels — *Corporate Chanakya on Management* and *Corporate Chanakya on Leadership* — are the best-selling examples. (Author, year and publisher confirmed against the Open Library search API; the sequels are listed in the Wikipedia article on Chanakya.) Two problems recur. First, conflation: most popular "Chanakya" material actually draws on the *Chanakyaniti* (also called *Chanakya Niti* or *Chanakya Neeti-shastra*), a separate collection of Sanskrit aphorisms traditionally ascribed to Chanakya, not on the Arthashastra at all. (Wikipedia's article on the *Chanakyaniti* describes it as a collection of aphorisms traditionally ascribed to Chanakya and does not date it; an earlier draft of this file called it "later" than the Arthashastra, which I cannot source and have dropped.) The two are routinely quoted interchangeably. Second, fabrication: the "Chanakya quotes" that circulate on quotation sites and social media are largely unsourced and many cannot be traced to either text. Anyone building on this material should treat an unattributed Chanakya quotation as false until located in a numbered chapter of a named translation. Examples of the genre — proverbs about not sharing your plans, about a snake and a wicked man, about the education of a son — are pervasive online; I found none of the common ones in the Shamasastry text by search, which does not prove they are absent from the Sanskrit but does mean they cannot be cited from the standard public-domain English.

**"Chanakya" as a political epithet.** In contemporary Indian political journalism, "Chanakya" is a routine honorific for any effective backroom strategist. The usage is harmless as journalism but it has the effect of fixing the popular image of the text at cunning-and-manipulation, which is the part of the book least characteristic of it.

**The internal critique the text makes of its own methods.** Worth registering because popular readings miss it. Kautilya forbids the king to use himself or his queen as the bait in loyalty tests, because the test may permanently corrupt the tested. He forbids fire in sieges wherever another method will serve. He forbids coercion against assemblies of influential men. He forbids harassing a broken army. He rejects, by name, his teacher's advice to keep the sceptre always raised. None of this is squeamishness; all of it is cost accounting. But a reading that presents the text as unlimited ruthlessness has not read Book I, Chapter IV or Book XIII, Chapter IV.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Kautilya is the most directly usable of the thirteen texts for a man starting from zero, for one reason: he is the only one of them who wrote a dedicated book for the party who is losing, and he did not write it as consolation. Book XII and Book VII, Chapter XIV are procedures. But the transfer has to be made carefully, because the assumptions underneath his advice have changed more than the advice has.

**What transfers.**

*The refusal of the heroic option.* The single most useful sentence in the book for a man with nothing is the rejection of Viśālākṣa. The brave gesture — quit the job and go all in, confront the person who has power over you, stake everything on one attempt — is the crab-and-the-sea sentence. What the text actually says is narrower than the draft version of this paragraph claimed. Kautilya gives no odds and runs no arithmetic; he gives a simile — the man who fights with a small army "perishes like a man attempting to cross the sea without a boat" — which asserts destruction rather than estimates it, and he reaches it by rejecting Viśālākṣa's premise that bravery is its own justification. So the doctrinal content is: the honourable last stand is ruled out on outcome, not on honour, and the grounds Viśālākṣa gave for it (bravery destroys all troubles, a warrior's duty is to fight) are not accepted as grounds. *My inference, not his:* the modern analogue is the single large bet made from a position that cannot absorb losing it, and its appeal is that it resolves the discomfort of being weak rather than the weakness. Kautilya does not say that; the diagnosis of why the gesture attracts people is mine.

*The equal refusal of pure submission.* The crab that bows to everything "lives in despair." A man who takes every condition offered, never negotiates, never sets a floor, and understands his own compliance as prudence, is executing the other failure mode. Kautilya's actual instruction is to occupy a defensible position and pay in the cheapest currency. Applied: take the job, and treat the terms as priced items — which of them costs you the thing you cannot replace (time to build a skill, a reputation, your health) and which of them costs you something you have in surplus (status, comfort, being right in an argument). Pay in the surplus. This is Book XII, Chapter I's list of what to hand the invader, and it is the most transferable single move in the text.

*The order of reconstruction.* Book VII, Chapter XIV closes with its own ordering: "Thus with the aid of one's own party, the power of deliberation, the treasury, and the army, one should get rid of the clutches of one's enemies." Party, then judgment, then money, then force. The chapter's remedy for a deficit of judgment is specific — "Whoever is wanting in the power of deliberation should collect wise men around himself, and associate with old men of considerable learning" — and its remedy for a deficit of treasury and army is not recruitment but the productive base: irrigation works, trade roads, mines, timber and elephant forests, pastures. *What transfers is the ordering and the two remedies:* people around you who know the work and are older in it, before anything else; then a source of income that exists independently of each transaction being fought for; force or headcount last, because the chapter treats them as outputs of the base rather than inputs to it. *My inference, not his:* that the common modern failure is the inversion — funding the appearance of an operation off a base that produces nothing. Kautilya describes a king whose revenue works are neglected as in *deterioration* (Book VII, Chapter I), but he is describing a state's accounts, not a person's self-presentation, and the analogy is mine.

*Intelligence before force, as a budget decision.* Kautilya's ranking of intrigue over power over enthusiasm is the claim that information and judgment are the highest-return input at low capital. What follows from the text is only this: in a contest between parties of unequal resources, Kautilya expects the better-informed and better-advised party to out-manoeuvre the better-resourced one, and he institutionalises information as a standing expense rather than a wartime one. *My inference, not his:* the allocation rule for an individual — put more of a fixed number of hours into learning how the thing works and who actually decides than into effort at the task itself. Kautilya never addresses an individual's time budget, and the claim that most people do not understand the structure of their own field is mine and unmeasured; I have removed the earlier draft's assertion that this ignorance is "the only edge available to someone with no money," which neither he nor I can support. What is his, and is worth having, is the category *silent battle*: a contest decided before it is fought, by what one side knew and the other did not.

*Position is relational, and interest is structural.* This item has to be stated carefully, because the "does not transfer" list below rejects the mandala's geometry and an earlier draft of this paragraph asserted exactly what that rejection denies. What does not survive is the presumption that adjacency *is* enmity — that premise depends on fixed borders and a fixed quantity of land. What survives is the method underneath it: Kautilya assigns friend and enemy by position and interest rather than by sentiment or desert, and he adds the *madhyama* and *udāsīna* precisely to find the parties whose interests bear on a contest without being a party to it. The usable residue is one question, not a map: who else is obstructed by the thing obstructing you, and what does it cost them? That question does not require you to treat the person next to you as an enemy, and Kautilya's own distinction between natural and factitious enemies (Book VI, Chapter II) already allows that position can be overridden.

*Attack the oppressed, not the poor.* Book VII, Chapter V's rule translates into competitive terms with almost no loss. A rival whose customers, staff or collaborators are merely getting a bad deal is not vulnerable — loyalty survives a bad deal. A rival whose customers, staff or collaborators are being treated with contempt is vulnerable to approach — Kautilya's claim is that the oppressed can be won over, not that they arrive unbidden, and Book VII, Chapter V gives a list of scripted approaches for exactly that reason. Look for the contempt, not the price.

*Internal danger first.* Two separate doctrines were run together in the earlier draft of this item and have to be kept apart. (i) "Internal troubles are more serious than external troubles which are like the danger arising from a lurking snake" (Book VIII, Chapter II) is about the *state's* interior — disaffected ministers, subjects, traitors — and Book IX, Chapter V ranks the internally-originated, internally-abetted danger as the gravest of four. (ii) The six enemies of Book I, Chapter VI — lust, anger, greed, vanity, haughtiness, overjoy — are the ruler's own passions, a different list with a different remedy. The text does join them, but in a third place, Book IX, Chapter VII: "Intensity of desire and other passions provoke one's own people; impolicy provokes external enemies." That is the actual chain — the passions do not open a door to the enemy directly; they generate disaffection at home, and disaffection is what the enemy then uses (Book I, Chapters XIII–XIV). *What transfers:* the ranking survives. The failure that ends the attempt is more likely to originate inside the operation than outside it, and the operator's own temper is a cause of internal failure rather than an external hazard. *My inference, not his:* that a man with no money and no position is more exposed to the six than a man with structure around him. Kautilya says nothing of the kind; he is writing to a king with a court.

*Do not consult the stars.* "Wealth is the star for wealth; what will the stars do?" The full sentence is "Wealth will pass away from that childish man who inquires most after the stars; for wealth is the star for wealth; what will the stars do?" (Book IX, Chapter IV), and it sits at the end of a list of obstructions to profit that is mostly internal states — passion, anger, timidity, bashfulness, haughtiness, deception, envy, "negligence of what is at hand," and, named last, "faith in the auspiciousness of lunar days and stars." Against that, Book XIII, Chapter I has the king manufacturing omens, staged miracles and a reputation for reading the stars, for other people to believe. So the doctrine is exact and double: divination is an instrument to be used outward and a leak to be sealed inward. *My inference, not his:* that the modern equivalents are the system that promises to explain everything and the preparation that never becomes an attempt. Kautilya names neither; "negligence of what is at hand" is the closest item on his list and it is not the same claim.

*Being fired is a scheduled event.* Book V, Chapter V's long list of signs that the patron's favour has turned — he stops consulting you, he praises your opponent, he enumerates your errors, he stops remembering you at meals — is a detection procedure. The transfer is exact: favour is legible in advance if you are watching, and the point of watching is to have an exit prepared before you need one. Kautilya's prescribed response, in full, is: "When wealth and honour are discontinued, such a king may be abandoned; or by recognising the character of the king as well as his own defects, he may rectify himself; or he may seek the protection of one of the best friends of the king." Leave, correct yourself, or attach to one of his allies. *My inference, not his:* that the absent options — appeal, explain, wait for fairness — are absent because they do not work. The text simply does not list them, which is weaker evidence than the earlier draft implied.

*Keep the promises you make to the people who helped you.* Book XIII, Chapter V's rule about paying defectors what they were promised, repeatedly, is stated as a hard constraint on the conqueror precisely because breaking it is tempting and cheap in the short run. "whoever fails to fullfil his promises becomes untrustworthy both to his own and his enemy's people" (the source's spelling). What the text supports is the second half of that sentence: the damage runs in both directions at once — "untrustworthy both to his own and his enemy's people" — so the cost of a broken promise is not contained to the party you broke it with. *My inference, not his:* that for a man with nothing this reputation is the one asset that compounds without capital. Kautilya is describing a conqueror administering a captured province, where the promise-keeping is a governing technique with a budget behind it, not a man whose word is all he owns.

**What does not transfer, and why.**

*The mandala's geometry.* Kautilya's friend-of-my-enemy rule works because states are contiguous and cannot move. Modern competitive positions are not contiguous. The structural reason your neighbour must be your enemy — that you both want the same piece of land and there is no other land — mostly does not hold where the market is large, entry is cheap, and you can simply go do something adjacent. Applying the mandala as a rule generates a needless enemy for every person near you. The part that survives is the analysis of interest; the part that does not is the presumption of hostility by proximity.

*The espionage apparatus.* The triple-corroboration rule is a genuine epistemic principle and it transfers. The institution does not. Kautilya's spies work because he can feed, pay, house and if necessary kill them, and because a fabricated report is punishable. A private individual attempting to run agents is not doing strategy; he is doing surveillance of people who have not consented, mostly illegally, and generating information he cannot check. Take the corroboration rule and leave the apparatus.

*Everything in Books XII–XIV involving poison, arson, assassination, false miracles and planted omens.* These require a state with a monopoly on violence, immunity from prosecution and a population that believes in gods appearing from altars. They also require what is easy to miss: Kautilya is describing acts committed against enemy kings during a declared or undeclared war between polities, by a sovereign who answers to no court. There is no modern private analogue. The instinct to read this material as "be ruthless in business" is the specific error Section 6 describes; it converts a sovereign's wartime prerogative into a personal disposition, which is neither legal nor, on Kautilya's own cost-accounting, profitable. The man with nothing has the least protection against retaliation of anyone in the system. Deception as a personal operating style is the one strategy where his position is worst, not best.

*The assumption that the king can restructure the economy he lives in.* Kautilya's advice to the depleted king — build irrigation works, open mines, cut trade roads — assumes ownership of the productive apparatus. A man with nothing owns none of it. The transferable residue is real but smaller than it looks: the principle that you should acquire a source of income that is not a wage, because a wage is a grant and can be withdrawn, and that this is worth doing before, not after, you have the money to do it comfortably. But he is describing state investment, and pretending otherwise inflates the advice.

*The patron relationship as described.* Book V's courtier operates in a system where the patron's favour is the only currency and there is precisely one patron. That is not the modern labour market, and the courtier's discipline — never contradict, never ask, flatter to steer him away from his vices — is calibrated to a world with no exit. Where exit exists, much of the technique becomes self-harm: the man who applies the full courtier protocol in a job market with alternatives has simply chosen to be a servant when he had another option, and has made himself worse at the negotiations that would have got him out. What survives is narrower and still useful: choose a principal who can take advice, get the terms explicit up front, do not ask openly for promotion, and watch for the signs that the relationship has ended.

*"Kautilya holds that wealth and wealth alone is important."* This one transfers as diagnosis and fails as prescription. As diagnosis it is correct and unwelcome: without money you cannot execute any of the other options, your time is not yours, and your judgment degrades under scarcity — Kautilya knew this, which is why the recruitment-of-lost-power chapter is about the revenue base. As a life-ordering principle it is a category error, because he is describing what a *state* must optimise. The state is an instrument that exists to be solvent. A person is not. Kautilya himself sets the limit in the same passage: wealth pursued to excess damages the other two pursuits and itself.

**The unflattering summary.** Kautilya's advice to someone in your position is: you are not going to win a contest with anyone stronger than you, so stop planning one. Find a position you can hold. Pay whatever the position costs in the currency you have most of. Spend your scarce resources on judgment and information, in that order, because they are the only inputs that are cheap and compounding. Build something that produces revenue without your presence, before you build anything that looks impressive. Work out who is obstructed by what obstructs you, and treat that, not sentiment or merit, as the map of who will help. Watch your own temper harder than you watch any competitor, because the failures that end an attempt mostly begin inside it. Keep the promises you make, because breaking one costs you with the people who were watching as well as the people you broke it with. And do not expect any of this to feel like strategy. Kautilya's advice to the weak, stripped of the parts that need a treasury and a fort behind them, is mostly patience with a ledger attached — which is the honest summary, and also a reminder that the man he is advising still has a treasury and a fort.
