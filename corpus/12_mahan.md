# 12. Alfred Thayer Mahan — The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660-1783

## 1. Identity

A single-volume argument, in English, by a serving officer of the United States Navy, that the rise and fall of the Atlantic powers between 1660 and 1783 is best explained by their command or loss of the sea, and that the United States of 1890 was making the same mistake France had made.

Its shape: a Preface; an **Introductory** chapter arguing that naval history yields permanent principles despite the arrival of steam; **Chapter I, "Discussion of the Elements of Sea Power,"** which is the theoretical core; then Chapters II–XIII, a narrative of the European naval wars from the accession of Charles II and Louis XIV to the end of the American Revolution; and **Chapter XIV, "Critical Discussion of the Maritime War of 1778,"** which returns to doctrine and states the book's conclusions. The Preface, the Introductory and Chapters I and XIV carry the theory. The eleven narrative chapters are evidence, and Mahan says as much: the historical thread is "intentionally slight."

**Edition used.** Project Gutenberg eBook #13529, set from the Twelfth Edition, Boston: Little, Brown and Company. The Gutenberg header carries "Copyright, 1890, by Captain A. T. Mahan" and "Copyright, 1918, by Ellen Lyle Mahan." There is no translation question: this is an English original. All quotations below were verified by grep against the local file `sources/mahan_seapower.txt` (1,311,813 bytes, CRLF line endings). Because the text is hard-wrapped, multi-line strings were verified against a whitespace-flattened copy produced by `tr -d '\r' < mahan_seapower.txt | tr '\n' ' ' | tr -s ' '`; the method is given per quotation in section 7.

**Corrections to the blog entry.**

*The blog gives the title as "The Influence of Sea Power Among History." The evidence says "upon History," because that is the word on the title page and in every running head.* The Gutenberg header reads `Title: The Influence of Sea Power Upon History, 1660-1783`, and the preposition is load-bearing: Mahan's claim is not that sea power sits among the causes of events but that it acts upon them. "Among" would concede the point the whole book exists to deny.

*The blog says "USA, 1890." Correct for publication; the writing finished earlier.* The Preface is signed "A. T. MAHAN / DECEMBER, 1889." The book therefore describes a world before the 1890 naval act and before the Spanish-American War, and its United States is a country with, on Mahan's own count, one of the three elements of sea power and no other.

*The blog says the book is "lectures at the Naval War College turned into the book." True biographically, but the book never says so, and the silence matters.* Grepping the full text for "War College" and for "Luce" returns zero hits. Stephen B. Luce, who founded the College, appointed Mahan lecturer in naval history and tactics in 1885; Mahan was president of the College from 1886 to 1889 and wrote the lectures in that period. But the published book presents itself as history, not as a course, and it strips out the pedagogical apparatus. The consequence for a reader is practical: the Introductory and Chapter I are a syllabus — a statement of what the student is about to be shown and why it will still apply — and they should be read as one, which is how the blog's reading instruction ("focus on the Introduction and Chapter I") happens to be right.

*The blog's list of the six elements is accurate, with a wording wobble that is Mahan's own.* At the head of Chapter I he enumerates: "I. Geographical Position. II. Physical Conformation, including, as connected therewith, natural productions and climate. III. Extent of Territory. IV. Number of Population. V. Character of the People. VI. Character of the Government, including therein the national institutions." The running section headings then read "V. _National Character._" and "VI. _Character of the Government._" and the Contents page prints "V. National character" and "VI. Character and policy of governments." Three spellings of the same two items, in one book.

*The blog says the book "argues for … the decisive battle." This is the entry's most consequential error, and it is an error of provenance rather than of substance.* Mahan does argue that the enemy's fleet is the proper objective and that fleets must concentrate. But the phrase "decisive battle" occurs twice in the whole book (once of the Metaurus in the Introductory, once of Rodney in 1782), and never as the name of a doctrine; "decisive action" occurs a further eight times, mostly in reported French complaints about being forbidden to seek one. Mahan's own operative vocabulary is **"the objective"** and **"control of the sea."** The "decisive battle" is what navies made of Mahan, not a term he coined here — a distinction that matters, because the doctrine built on it in Tokyo and Berlin was in each case a caricature (section 6).

*The blog says "Read by Roosevelt, Wilhelm II, the Japanese navy." Verified, with different weights.* Roosevelt wrote to Mahan on 12 May 1890, days after publication, and reviewed the book in *The Atlantic Monthly* for October 1890. Wilhelm II telegraphed Poultney Bigelow on 26 May 1894: "I am just now not reading but devouring Captain Mahan's book; and am trying to learn it by heart." The Japanese reading was mediated by translation and by Satō Tetsutarō, and is best documented in the secondary literature rather than in anything Mahan wrote (section 6).

## 2. The world the text assumes

**An economy in which water carriage is decisively cheaper than land carriage.** Mahan opens Chapter I with it, and concedes at once that it was "yet more marked in a period when roads were few and very bad" — that is, in the era he is about to narrate rather than in 1890. Everything downstream depends on the premise that the cheapest way to move goods is by sea, so that whoever can close the sea can throttle an economy. Rail and, later, air and pipeline erode this without destroying it.

**Mercantilism as the default political economy.** Monopoly, navigation acts, prohibitory regulation; colonies understood by every home government as "a cow to be milked"; the assumption that trade is a fixed pie to be fought over. Mahan writes at the end of this world and partly against it — he is a protectionist about shipping and a realist about the rest — but his historical actors are all mercantilists and his analysis takes their premises as data.

**A technological vacuum.** This is the assumption that makes the book what it is. "Steam navies have as yet made no history which can be quoted as decisive in its teaching. Of the one we have much experimental knowledge; of the other, practically none." Nobody in 1889 knew what steam fleets would do. Mahan's response — reason from sailing warfare because principles outlive weapons — is not a preference but a forced move, and it drives the whole methodological argument of the Introductory.

**Surface ships and nothing else.** No aircraft, no submarines, no radio. The telegraph exists and he imagines it coordinating a blockade along a coast. Torpedoes exist and he treats them as short-range smashing weapons analogous to carronades. Mines exist and he assigns them to the army as passive defence.

**Coal as the new master constraint.** A recurring footnote theme. "It is vain to look for energetic naval operations distant from coal stations. It is equally vain to acquire distant coaling stations without maintaining a powerful navy; they will but fall into the hands of the enemy." This is the hinge on which his argument for American overseas bases turns.

**A post-1856 neutral-rights regime.** He assumes "the principle that the flag covers the goods is forever secured" and that "there will be no more paper blockades." This is why his commerce-war chapter reaches the conclusion it does: with neutral shipping protected, raiding buys even less than it used to.

**A Britain at apex and a United States with almost nothing.** England "is at the present time the greatest maritime nation in the world"; the United States has no colonies, a vanished merchant marine, undefended Gulf ports, and warships that "will be like land birds." An isthmian canal is imminent and will bring the great powers to American shores. The book is, throughout, a policy argument dressed as history, and its intended reader is an American legislator.

**National character as a stable, near-racial substance.** The English and Dutch are "radically of the same race" and are natural traders; the Spanish are avaricious, the French vain and thrifty. Mahan treats commercial aptitude as inherited and governments as able to encourage or obstruct it but rarely to create it. This is the assumption that has dated worst and that transfers least.

**A government that must be persuaded and a public that will not pay.** "Popular governments are not generally favorable to military expenditure, however necessary." The whole rhetorical architecture — the flattery of England, the pity for France, the warning to America — exists because Mahan is arguing to a legislature against the natural preference of a commercial democracy for cheapness.

## 3. Core doctrine

Eighteen principles. Loci are given by chapter and, inside Chapter I, by Mahan's roman-numbered section.

**1. Water carriage is cheaper than land carriage; therefore the sea is a common, and the routes across it are the arteries of national wealth.**
*Locus:* Chapter I, opening.
*His reasoning:* The sea presents itself first as "a great highway; or better, perhaps, of a wide common," on which "some well-worn paths show that controlling reasons have led them to choose certain lines of travel rather than others." Those paths are trade routes, and they exist for economic reasons, not military ones.
*Limits:* He concedes the advantage was greater in the seventeenth century than in his own, and that internal coasting trade had by 1890 become a convenience rather than a necessity in most civilized countries.

**2. Sea power has three links: production, shipping, colonies — in that order.**
*Locus:* Chapter I.
*His reasoning:* A country must have something to exchange; it must have hulls to carry the exchange; it must have secure points at the far end where goods accumulate and ships shelter. "In these three things … is to be found the key to much of the history, as well as of the policy, of nations bordering upon the sea."
*Limits:* He immediately subordinates policy to nature: the history of seaboard nations "has been less determined by the shrewdness and foresight of governments than by conditions of position, extent, configuration, number and character of their people" — while conceding that individual men have at times modified this greatly.

**3. The navy is a derivative of commerce, not its cause; a fleet without a merchant marine behind it has no root.**
*Locus:* Chapter I; restated Chapter I §VI.
*His reasoning:* "The necessity of a navy, in the restricted sense of the word, springs, therefore, from the existence of a peaceful shipping, and disappears with it." And: "History has proved that such a purely military sea power can be built up by a despot, as was done by Louis XIV.; but though so fair seeming, experience showed that his navy was like a growth which having no root soon withers away."
*Limits:* The exception is explicit — "except in the case of a nation which has aggressive tendencies, and keeps up a navy merely as a branch of the military establishment." A purely aggressive state can have a rootless navy; it simply cannot keep one.

**4. Six conditions govern how much sea power a nation can have.**
*Locus:* Chapter I, §§I–VI.
*His reasoning:* Geographical position, physical conformation, extent of territory, number of population, national character, character of government. The first four are given; the fifth is inherited; only the sixth is chosen. His method is to run each of England, Holland, France, Spain and the United States through the list and read off the result.
*Limits:* He disavows exhaustiveness ("the aim here is not an exhaustive discussion") and he concedes that individual action modifies the outcome. Both concessions are routinely dropped by his readers.

**5. A nation with only one frontier concentrates; a nation with two disperses.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §I.
*His reasoning:* England, "neither forced to defend itself by land nor induced to seek extension of its territory by way of the land," has "by the very unity of its aim directed upon the sea, an advantage." France, touching both Mediterranean and Atlantic, can unite her fleets only through the Straits of Gibraltar, "in attempting which they have often risked and sometimes suffered loss."
*Limits:* Colonial empire sacrifices this advantage, and Mahan says England made the sacrifice knowingly and rightly — "the gain was greater than the loss, as the event proved." Spain, with the same dispersion and no fleet, "but offered so many points for insult and injury."

**6. Harbours are wealth in peace and doors in war.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §II.
*His reasoning:* "Numerous and deep harbors are a source of strength and wealth, and doubly so if they are the outlets of navigable streams … but by their very accessibility they become a source of weakness in war, if not properly defended." The Thames in 1667, the Mississippi in 1862, the Chesapeake in 1814.
*Limits:* The rule is conditional on defence: the same conformation that ruined the Confederacy would have strengthened it had it had the population and the navy.

**7. Coastline is a liability unless population is proportioned to it.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §III.
*His reasoning:* "A country is in this like a fortress; the garrison must be proportioned to the _enceinte_." The Union blockade of the South succeeded not because such blockades are easy but because the South was thinly peopled and unseafaring; "it would have been an impossible feat had the Southerners been more numerous, and a nation of seamen."
*Limits:* He is explicit that the lesson runs both ways — the same inlets that were the South's potential strength became the channels of its destruction.

**8. Reserve decides long wars, and time is the supreme factor.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §IV.
*His reasoning:* France could man fifty ships-of-the-line at once in 1778 and never exceeded seventy-one; England struggled to man forty and had a hundred and twenty by 1782. What separates them is depth: "organized reserve first, then reserve of seafaring population, reserve of mechanical skill, reserve of wealth." And: "If time be, as is everywhere admitted, a supreme factor in war, it behooves countries whose genius is essentially not military … to see to it that they are at least strong enough to gain the time necessary."
*Limits:* He states the counter-case honestly — Ulm, Austerlitz, Jena, Trafalgar all suggest a single campaign can settle matters — and then argues those were blows by exceptional men against already-demoralised opponents. He concedes "sea warfare has given no answer."

**9. Commercial aptitude, not martial spirit, is the national character that produces sea power.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §V.
*His reasoning:* Spain and Portugal sought gold and got neither commerce nor navy; the English and Dutch "sought riches not by the sword but by labor … for thus they took the longest, instead of what seemed the shortest, road to wealth." Wealth in a few treasure-ships can be cut by one stroke; wealth in thousands of hulls "can stand many a cruel shock and lose many a goodly bough without the life being touched."
*Limits:* "Save the Romans, there is no marked instance to the contrary" — he knows of one exception and does not explain it away. He also concedes that "something does not grow from nothing": Dutch poverty drove them to sea, but poverty alone did not make them rich.

**10. Colonies grow from the colonist, not from the ministry.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §V.
*His reasoning:* "The character of the colonist, not the care of the home government, is the principle of the colony's growth." France systematised more carefully than England and colonised worse; the Dutch planted brilliantly and stalled, because they sought trade without political ambition and so "killed the natural principle of growth."
*Limits:* "The military administration, however, so long as it remains a colony, is the proper and necessary attribute of the home government." Growth is to be left alone; defence is not.

**11. Government can favour, obstruct, or force sea power — but forced growth withers when the favour stops.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §VI.
*His reasoning:* Colbert took the French navy from thirty ships in 1661 to 196 in 1671, with dockyards more efficient than England's. "Yet all this wonderful growth, forced by the action of the government, withered away like Jonah's gourd when the government's favor was withdrawn. Time was not allowed for its roots to strike down deep into the life of the nation."
*Limits:* Despotism is faster. "Despotic power, wielded with judgment and consistency, has created at times a great sea commerce and a brilliant navy with greater directness than can be reached by the slower processes of a free people. The difficulty in the latter case is to insure perseverance after the death of a particular despot."

**12. Strategy is permanent; tactics perish. A principle outranks a precedent.**
*Locus:* Introductory.
*His reasoning:* "But a precedent is different from and less valuable than a principle. The former may be originally faulty, or may cease to apply through change of circumstances; the latter has its root in the essential nature of things." Hence: "From time to time the superstructure of tactics has to be altered or wholly torn down; but the old foundations of strategy so far remain, as though laid upon a rock."
*Limits:* He does not exempt tactics from study — the tactical value is "rather in the mental training, in the forming of correct tactical habits of thought, than in supplying models for close imitation." And he concedes to Morogues that naval tactics rest on conditions that change, disputing only the further claim that they rest on no invariable principles.

**13. Naval strategy operates in peace as well as war.**
*Locus:* Introductory.
*His reasoning:* He endorses a French author's definition: naval strategy "differs from military strategy in that it is as necessary in peace as in war. Indeed, in peace it may gain its most decisive victories by occupying in a country, either by purchase or treaty, excellent positions which would perhaps hardly be got by war." Cyprus and Egypt are his examples. Hence: "Naval strategy has indeed for its end to found, support, and increase, as well in peace as in war, the sea power of a country."
*Limits:* This sentence is a quotation Mahan adopts, not a sentence he composed; he attributes it to "a recent French author" whom he does not name.

**14. The objective is not the object.**
*Locus:* Chapter XIV.
*His reasoning:* "In a war undertaken for any object, even if that object be the possession of a particular territory or position, an attack directly upon the place coveted may not be, from the military point of view, the best means of obtaining it. The end upon which the military operations are directed may therefore be other than the object which the belligerent government wishes to obtain, and it has received a name of its own,—the objective." Spain wanted Gibraltar and besieged Gibraltar for three years; Mahan's counter-question, put in the Introductory, is whether Gibraltar would not have fallen faster by controlling the English Channel.
*Limits:* He offers this as a method of criticism, not a formula: first state each side's object, then ask whether the chosen objective would have secured it, then judge the movements.

**15. The enemy's fleet is the true objective, because the fleet is what holds the communications.**
*Locus:* Chapter XIV; illustrated in the Introductory by the Second Punic War.
*His reasoning:* "In North America and India sound military policy pointed out, as the true objective, the enemy's fleet, upon which also depended the communications with the mother-countries." And of the West Indies: "The key of the situation, it must be repeated, was in the fleet." Rome beat Hannibal not by battle but by holding the sea between Spain and Italy, so that Hasdrubal had to come by land and arrived two hundred miles from his brother.
*Limits:* He states the qualification himself, and it is large: "The control of the sea, however real, does not imply that an enemy's single ships or small squadrons cannot steal out of port … history has shown that such evasions are always possible, to some extent, to the weaker party, however great the inequality of naval strength." Control forbids sustained communication; it does not forbid raids.

**16. Concentrate the battle force; disperse only for raiding — and raiding does not decide.**
*Locus:* Chapter I §I; Chapter XIV; final paragraph.
*His reasoning:* "The essence of the one is concentration of effort, whereas for commerce-destroying diffusion of effort is the rule. Commerce-destroyers scatter, that they may see and seize more prey." The book's last sentence makes the contrast its verdict: "Commerce-destroying by independent cruisers depends upon wide dissemination of force. Commerce-destroying through control of a strategic centre by a great fleet depends upon concentration of force. Regarded as a primary, not as a secondary, operation, the former is condemned, the latter justified, by the experience of centuries."
*Limits:* Concentration is subject to the defender's dilemma, which he states sympathetically: the side on the defensive is "anxious about many points" and disperses because it cannot know where the blow falls. He calls this an explanation, not a justification.

**17. Commerce-destroying cannot win a war alone; unsupported, it ends in the capture of the cruisers.**
*Locus:* Chapter II (after the Medway raid of 1667); Chapter VIII, footnote 112; Chapter XIV, footnote 245.
*His reasoning:* "Such a war, however, cannot stand alone; it must be _supported_, to use the military phrase; unsubstantial and evanescent in itself, it cannot reach far from its base." The French privateers of 1693 succeeded because the allied battle fleets were pinned elsewhere; when the fleets were free, the cruisers were taken. English trade grew through the whole of the Seven Years' War while French privateers took 2,500 prizes. "Such injuries, unaccompanied by others, are more irritating than weakening."
*Limits:* He concedes it is "doubtless a most important secondary operation of naval war, and is not likely to be abandoned till war itself shall cease," and he concedes the exception: against an enemy whose wealth travels in a few treasure-ships, "the sinew of war may perhaps be cut by a stroke."

**18. Defence at sea is properly offensive; passive defence belongs to the army.**
*Locus:* Chapter I, footnote 15.
*His reasoning:* "There is defence pure and simple, which strengthens itself and awaits attack. This may be called passive defence. On the other hand, there is a view of defence which asserts that safety for one's self, the real object of defensive preparation, is best secured by attacking the enemy." England defended her coasts by stationing fleets off French ports. "Passive defences belong to the army; everything that moves in the water to the navy, which has the prerogative of the offensive defence."
*Limits:* He insists the distinction is of purpose, not method: the same blockade is defensive or offensive depending on whether its object is the enemy's fleet or the enemy's country. He also endorses Monk's harder version — "the nation that would rule upon the sea must always attack" — while noting that Monk's own application of it in 1666, attacking at heavy odds after detaching a quarter of his fleet, "is not war."

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

Mahan addresses the weak actor **directly, at length, and by name** — the actor is the United States of 1890, and the book is an argument to it. This is not inference. "In short, the United States has only one link of the three." No colonies, no merchant marine, no station where a warship can coal. "Having therefore no foreign establishments, either colonial or military, the ships of war of the United States, in war, will be like land birds, unable to fly far from their own shores."

What he tells that actor is mostly unwelcome.

**He forbids the cheap strategy first.** The obvious move for a poor navy is commerce raiding, and Mahan knows exactly why: it "involves only the maintenance of a few swift cruisers and can be backed by the spirit of greed in a nation, fitting out privateers without direct expense to the State," and so "possesses the specious attractions which economy always presents." He then spends the rest of the book demonstrating it does not work, and names the failure mode precisely: it is "a delusion, and a most dangerous delusion, when presented in the fascinating garb of cheapness to the representatives of a people." He goes further and denies the United States even the geography for it — "her geographical position is therefore singularly disadvantageous for carrying on successful commerce-destroying, unless she find bases in the ports of an ally."

**He concedes the weak can always evade.** "History has shown that such evasions are always possible, to some extent, to the weaker party, however great the inequality of naval strength." Bomilcar landed 4,000 men and elephants in Italy while Rome held the sea. But Mahan's point in conceding this is to deny the inference from it: evasion is compatible with the enemy's total control, and "they do tend to create a natural impression that such help could have been given" when it could not.

**He gives the weak three things that actually work.**

*Time.* The reserve argument in Chapter I §IV is entirely an argument for the weaker party. If you can survive the first campaign, latent strength enters the account; if you cannot, "the most magnificent possibilities of natural power will not save it from humiliating conditions." Everything turns on the runway.

*Position.* Position is cheap and it is the one element in his six that a weak actor can sometimes buy. This is the force of principle 13: in peace, strategy "may gain its most decisive victories by occupying in a country, either by purchase or treaty, excellent positions which would perhaps hardly be got by war." England's central position let inferior numbers interpose between two enemies; Rome's interior line was the sea itself.

*Local superiority against the part that cannot be helped.* This is the only tactical doctrine in the book addressed to inferiority, and he states it twice. Of Nelson at the Nile: the underlying principle is "to choose that part of the enemy's order which can least easily be helped, and to attack it with superior forces." Of Jervis at Cape St Vincent, "when with fifteen ships he won a victory over twenty-seven," the same principle, applied to a fleet under way rather than at anchor.

**One passage speaks to having nothing at all, and I flag it as a quotation within a quotation.** Describing the French navy under Louis XV, rotting at its moorings, Mahan quotes a French officer: "The sad condition of the navy in the reign of Louis XV., by closing to officers the brilliant career of bold enterprises and successful battles, forced them to fall back upon themselves. They drew from study the knowledge they were to put to the proof some years later, thus putting into practice that fine saying of Montesquieu, 'Adversity is our mother, Prosperity our step-mother.'" Mahan endorses it: Morogues wrote the first original work on naval tactics since Hoste "in days when France had no fleet, and was unable so much as to raise her head at sea." England, then supreme, had no equivalent book.

**And one remark is, for a small actor, the best sentence in the book.** Mahan observes that the gap between a change of weapons and the corresponding change of tactics is always "unduly long," because "an improvement of weapons is due to the energy of one or two men, while changes in tactics have to overcome the inertia of a conservative class." Then: "History shows that it is vain to hope that military men generally will be at the pains to do this, but that the one who does will go into battle with a great advantage,—a lesson in itself of no mean value." The advantage is available to anyone who does work that the established will not do. It is the only structural edge the book concedes to the outsider, and Mahan states it flatly, without encouragement.

**What he never does is address an individual.** Every actor in this book is a state, a fleet, or an admiral commanding a fleet. Any application to a person is my inference and is marked as such in section 8.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Central, and the book's polemical purpose. His diagnosis of why preparation fails is psychological and democratic: "a peaceful, gain-loving nation is not far-sighted, and far-sightedness is needed for adequate military preparation." He quotes De Witt on the Dutch — "unless danger stares them in the face, they are indisposed to lay out money for their own defence" — and applies it to America without softening: "That our own country is open to the same reproach, is patent to all the world."

**Knowledge and intelligence.** Thin. He has no doctrine of scouting, spies or deception-detection. What he has is a doctrine of *judgement*: Nelson "did not, indeed, fathom the intentions of Napoleon," but "it is insight enough to fasten on the key of a situation; and this Nelson rightly saw was the fleet, not the station." Knowing the right thing to watch substitutes for knowing the enemy's plan.

**Timing.** Real but scattered. "If time be, as is everywhere admitted, a supreme factor in war." Trafalgar was "separated by a space of two months from the extensive movements of which it was nevertheless the outcome." The West Indian campaigning season governed when fleets could combine. He has no theory of the moment; he has a theory of duration.

**Terrain and position.** The strongest material in the book. Sections I and II of Chapter I are a treatise on maritime geography: single versus double frontage, concentration versus dispersion, central position and interior lines, chokepoints (the Channel, the Straits, Suez, the coming isthmian canal), the value of a harbour and the danger of one. He also gives the method: study the Mediterranean, whose strategic conditions "have received ample illustration," as "an excellent prelude to a similar study of the Caribbean, which has comparatively little history."

**Deception and information.** Effectively **silent**. The nearest thing is his account of Napoleon's 1805 West Indies feint, designed so that "confusion and distraction would arise from ignorance of the destination of the French squadrons" — and his interest there is in why it failed. He offers no positive doctrine of deception.

**Alliances.** Substantial and bitter. France and Holland were natural allies and destroyed each other: "France was helping to destroy a probable, and certainly an indispensable, ally." England made Holland carry the land war and took the sea war, and at Utrecht "Holland, having established no sea claim, gained nothing." Spain pursued Gibraltar instead of the common objective, "a policy as shortsighted as it was selfish." His rule, stated by example rather than maxim: allies who pursue their own object separately lose the war together.

**Logistics and economy.** The foundation of the whole book. Production is the first link; wealth is the sinew; coal is the modern chain. The navy "sweeps the sea for the service of the land." He is equally clear that bases and fleets are mutually dependent: "the service between the bases and the mobile force between the ports and the fleets is mutual."

**Morale and discipline.** Present, mostly as pathology. The French defensive system "sapped moral power to save material resources" and produced, over three-quarters of a century, "acts of indiscipline and defection under fire." Lending royal ships to privateering firms "would be demoralizing to any military service." On the other side, the English had "the single eye that looked to England's naval prestige," which he credits with carrying them through "many blunders through the centuries."

**The decisive point.** Yes, and it is his core: the enemy's fleet, because it holds the communications; and within a battle, the part of the enemy's order that can least easily be helped. Also the converse rule for exploitation: "the true way to secure ulterior objects is to defeat the force which threatens them."

**The culminating point and when to stop.** Essentially **silent**, and the silence is characteristic. Mahan has no theory of over-extension in the Clausewitzian sense. His complaint runs the other way: his criticism of Rodney after 12 April 1782 is that the victory was not followed up hard enough. Where Clausewitz warns about going too far, Mahan warns about stopping too soon.

**When not to fight.** Thin, and he is hostile to the question. He records without endorsement "the inexpediency of such inferior numbers risking a decisive action," and he acknowledges that a lee-gage fleet could rationally decline. But his settled position is Monk's: the ruling power must always attack, and the French habit of declining was the cause of their ruin. He does add one qualification against headlong contact: a fleet "must close with its enemy, but not until some advantage has been obtained for the collision, which will usually be gained by manoeuvring."

**Defence versus offence.** His clearest single statement, in footnote 15 to Chapter I (see principle 18). Passive defence is fortification and mines; active defence is the fleet off the enemy's port; the two differ in purpose, not method; and the navy owns the second.

**The character required of the commander.** Present but undeveloped in this volume. Nelson supplies the portrait: "tenacity of purpose and untiring energy in execution can repair a first mistake and baffle deeply laid plans," and the "greatness of mind" to abandon his assigned station on his own responsibility. And the book's last line of theory makes character permanent where everything else changes: "among all changes, the nature of man remains much the same; the personal equation, though uncertain in quantity and quality in the particular instance, is sure always to be found."

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The decisive-battle doctrine.** The most damaging misreading, and it was operational rather than academic. In Japan, Mahan was translated and absorbed into *kantai kessen* — the doctrine that a war against a superior power could be won in one great fleet action fought at a chosen point. Satō Tetsutarō is the theorist usually named for its Japanese formulation. David C. Evans and Mark R. Peattie, *Kaigun: Strategy, Tactics, and Technology in the Imperial Japanese Navy, 1887–1941* (Naval Institute Press, 1997), is the standard treatment; they summarise the doctrine as "the concept of the decisive fleet engagement determined by big guns; the validity of a strategy of attrition against a numerically superior enemy; the preference for quality over quantity in naval weaponry; and the importance of nighttime torpedo tactics." Its price was the corollary: resources went to the battle line and not to escorts, so Japanese merchant shipping was destroyed by American submarines while the fleet waited for a battle that arrived too late and on the wrong terms. Mahan did not teach the neglect of trade protection — his entire commerce chapter says that a trading nation's life is in its shipping — but the doctrine his readers extracted did.

**Tirpitz's risk fleet.** Wilhelm II "devoured" the book in 1894 and had it circulated in the German navy; Tirpitz built a battle fleet whose theory of victory was a decisive North Sea action. The fleet fought once inconclusively, spent the war in harbour, and in the meantime converted Britain from a possible partner into a certain enemy. Michael Epkenhans's work on German naval strategy is the accessible treatment.

**The elided clause.** The most-quoted sentence in Mahan is routinely truncated. The full text reads: "it is the possession of that overbearing power on the sea which drives the enemy's flag from it, **or allows it to appear only as a fugitive**." Quoters stop at "from it," which converts a statement about degree into a statement about absolutes. The point is made in a *U.S. Naval Institute Proceedings* article, "Mahan's Illusory Command of the Seas" (June 2021, vol. 147/6/1,420); I could not open the article directly — usni.org returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch — so I have the observation from a search abstract, but I verified the full sentence myself against the primary text (section 7, quotation 4).

**Corbett's correction, 1911.** The serious contemporary rebuttal, and the one that has worn best. Julian Corbett, *Some Principles of Maritime Strategy*, attacks the assumption that the sea is normally commanded by somebody: "the most common situation in naval war is that neither side has the command; that the normal position is not a commanded sea, but an uncommanded sea … the command is normally in dispute. It is this state of dispute with which naval strategy is most nearly concerned." He also corrects the "seek out the enemy's fleet" maxim, and records that Mahan himself conceded ground: "Captain Mahan has since modified this maxim as follows:—'Seek out the enemy's fleet, if you are sure of getting contact.' A truer maxim would seem to be 'Seek contact with the enemy's fleet in the most certain and favourable manner that is open to you.'" (Both passages verified in the local cache, `sources/corbett_maritime.txt`.)

**Determinism and selective history.** The six elements read as a checklist that makes British supremacy inevitable, and the eleven narrative chapters select for the thesis: Mahan's own Preface admits the historical thread is "intentionally slight," and the chapters function as illustration rather than test. The charge is fair against the reputation, less fair against the text, which contains its own hedge — "the wise or unwise action of individual men has at certain periods had a great modifying influence upon the growth of sea power." Jon Tetsuro Sumida, *Inventing Grand Strategy and Teaching Command: The Classic Works of Alfred Thayer Mahan Reconsidered* (Johns Hopkins / Woodrow Wilson Center Press, 1997), presses this further: he argues Mahan has been gravely misread, that Mahan cared more about forming an officer's judgement through historical study than about transmitting principles, and that "decisive" in Mahan's usage means indispensable to the eventual outcome rather than war-ending.

**Economics reversed.** Paul Kennedy, *The Rise and Fall of British Naval Mastery* (1976), argues that British naval strength tracked British economic and industrial growth rather than producing it, and that Mahan's battle-fleet-centred account inverts the causal arrow; Kennedy also denies that the British were natural "sons of the waves."

**The land-power answers.** Halford Mackinder, "The Geographical Pivot of History" (1904), and Nicholas Spykman, *The Geography of the Peace* (1944), are the standing geopolitical rebuttals — that the Eurasian heartland, or the rimland where sea and land meet, and not the sea, is the decisive theatre. Peter Paret's objection is narrower and harder: Mahan neglects diplomacy and land forces, and does not explain the rise of land empires.

**Mahan as imperial polemic.** Charles Beard, *The Navy: Defense or Portent?* (1932), read Mahanian navalism as a cause of war rather than a defence against it. Walter LaFeber, *The New Empire* (1963), placed Mahan among the conscious architects of American overseas empire. Andrew Lambert, *Seapower States* (2018), reads *Influence* as polemic rather than history and separates "seapower states" — a political type — from states that merely possess navies, a distinction Mahan blurs.

**Technology as a strategic, not merely tactical, variable.** Wayne P. Hughes, *Fleet Tactics* (1986; later editions 2000, 2018), argues that technological change alters the strategic principles Mahan treated as constant, not just the tactics. This is a direct hit on principle 12.

**Migration into policy vocabulary.** Barry Posen, "Command of the Commons: The Military Foundation of U.S. Hegemony," *International Security* 28:1 (Summer 2003), 5–46, carries Mahan's word "command" and his image of the sea as a common into an argument about air, sea and space. This is adaptation rather than misuse, but it is how the vocabulary spread.

**Business and self-help literature.** Here I must be honest about the limits of what I verified. I searched for scholarship specifically on Mahan's misuse in management writing and found none. What I can state: the business-strategy literature has long borrowed the military metaphor wholesale — the standing critique is that writers "look for reassuring parallels in business metaphors instead of troubling differences" (Kaihan Krippendorff and Mihnea Moldoveanu, "The Fruitful Flaws of Strategy Metaphors," *Harvard Business Review*, September 2003, which is a general critique and does not name Mahan) — and that the Mahan sentences that circulate in this register are the "overbearing power" clause and "concentration of force," both detached from the conditions Mahan attaches to them. The specific corporate misreading, on the evidence I have, is structural rather than textual: Mahan is quoted for the doctrine that you should dominate a space, by writers who omit that his entire argument is that the dominating instrument is downstream of production and cannot be built first.

**False and unverifiable attributions.**
- *"Whoever rules the waves rules the world."* Widely attributed to Mahan. It is **not in this book**: grep for "rules the waves," "rules the world" and "rule the world" in `sources/mahan_seapower.txt` returns zero hits. I have found no primary source for it anywhere in Mahan and treat it as spurious.
- *"Those far distant, storm-beaten ships, upon which the Grand Army never looked, stood between it and the dominion of the world."* This one is genuinely Mahan's, but it is **not from this book** — grep for "storm-beaten" and "Grand Army" returns zero hits in the 1890 text. It belongs to the sequel, *The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire, 1793–1812* (1892). It is constantly cited as though it came from *Influence … 1660-1783*.
- *"Naval strategy has indeed for its end to found, support, and increase … the sea power of a country."* Quoted everywhere as Mahan's definition. It is in this book, but Mahan presents it in quotation marks as the words of "a recent French author" whom he does not name. He adopts it; he did not write it.
- *"The nation that would rule upon the sea must always attack."* Mahan attributes this to Monk, in indirect speech, and does not quote him directly. Treat it as Mahan reporting Monk, not as either man's verbatim sentence.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Read as a manual for an individual, this book is unusual in the thirteen: it is the one written *to* a weak actor rather than about one, and almost everything it tells that actor is a refusal.

**The three links transfer, and the order is the whole lesson.** Production, then shipping, then positions. A man with nothing has none of them, and the temptation is always to acquire the last one first, because it is the visible one. Mahan's case against the American navy of 1890 is exactly this: it had the fighting arm without the earning arm, and such a thing "will be like land birds." His case against Colbert is the same at a higher level of achievement: 196 ships in ten years, dockyards better than England's, and all of it gone within a reign because "time was not allowed for its roots to strike down deep." The transfer is brutal and specific. The credential, the title, the following, the office — these are the navy. They are downstream of production. Built first, they last exactly as long as the patron's favour, and nothing about how impressive they look while they stand contradicts this. A man who begins with nothing must begin by making something that other people actually want to exchange for, and must accept that this is slow.

**The demolition of commerce-raiding is the most useful thing in the book, because it names the strategy the poor actually choose.** Mahan's account of why privateering is attractive is an account of a man with no capital: it "involves only the maintenance of a few swift cruisers," it can be financed by other people's greed, it produces visible results immediately, and it costs nothing. His verdict is that it produces injury without decision — "more irritating than weakening" — and that unsupported, it ends with the cruisers captured. The modern forms are easy to name: attacking incumbents rather than building an alternative to them, harvesting attention off other people's traffic, arbitrage that depends on someone else's system continuing to run, criticism as a career. These work, in the narrow sense: they take prizes. What Mahan shows across a century of evidence is that they do not change the balance, because the party whose traffic you are taking is meanwhile growing, and because the moment his main force is free it comes for you. And his condition is precise and testable: raiding pays only while a real force elsewhere is pinning the enemy. If you have no such force, you are not running a strategy; you are running a harvest that ends when someone notices.

**Time is the resource, and the reserve argument is about runway.** Mahan's ranking of reserves — "organized reserve first, then reserve of seafaring population, reserve of mechanical skill, reserve of wealth" — is worth copying exactly, because it says that what is already trained and mobilised beats what is merely latent. Latent capacity you cannot deploy inside the relevant time is worth nothing in that war. For a man: an existing skill that can earn this month outranks a better skill that would take three years; money in hand outranks equity; a low burn rate is a strategic asset and not a personal virtue. And the governing sentence stands: if you can survive the first campaign, your latent strength enters the account; if you cannot, "the most magnificent possibilities of natural power will not save it."

**Position transfers well, because position is the cheapest of the six elements.** Mahan's geography is about where traffic already passes and whether you sit on one front or three. England beat France not by force but by having a single frontier while France had two and could unite its fleets only through a strait an enemy held. Applied: work where the exchange already happens rather than where you would prefer it to happen; take on one theatre rather than three; and note that in peace, positions can be *bought* — "in peace it may gain its most decisive victories by occupying in a country, either by purchase or treaty, excellent positions which would perhaps hardly be got by war." A place acquired cheaply in a quiet period is worth more than a battle won in a loud one.

**Concentration, because a man with nothing has exactly one unit of force.** The book's last sentence sets diffusion against concentration and condemns diffusion as a primary operation. The man with no resources has no business dispersing the resources he has across several attempts in the hope of seeing more prey. Diffusion is a harvesting posture. It is correct for harvesting and wrong for deciding anything.

**The objective is not the object.** Mahan's formal statement — the thing you attack may not be the thing you want — is the best analytical tool in the book, and his own worked example is the right shape: Spain wanted Gibraltar and besieged Gibraltar for three years and failed; the Channel might have delivered it. Applied: state what you actually want; then ask what, if defeated or taken, delivers it; then check whether those are the same thing. They usually are not, and attacking the object directly is the commonest error of people with few resources, because the object is the only thing they can see.

**Local superiority against the part that cannot be helped.** Jervis, fifteen against twenty-seven. This is the only doctrine of inferiority in the book and it is exact: not general superiority, which you will never have, but overwhelming local superiority against an isolated segment that support cannot reach in time. The segment has to be genuinely isolated — that is the whole condition — and identifying which segment is isolated is the work.

**The inertia advantage, stated without encouragement.** Mahan observes that weapons change faster than tactics because "an improvement of weapons is due to the energy of one or two men, while changes in tactics have to overcome the inertia of a conservative class," and that "the one who does" the work of adaptation "will go into battle with a great advantage." This is the only structural edge the book grants an outsider, and it is real because it is an edge of *absence*: a man with nothing has no institution to convince, no sunk cost to defend, no class to offend. It is also small, temporary, and useless without principle 2 — an adaptation advantage still has to be attached to something you produce.

**Study when you cannot act — with the ending included.** The French officers of the 1750s, with no fleet, "drew from study the knowledge they were to put to the proof some years later." It is the passage in this book that speaks most directly to a man who has nothing to operate with, and it should be taken with its outcome attached: they studied for twenty years, produced the best tactical literature in Europe, went to sea in 1778, fought honourably, and still lost the empire, because study was never the missing link — production, shipping and government were. Take the discipline. Do not take it as a promise.

**What does not transfer.**

*The six elements as a self-diagnosis.* Four of them — position, conformation, extent, population — are fixed properties of a state and have no personal analogue. Worse, used on a person the framework becomes deterministic in exactly the way Mahan's critics say the book is: it tells you what you are and implies you cannot change it. That is an excuse with a bibliography.

*National character.* The account of why the Dutch prospered and the Spanish did not is moralised Victorian ethnography — avarice, vanity, thrift, race. As an explanation of other people it is unreliable; as an explanation of yourself it is poison, because it converts a position into a nature.

*The colonial system.* Mahan's colonies are expropriation, and he says so without embarrassment: the colony "became to the home country a cow to be milked." Nothing in that chapter describes a decent relation between a person and other people, and the structural lesson it carries — that you need secure positions at the far end of your traffic — can be taken without any of the rest of it.

*Scale and horizon.* Colbert's navy took twenty-two years to build and one reign to lose. England's consistency of policy ran over a century and depended on a hereditary governing class that "readily takes up and carries on a sound political tradition." These are state timescales with state instruments. The specific prescriptions — acquire bases, fortify terminals, maintain a reserve fleet — assume resources no individual has, and the man who tries to imitate the form without the resources is building Colbert's navy.

*"Command."* Corbett's correction applies harder to a person than to a navy. The normal condition is contested, not commanded; nobody achieves command of a field, and planning as though command is the goal produces the Tirpitz error — building an instrument whose only use is a decisive engagement that never comes. Mahan's own text is less absolute than his reputation, and the clause everyone cuts — "or allows it to appear only as a fugitive" — is the clause that admits it.

*The decisive battle, in any form.* There is no single engagement that settles a life. The navies that took this reading from Mahan lost wars with it.

**The unflattering summary.** This book tells a man with nothing that the things he can afford are the things that do not work, that the thing that works is slow and invisible for years, and that the instrument he is tempted to acquire first is the one that cannot stand without the thing he does not yet have. Of the eighteen principles, four transfer to him with real force — build production before instrument; do not mistake raiding for strategy; hold position and concentrate; buy time. The rest are either fixed conditions he cannot alter or state machinery he cannot operate. It is a short list from a five-hundred-page book, and it is short for the reason Mahan gives everywhere: most of sea power is not strategy at all, but the slow accumulation of something worth carrying.
