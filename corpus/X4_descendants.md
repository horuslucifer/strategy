# X4. Descendants — the strategic thinkers after the thirteen texts

## 0. What this file is

The thirteen texts of `00_FOUNDATION.md` stop at Corbett in 1911. Everything written about strategy since — and a few things written alongside the canon that the list omits — has to be accounted for, or the book is open to the charge that it ignored the last century because the last century is harder to read.

Each thinker below is treated as an **annex candidate** and asked three questions in order: what does this one add that the canon lacks; what does it merely restate in newer vocabulary; and does a book for a man who has just entered the modern world with nothing actually need it. The third question decides. A thinker can matter to the history of strategy and still be dead weight in a book whose reader commands nobody and has no second life to spend on a failed campaign.

Two names here are not descendants. Jomini was Clausewitz's contemporary and rival; du Picq died in 1870. They appear because the foundation list skipped them.

### Verification method and its limits

No invented quotations. Anything not confirmed against a source actually opened is marked **UNVERIFIED** in place.

Public-domain and openly-hosted texts were cached under `sources/` and every quotation confirmed by string search against a whitespace-flattened copy, the method used in the Clausewitz and Mahan files: Jomini, du Picq, Mao, Lawrence, Fuller, Liddell Hart's 1929 book, Boyd's 1976 essay, and the FBIS *Unrestricted Warfare*. Boyd's 1986–87 briefings survive only as image scans with no text layer; they were read page by page and cited by his own hand-numbered slides. Four in-copyright works — Luttwak, Rumelt, Freedman's article, Axelrod–Hamilton — were opened during research and quoted under twenty-five words with chapter or page; working copies were kept out of `sources/`, so those citations give edition and locus, not a file path.

**Beaufre**, **Wylie** and **Schelling** could not be opened at all: they exist online only as access-restricted library scans that refuse the text layer. **Porter**'s article is paywalled and was read through the publisher's own page. Those entries carry no direct quotation and say so; the doctrine is described from substance, but this file will not print words it has not seen. The same caution applies to Moltke, where the sentence is confirmed twice over but the 1900 volume was never in front of me.

## 1. Antoine-Henri Jomini — *Précis de l'art de la guerre* (1838)

Jomini is the canon's missing twin. He and Clausewitz drew opposite conclusions from the same Napoleonic decade, and for most of the nineteenth century it was Jomini whom soldiers actually read. He offers what Clausewitz refuses: a geometry. War has a theatre; the theatre has bases, lines of operation and decisive points; success consists in getting more weight onto the decisive point than the enemy can.

He states it as one principle in four maxims, the first being "To throw by strategic movements the mass of an army, successively, upon the decisive points of a theater of war, and also upon the communications of the enemy as much as possible without compromising one's own." His definition is equally bare: "Strategy is the art of making war upon the map, and comprehends the whole theater of operations." Against the objection that war has no rules he is contemptuous — "Correct theories, founded upon right principles, sustained by actual events of wars, and added to accurate military history, will form a true school of instruction for generals."

What he adds is the vocabulary of position: interior and exterior lines, the base of operations, the idea that terrain and communications can be read as a diagram before a shot is fired. Sun Tzu knows about ground and Vegetius about roads, but neither gives a usable notation for where the leverage is.

What he restates is concentration, already in Sun Tzu, Vegetius, de Saxe and Frederick. His "one great principle" is the oldest there is, dressed in compasses.

The cost is the disease Clausewitz warned about, and the one a man with nothing is likeliest to catch: the belief that a correctly drawn diagram compels the world to comply. The system has no politics, no friction, no enemy will, and no account of what happens when the plan meets a person.

**Verdict: annex, not import.** One page. Take the decisive point and the discipline of asking where one's own communications are exposed. Leave the geometry, which promises a certainty the rest of the book exists to deny.

## 2. Charles Ardant du Picq — *Études sur le combat* (posthumous, 1880)

A French colonel who circulated a questionnaire to fellow officers asking what men actually did in battle, then was mortally wounded outside Metz in August 1870 and died in November, leaving the work unfinished. It is the canon's missing chapter on fear.

His premise is stated flatly: "Battle is the final objective of armies and man is the fundamental instrument in battle. Nothing can wisely be prescribed in an army — its personnel, organization, discipline and tactics, things which are connected like the fingers of a hand — without exact knowledge of the fundamental instrument, man, and his state of mind, his morale, at the instant of combat."

From that comes the one idea everyone remembers: "Four brave men who do not know each other will not dare to attack a lion. Four less brave, but knowing each other well, sure of their reliability and consequently of mutual aid, will attack resolutely. There is the science of the organization of armies in a nutshell." And on the permanence of the problem: "Nothing is changed in the heart of man."

What he adds is a theory of cohesion that is mechanical rather than moralising. Courage is not a quality of individuals to be exhorted; it is a property of a group in which each member is confident of the others. Vegetius and Maurice care about drill, Machiavelli about who does the fighting, but none isolates *mutual confidence between known men* as the variable deciding whether a body of troops closes or breaks. Clausewitz's moral forces are real but unresolved; du Picq resolves them into something you can build.

What he restates is the canon's insistence that armies are made in peacetime and that the moral outweighs the physical. He is more precise about the mechanism, not more original about the claim.

For a man starting with nothing this is unusually direct. He has no organisation; du Picq tells him the few people who know him well are structurally, not sentimentally, valuable, and that four reliable acquaintances outperform forty strangers on any task involving risk. It is also an honest account of fear, about which self-help literature systematically lies.

**Verdict: import.** One of the few genuine additions on this list.

## 3. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder — "Über Strategie" (1871)

Chief of the Prussian General Staff from 1857 to 1888, and author of the sentence everyone misquotes.

The popular slogan — "no plan survives contact with the enemy" — is a twentieth-century compression, not Moltke's wording. What he wrote in the 1871 essay "Über Strategie" is: **"Kein Operationsplan reicht mit einiger Sicherheit über das erste Zusammentreffen mit der feindlichen Hauptmacht hinaus."** No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main force.

*Verification note.* The German and its locus were confirmed against two independently opened sources that agree exactly: German Wikiquote (cached at `sources/moltke_de_wikiquote.html`), citing *Militärische Werke*, Band 2, Teil 2 (Berlin: Mittler & Sohn, 1900), p. 291; and Quote Investigator, which gives the same German, places the essay at pp. 287–291 of that volume, and records an 1900 *Edinburgh Review* rendering as "no plan of operations can with any certainty reach beyond the first encounter with the enemy." The 1900 volume itself was not opened: the attribution is firm, the page reported rather than inspected. The formulation "Strategie ist ein System der Aushilfen" — strategy is a system of expedients — is routinely attributed to the same essay and is probably genuine, but is **UNVERIFIED** here, appearing in neither source I opened.

The difference between the sentence and the slogan is the point. The slogan says plans are useless. Moltke says something narrower and far more useful: a plan is reliable *up to* the first collision and not past it, which argues for planning hard and for building something that can act without further orders once the plan expires. That is the origin of direction by intent. Boyd, reading Moltke through van Creveld, cites approvingly "Moltke's message 'directives' of few words" (*Organic Design*, slide 28).

What he adds is the industrial-scale planning problem and its resolution: the general staff as an institution, the directive as a form, and the explicit acceptance that a plan has a shelf life measured in hours. Clausewitz has friction; Moltke has an answer to it.

**Verdict: import, and correct the quotation in the text.** For a reader with nothing this is the most immediately useful correction on the list. It licenses planning while forbidding attachment to the plan, and names the exact moment — first contact — at which the plan stops being a source of authority.

## 4. Mao Zedong — *On Guerrilla Warfare* (1937), *On Protracted War* (1938)

The canon has almost nothing on how the weaker side wins over time. Mao supplies it as doctrine rather than anecdote.

*On Protracted War* is a set of lectures delivered at Yenan in late May 1938, arguing against both the view that China would be quickly conquered and the view that it would quickly win. He divides the war into three stages — the enemy's strategic offensive, a stalemate in which the weaker side consolidates and prepares, and the counter-offensive — and insists the second stage is where the war is actually decided.

Two passages carry it. On material inferiority: "Weapons are an important factor in war, but not the decisive factor; it is people, not things, that are decisive." On the object of fighting at all: "The object of war is specifically 'to preserve oneself and destroy the enemy'."

*On Guerrilla Warfare* supplies the base. Irregular action without a political aim is worthless: "Without a political goal, guerrilla warfare must fail, as it must, if its political objectives do not coincide with the aspirations of the people and their sympathy, co-operation, and assistance cannot be gained." And the famous figure, in Griffith's translation: "The former may be likened to water the latter to the fish who inhabit it."

What he adds is time as a weapon. The canon knows protraction — Vegetius on avoiding battle, Kautilya on waiting — but nobody in the thirteen builds a staged theory in which the weaker party deliberately trades space and years for a change in the ratio of forces, and specifies what must be built during the trade. He adds, too, that for the weak the political and the military are not separable: the base is the strategy.

What he restates is Sun Tzu, whom he read and cites. Preserve yourself, strike where the enemy is not, refuse decisive battle until the odds have moved.

**Verdict: import.** For a man with nothing the three-stage structure is the best available model of what building from zero looks like, and the warning that the middle stage is long and undramatic is worth more than any encouragement. The political content should be described, not laundered.

## 5. T. E. Lawrence — *Seven Pillars of Wisdom*, chapter XXXIII; "The Evolution of a Revolt" (1920)

Lawrence worked the weak actor's problem out while lying ill in a tent at Wadi Ais in March 1917, and wrote it up twice: as chapter XXXIII of *Seven Pillars*, and as "The Evolution of a Revolt" in *The Army Quarterly* 1, no. 1 (October 1920). Only the book was available to me; the article is **UNVERIFIED** — I could not open a copy, and the two texts, though overlapping heavily, are not identical.

The chapter breaks war into three elements: "the Algebraical element of things, a Biological element of lives, and the Psychological element of ideas." The algebraic element is the calculable part — space, time, railways, numbers — and his insight is that the Arabs' possession of unlimited space made the Turkish garrison problem arithmetically unsolvable.

On the asymmetry of assets: "We had nothing material to lose, so our best line was to defend nothing and to shoot nothing. Our cards were speed and time, not hitting power."

And on the mismatch of doctrine, the line the counter-insurgency literature has quoted ever since: the Turks and Germans "would believe that rebellion was absolute like war, and deal with it on the analogy of war. Analogy in human things was fudge, anyhow; and war upon rebellion was messy and slow, like eating soup with a knife."

What he adds is a way of *auditing your own weakness for advantages*. Having nothing to defend is not only a disadvantage; it removes a class of vulnerability the strong party cannot remove. That inversion is not in the thirteen texts, which are written almost entirely from inside establishments with cities, fleets, treasuries and reputations to protect.

What he restates is Sun Tzu on formlessness and Mao on protraction, arrived at independently and expressed better.

**Verdict: import, at one page.** The three elements and the "nothing material to lose" passage. Handle the author with care: *Seven Pillars* is a literary performance by a man managing his own legend, and the book should say so rather than treat him as a neutral witness.

## 6. B. H. Liddell Hart — *The Decisive Wars of History* (1929) / *Strategy* (1954, 1967)

The most influential English strategic writer of the twentieth century, and the one whose reputation has aged worst.

The 1929 book contains the whole thesis already: "in all the decisive campaigns the dislocation of the enemy's psychological and physical balance has been the vital prelude to a successful attempt at his overthrow. This dislocation has been produced by a strategic indirect approach, intentional or fortuitous." And the formulation that earns its keep: "In the profoundest sense, it takes the line of least resistance. The equivalent in the psychological sphere is the line of least expectation. They are the two faces of the same coin."

A small finding worth recording, because it shows the man revising himself. The famous definition — "art of distributing and applying military means to fulfill the ends of policy," which Freedman quotes from the 1967 edition and which Michael Howard called "as good as any, and better than most" — is *not* the 1929 wording. In 1929 he wrote: "the distribution and transmission of military means to fulfil the ends of policy." *Transmission* became *application*, and a clause about art was added, somewhere between editions.

What he adds is the pairing of physical and psychological approach as one object. The canon knows surprise and knows avoiding strength; Liddell Hart insists the physically unexpected route and the psychologically unexpected route are the same phenomenon seen from two sides, and that dislocation is the aim, with the battle merely collecting the result.

What he restates is Sun Tzu, at length, and he knew it.

The problem is method. The indirect approach is demonstrated by surveying twenty-five centuries of campaigns in which the winner's approach is classified as indirect and the loser's direct, which risks defining the thesis into truth. His postwar rehabilitation of the German panzer generals, partly as witnesses to his own influence, is well documented and should be mentioned rather than passed over.

**Verdict: annex, with one import.** Take dislocation and the least-resistance/least-expectation pairing. Leave the historiography, and do not present him as the discoverer of what Sun Tzu wrote.

## 7. J. F. C. Fuller — *The Foundations of the Science of War* (1926)

Fuller gave the English-speaking armies their lists of principles, and his own book is the best argument against treating them as a list.

He tells the story himself. He began from the 1909 *Field Service Regulations*, which stated only that "The fundamental principles of war are neither very numerous nor in themselves very abstruse, but the application of them is difficult, and cannot be made subject to rules." He wrote an anonymous R.U.S.I. Journal article in December 1915, adding "the principle of economy of force and the principle of co-operation" to six existing ones. By 1926 he had rebuilt the scheme; against the 1924 regulations' eight, "For the first I substituted the principle of direction, which is both more general and more accurate. For economy of force I substituted the principle of distribution, and exalted economy of force to the position of the law of war. I scrapped co-operation and introduced two new principles, those of endurance and determination."

The result is nine principles under one law: "Our directing law is economy of force, our means are our instrument, which is governed by the nine principles of war, which are, so to speak, emanations of the one law as applied by our intelligence." (The scan breaks "instru- ment" across a line; words so broken are rejoined in quotations from this OCR.)

Underneath sits the real architecture, which almost nobody imports: man "is possessed of three spheres of force; his mind works in the mental sphere, and his soul and muscles in the moral and physical spheres respectively," and every principle is applied three times, once per sphere.

What he adds is the ambition of a *system*, with the threefold sphere as its spine. What he restates is everything on the list, all of it already in Vegetius, Sun Tzu, Clausewitz or de Saxe.

The honest judgement is that the system does not work, and its wreckage — the doctrinal checklist, memorised and never applied — has done damage. Fuller kept changing the list, which tells you what it was worth. His interwar politics, including the British Union of Fascists, belong in any note that names him.

**Verdict: annex as a cautionary case.** The argument that principles are not a checklist is better made by showing Fuller building and rebuilding the checklist than by asserting it.

## 8. André Beaufre — *Introduction à la stratégie* (1963)

*No quotation in this entry. Every accessible copy is an access-restricted library scan that refuses its text layer, and this file does not print words it has not opened. What follows describes doctrine, not wording; the definition Beaufre is most often quoted for — strategy as the art of the dialectic of opposing wills using force to resolve their dispute — is **UNVERIFIED** and should not be quoted in the book unless someone checks a physical copy.*

Beaufre was a French general who watched the 1940 collapse as a young officer, then Indochina and Algeria, and wrote to explain why French strategic thought had failed twice. Freedman, in the one source I could open on the point, records that Beaufre credited Liddell Hart's 1929 book with the shock that started him, against a prewar French school running on Foch.

Three things are genuinely his. First, the insistence that strategy is *dialectical* — not the application of force to a problem but a contest between two thinking wills, each adapting to the other, which makes it categorically unlike engineering. Second, a layered scheme in which a total strategy coordinates political, economic, diplomatic and military instruments, directing overall and operational strategies beneath it; this is the ancestor of what English writers call grand strategy and whole-of-government. Third, the distinction between an exterior manoeuvre, working on the opponent's freedom of action by arranging the surrounding political field, and an interior manoeuvre, the contest at the point of decision.

What he adds is a systematic treatment of the nuclear and colonial condition, in which direct military decision is unavailable and everything must be done by indirect pressure inside a constrained field. Clausewitz assumes the option of battle; Beaufre writes for a world where it has been foreclosed.

What he restates is Clausewitz on the duel and Liddell Hart on indirectness, in a French analytic idiom.

**Verdict: skip.** Nothing here is unavailable to a reader who has Clausewitz for the dialectic and Luttwak for the paradox. The layered scheme is real but is organisational furniture, and a man with nothing has no layers.

## 9. J. C. Wylie — *Military Strategy: A General Theory of Power Control* (1967)

*No quotation. Same reason as Beaufre: the only copies are restricted scans. What follows summarises the argument, not its wording.*

Wylie was a US Navy rear admiral who wrote a very short book attempting what nobody else here attempts: a general theory holding across services, rather than the four competing service theories — continental, maritime, air, and Mao's — that he catalogued and then declined to choose between.

His indispensable contribution is the distinction between **sequential** and **cumulative** operations. A sequential campaign is a chain: each action depends on the one before, the whole has a visible line of advance, and losing a link costs the chain. A cumulative campaign is a heap: many small, individually indecisive actions, none depending on the others, whose effect is statistical and shows up only in aggregate. Submarine commerce warfare, strategic bombing and sanctions are cumulative; a land advance is sequential. The two run on different clocks; people habitually plan sequentially and then expect cumulative results; and a cumulative effort cannot by itself produce a decision, only prepare one.

His second idea, less portable, is that control is ultimately exercised by the man on the scene with a gun — a blunt way of saying every indirect instrument must eventually convert into presence at the decisive place.

What he adds is the sequential/cumulative pair, which is not in the thirteen texts in any form. Mahan and Corbett argue about commerce warfare without naming the distinction underneath the argument; Mahan's dismissal of independent commerce-destroying is exactly a claim about the limits of cumulative effort, made without the vocabulary for it.

**Verdict: import the distinction, skip the book.** For a man with nothing this is among the two or three most useful ideas in the file, because almost everything available to someone without resources is cumulative — applications, small pieces of work, relationships, reps — and the characteristic error is to expect a cumulative effort to produce a sequential-looking result on a sequential-looking schedule. A paragraph, correctly attributed, carries all of it.

## 10. Thomas Schelling — *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), *Arms and Influence* (1966)

*No quotation. Both books exist online only as restricted scans. His concepts are described below and are not in dispute, but nothing here should be quoted in the book without verification against a physical copy. Terms in quotation marks are terms of art, not quotations of sentences.*

Schelling is the most important name on this list for a reader who is weak, because he is the only one who explains rigorously how a weak party manufactures power out of structure alone.

**Commitment.** The power to constrain an opponent often comes from voluntarily destroying your own freedom of action. Make it impossible or ruinous for yourself to retreat — burn the bridge, publish the promise, put the decision beyond your own reach — and an incredible threat becomes a credible one. The core is that options are not always assets; visibly having fewer can be a source of strength.

**Focal points.** In a coordination problem without communication, people converge on solutions salient for reasons outside the logic of the game — a round number, a landmark, a tradition, a river. Salience, not optimality, does the coordinating. This is why arbitrary conventions hold and why negotiations cluster at obvious numbers.

**The threat that leaves something to chance.** Where a threat is too extreme to be credible, it can be made credible by converting it from a decision into a risk — creating a situation that might escalate beyond anyone's control. You are not threatening to act; you are threatening to lose control, which is believable in a way deliberate self-destruction is not.

**Deterrence versus compellence.** Deterrence asks the opponent not to act and can succeed invisibly; compellence asks him to act and requires visible compliance, which makes it far harder because it demands his humiliation.

What he adds is that none of this is in the canon. The thirteen texts treat bargaining as a residue of fighting. Schelling treats fighting as one move inside bargaining, and gives the structure of the bargaining.

**Verdict: import, heavily.** Commitment and focal points are directly operable by a person with no resources, which cannot be said of most of this list.

## 11. John Boyd — *Destruction and Creation* (1976), *Patterns of Conflict* (1986), *Organic Design for Command and Control* (1987)

Boyd published almost nothing and briefed relentlessly, which is why he is quoted more often than read. Start with what the briefings say, because the popular version is wrong.

The loop is first named on *Patterns of Conflict* slide 5, arriving as a conclusion about tempo, not a procedure: "Idea of fast transients suggests that, in order to win, we should operate at a faster tempo or rhythm than our adversaries — or, better yet, get inside adversary's Observation-Orientation-Decision-Action time cycle or loop." The goal, slide 7, is not speed for its own sake but to "Collapse adversary's system into confusion and disorder."

The correction to the four-step cartoon is explicit in the 1987 briefing, slide 26: "The second O, orientation — as the repository of our genetic heritage, cultural tradition, and previous experiences — is the most important part of the O-O-D-A loop since it shapes the way we observe, the way we decide, the way we act." The loop is not four equal boxes in sequence but a system in which one element conditions the rest, which is why two people looking at the same facts run different loops. (The fully drawn diagram, with orientation feeding forward and back, is in the 1996 *Essence of Winning and Losing*, which I could not open; that sketch is **UNVERIFIED** here, but slide 26 carries the correction alone.)

The same briefing explains why organisations fail: "Any command and control system that forces adherents to look inward, leads to dissolution/disintegration" (slide 21). The remedy is implicit rather than explicit coordination, "to gain a favorable mismatch in friction and time" (slide 22).

*Destruction and Creation* is the epistemology underneath, and its goal is human rather than military: "a basic aim or goal, as individuals, is to improve our capacity for independent action." Its central move argues from Gödel, Heisenberg and the second law that no system validates itself from inside — "we cannot determine the character or nature (consistency) of such a system within itself, since the system is moving irreversibly toward a higher, yet unknown, state of confusion and disorder." Your model of the world decays, and must periodically be broken apart and rebuilt from the pieces.

What he adds is tempo as a weapon and orientation as the thing under attack. What he restates is Sun Tzu, Musashi on rhythm, and Clausewitz on friction, all of which he read and credited.

**Verdict: import**, on condition the book prints the real loop and kills the four-step version.

## 12. Edward Luttwak — *Strategy: The Logic of War and Peace* (1987; rev. and enl. ed. 2001)

Luttwak's claim is large and he states it as such, in Part I, chapter 1: "the entire realm of strategy is pervaded by a paradoxical logic very different from the ordinary 'linear' logic by which we live in all other spheres of life."

The illustration is the Roman tag opening the book — "Si vis pacem, para bellum. If you want peace, prepare war" — and the observation that the same preparation which deters an attack can also provoke one, or invite surrender, depending on who reads it. Where an intelligent opponent is adapting to you, the ordinary relation between means and ends inverts. The efficient route is the guarded one. The worse road is the better road because it is unwatched. Success, pressed, turns into failure at a definite point: chapter 3 is "Efficiency and the Culminating Point of Success," chapter 4 "The Coming Together of Opposites."

The second contribution is architecture. Strategy is analysed at five levels — technical, tactical, operational, theatre, grand — with the insistence that a result at one level can be reversed at the level above, so tactical brilliance regularly produces strategic defeat.

What he adds is the systematic statement of something the canon keeps stumbling over without naming. Sun Tzu's reversals, Clausewitz's culminating point, Machiavelli's inversions of virtue and Mahan's warning that Louis XIV's forced naval growth "withered away like Jonah's gourd" are all instances of a logic Luttwak is first to state as a general law.

What he restates is therefore a great deal — but the restatement is the contribution, because naming the pattern is what makes it usable.

**Verdict: import as an inoculation.** For a reader who has just entered the modern world with nothing, this book's most valuable function is negative: it explains why ordinary optimisation advice fails wherever another person is adapting to you, and why the obvious route being obvious is itself a fact about the route.

## 13. Lawrence Freedman — *Strategy: A History* (2013)

Freedman's book is a nine-hundred-page history of the idea, from chimpanzee coalitions to management consultancy, and its effect is deflationary in a useful way.

I could not open the book. I could open his article "The Meaning of Strategy, Part II: The Objectives," *Texas National Security Review* 1, no. 2 (February 2018), pp. 34–56, which covers the same ground in his own words. Its epigraph line is the thesis in miniature: "By the end of the 20th century, it had become a matter of endless fascination for theorists, but a puzzle for practitioners." The article traces how the word drifted — recording Raymond Aron's observation that by 1970 there was "now no difference between what was once called a policy and what one now calls strategy," and Hew Strachan's 2005 argument that the term had lost its meaning by absorbing everything around it.

It also does something this file has used above: it shows Liddell Hart's definition hardening into the standard one only after 1945, carried by his reputation rather than its merits.

The book's own central claims — that strategy is best understood as the art of creating power; that there is no master strategist and no view from above; that strategy is what you resort to precisely when the situation is *not* under your control; that strategies work by supplying a narrative others can act inside — are described here from substance and marked **UNVERIFIED** as wording, since I did not open the book.

What he adds is the history of the concept and, with it, immunity to the assumption that "strategy" names one stable thing generals, executives and individuals are all doing.

What he restates is Clausewitz, by way of demonstrating that most subsequent theorists were restating Clausewitz badly.

**Verdict: import the deflation, not the book.** One section. Freedman is the corrective to the master-plan fantasy, which is exactly the fantasy a reader with nothing will want the book to feed.

## 14. Richard Rumelt — *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (2011)

The most useful thing written about strategy outside the military tradition in thirty years, and the only item here that transfers to an individual with essentially no translation loss.

Its core is chapter five, "The Kernel of Good Strategy": "A good strategy has an essential logical structure that I call the kernel. The kernel of a strategy contains three elements: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent action." Spelled out in the same chapter: "1. A diagnosis that defines or explains the nature of the challenge. A good diagnosis simplifies the often overwhelming complexity of reality by identifying certain aspects of the situation as critical. 2. A guiding policy for dealing with the challenge." And on what that policy is: "It is like a signpost, marking the direction forward but not defining the details of the trip."

The negative half is equally valuable. His account of *bad* strategy — goals restated as strategy, fluff, failure to face the actual obstacle, and a genre in which intense desire is expected to produce results — is aimed at the literature this book's reader will otherwise be handed. His verdict on that genre, in the pages just before the kernel chapter, is blunt: believing focused desire changes the physical world is a form of psychosis and "cannot be recommended as approaches to management or strategy."

What he adds is a *test*. Most of the canon tells you what good strategy looks like once you see it. Rumelt gives a three-part structure you can hold your own plan against and watch it fail: if you cannot state the obstacle, you have a wish; if the policy does not follow from the diagnosis, you have a slogan; if the actions do not reinforce each other, you have a list.

What he restates is Clausewitz's demand to first establish the kind of war you are in, and Sun Tzu's estimates before the campaign.

**Verdict: import, structurally.** The kernel is scale-free — identical for a state, a firm and a man with one room and a job — and is a candidate for the book's own organising test.

## 15. Michael Porter — "What Is Strategy?" (*Harvard Business Review*, November–December 1996)

The article that told a generation of executives that being better is not a strategy.

*Verification note.* The article is paywalled and was read through the publisher's page at hbr.org, which yields section headings and some sentences verbatim. Two independent readings returned the headings and the positioning sentence identically, but returned *different* sentences for "the essence of strategy" — consistent with Porter using the phrase more than once. I quote only what came back identically twice.

The section headings run: "Operational Effectiveness Is Not Strategy"; "Operational Effectiveness: Necessary but Not Sufficient"; "Strategy Rests on Unique Activities"; "The Origins of Strategic Positions"; "The Connection with Generic Strategies"; "A Sustainable Strategic Position Requires Trade-offs"; "Fit Drives Both Competitive Advantage and Sustainability"; then on through "The Growth Trap" and "The Role of Leadership." The first heading is the argument.

The definitional sentence, confirmed twice: "Competitive strategy is about being different. It means deliberately choosing a different set of activities to deliver a unique mix of value."

The two formulations of the essence — that it lies in performing activities differently from rivals, and that it lies in choosing what *not* to do — are both attributed to the article by the publisher's page, in the unique-activities and trade-offs sections respectively. Each appeared in only one of my two readings, so both are **UNVERIFIED** as exact wording.

What he adds is the **trade-off** as the thing making a position defensible. A position competitors could copy without giving anything up is not a strategy; it is a practice, and it will be copied. Strategy exists only where imitating you would cost the imitator something he is unwilling to lose.

What he restates is the canon's oldest idea about position and the impossibility of being strong everywhere, in the vocabulary of firms.

**Verdict: annex, one import.** The trade-off test — *what does my position cost me, and would a competitor pay that price?* — is genuinely useful to an individual. The rest is firm-scale machinery for entities that have activity systems, which a man starting with nothing does not.

## 16. Qiao Liang and Wang Xiangsui — *Unrestricted Warfare* (1999)

Two PLA senior colonels, published by the PLA Literature and Arts Publishing House in February 1999. The text used is the FBIS translation at `sources/unrestricted_warfare_cryptome.txt`, which carries all eight chapters.

**A correction the book should make.** The line everyone quotes — "the first rule of unrestricted warfare is that there are no rules, with nothing forbidden" — is *not* from the book. In the FBIS text it appears in the translator's editorial note, attributed to Qiao in a *Zhongguo Qingnian Bao* interview of 28 June 1999. Anyone citing it as the book's opening sentence has not opened the book.

What the book itself says is broader. Its core claim, in the chapter on the weapons revolution: "there is nothing in the world today that cannot become a weapon, and this requires that our understanding of weapons must have an awareness that breaks through all boundaries." Chapter 8, "Essential Principles," then lists eight, set as separate lines in the translation and run together here: omnidirectionality, synchrony, limited objectives, unlimited measures, asymmetry, minimal consumption, multidimensional coordination, and adjustment and control of the entire process. Omnidirectionality is glossed as "360-degree Observation and Design, Combined Use of All Related Factors," asymmetry as seeking "Nodes of Action in the Opposite Direction from the Contours" of the opponent's strength.

What they add is a systematic argument that the boundary around "military" instruments has dissolved, so finance, law, media, networks and trade are now the same field as force, to be combined rather than sequenced. It is the most explicit statement anywhere of *combination across domains* as the primary operation.

What they restate is Sun Tzu, Kautilya — whose *Arthashastra* is already a manual of war by non-military means — and Lawrence's insight that the weaker party should fight where the stronger party's advantage does not apply.

**Verdict: annex, with a warning.** As description of the modern field it is accurate and worth a section. As doctrine for an individual it is useless and faintly corrosive: the moves it recommends are available only to states, and its spirit — unlimited measures for limited objectives — is the reasoning by which a man with nothing destroys the one asset he has, his reputation for keeping to terms.

## 17. Game theory's usable core

Most of game theory does not transfer to a man with no resources. Four results do.

**Repetition changes the game.** In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection dominates. In an indefinitely repeated one it need not, and the deciding variable is the probability of meeting the same person again. Axelrod and Hamilton, "The Evolution of Cooperation," *Science* 211, no. 4489 (27 March 1981), pp. 1390–96, prove tit-for-tat resists invasion "provided that the individuals who interact have a sufficiently large probability, w, of meeting again." Axelrod's 1984 book names this the *shadow of the future*; that phrase does not appear in the 1981 paper and is **UNVERIFIED** as his wording.

**Tit-for-tat, and why it wins.** Rapoport's four-line program won both of Axelrod's tournaments. Analysing three million choices in the second round, the paper finds its robustness depended on three features — "it was never the first to defect, it was provocable into retaliation by a defection of the other, and it was forgiving after just one act of retaliation." Nice, provocable, forgiving, and transparent enough that the other side can work out what you are doing. On the resulting dynamic: "The gear wheels of social evolution have a ratchet."

**Credible commitment.** Schelling's result, above: binding yourself is a source of power, because a promise you cannot break is worth more than one you merely intend to keep. Formally the sub-game perfection problem; practically, why contracts, deposits, public declarations and burnt bridges work.

**Mixed strategies.** Where a pure policy can be predicted and countered, the equilibrium is randomised. Von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in *Mathematische Annalen* 100 (1928), pp. 295–320, and developed it with Morgenstern in *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944); Nash generalised equilibrium existence to n players in *PNAS* 36 (1950) and *Annals of Mathematics* 54 (1951). These are cited from bibliographic record; I did not open them, and quote nothing from them.

What this adds to the canon is a reason. Sun Tzu says be unpredictable; minimax says when unpredictability is optimal. Kautilya says keep faith with allies who will still be there next year; Axelrod says how long "next year" has to be.

**Verdict: import the first three, mention the fourth.** For a man with nothing, the most consequential decision is which games he plays repeatedly and with whom, because that alone determines whether cooperation is a strategy or a donation.

## 18. State, firm, person: three things called strategy

Much of this material misfires when handed to an individual because "strategy" names three activities, distinguished by what the actor is and what it can lose.

**The state.** A state has a monopoly on legitimate violence, a population it can spend, revenue it can compel, and no exit — it cannot decide to stop competing. Its strategy is therefore about force among rivals who cannot be eliminated, and Freedman's article shows how thoroughly the word was shaped by that setting: strategy entered English as the general's art, and its modern vocabulary descends from an institution that plans the use of armies. The distinction Liddell Hart insists on at the head of Freedman's article — that the military objective is only the means to a political end — is the characteristic state-level problem, and arises for nobody else.

**The firm.** A firm has an exit, a balance sheet, and competitors it is forbidden to destroy by force. It competes for the voluntary choices of customers, so its strategy is positional rather than coercive — which is why Porter's answer takes the form it does: a different set of activities, trade-offs that make imitation costly, fit so the whole cannot be copied piecemeal. A firm also has what no state and no person has: it can be wound up and its capital redeployed. Failure is expensive, not terminal.

**The person.** One body, one lifetime, no staff, no reserve, and a failure mode that is absorbing. This is the difference that matters, and the reason the canon's grand-strategic vocabulary translates badly. A state can lose an army and recruit another; a person who is ruined is out of the game. Ruin is not a bad outcome on a distribution, it is the end of the distribution. Everything in the thirteen texts about accepting losses to gain position must be reread with that constraint welded on.

Three things transfer intact. Rumelt's kernel, because diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action are structural rather than institutional. Schelling's commitment, because a person can bind himself more credibly than an organisation can, having no committee to unbind him. Du Picq's cohesion, because reliability between known people works the same at four as at four thousand.

Two transfer with modification. Wylie's distinction changes emphasis: nearly everything available to a person without resources is cumulative, and the error is expecting it to behave sequentially. Boyd's tempo transfers not as speed but as orientation, since an individual's advantage over a larger opponent is almost never a faster decision cycle and is often a better-oriented one.

One thing does not transfer at all: the idea that strategy is a plan held over time. Freedman's deflation and Moltke's sentence say this from two directions. A state has a staff to re-plan continuously; a person must build the equivalent habit, or the plan becomes an identity and outlives its usefulness.

## 19. What to import, and why

My judgement, stated as recommendation rather than finding, since this is where the file stops reporting and starts arguing.

**Import, a named section or a paragraph each.**

*Moltke*, for the planning doctrine, with the real sentence printed. Nothing else here does as much work per line for a reader about to confuse having a plan with having a strategy.

*Rumelt*, for the kernel, as a working test the reader applies to his own situation. It is the only modern framework that survives translation to one person without loss, and doubles as the book's defence against the self-help genre the reader will otherwise be sold.

*Boyd*, for orientation and tempo, with the four-box cartoon explicitly killed and slide 26 quoted in its place.

*Schelling*, for commitment and focal points — the weak actor's two manufacturing processes for power — on condition someone verifies the wording before any sentence of his is printed.

*Mao and Lawrence together*, as the weak actor's pair: Mao for time and the staged build from nothing, Lawrence for the audit that finds advantages inside weakness. They make one argument and should share a chapter.

*Du Picq*, for cohesion and fear, which the canon leaves as an unexplained moral quantity and he turns into a mechanism.

*Wylie's distinction*, as a paragraph inside whatever chapter deals with effort that does not visibly accumulate.

*Axelrod*, for repetition and reciprocity, because whom the reader will meet again decides whether his decency is a strategy.

*Luttwak*, as an inoculation against linear reasoning, placed early, before the reader has anything resembling a technique.

*Freedman*, as the deflation — no master strategist, no view from above — placed late, where it brakes rather than discourages.

**Annex only.** *Jomini*, for the decisive point and as the specimen of geometry-as-false-certainty. *Liddell Hart*, for dislocation and the least-resistance/least-expectation pairing, with his method and his reputation-management stated plainly. *Fuller*, as the case study in why principle-lists fail, told through his own rebuilding of the list. *Porter*, for the trade-off test alone. *Qiao and Wang*, as description of the modern field, with the warning attached and the misattributed "first rule" corrected.

**Skip.** *Beaufre*, whose distinctive material is organisational and whose dialectic the reader already has from Clausewitz and Luttwak.

The shape this implies is that the descendants contribute one new chapter and a scattering of corrections, not a second canon. The thirteen texts remain the spine. That the addition is real and also short is the finding this file was written to establish.
