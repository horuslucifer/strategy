# 1. Sun Tzu (Sunzi) — The Art of War (Sunzi bingfa)

## 1. Identity

The text is thirteen short chapters of aphoristic military prescription, running to roughly 6,000 characters in Chinese (the conventional figure) and 10,904 words in Giles's English, counted from the thirteen chapters as they stand in the Gutenberg text. The Chinese title, 孫子兵法 *Sunzi bingfa*, means "Master Sun's military methods." "The Art of War" is a translator's title inherited from Amiot's French *Art militaire des Chinois* (1772); it imports a European connotation of *ars*, a refined practice, that "methods" does not carry. Where the English suggests an art, the Chinese suggests a procedure.

The translation used here is Lionel Giles, published in London in 1910, read in full from the Project Gutenberg files cached locally. Giles is out of copyright, textually careful (he gives text, translation and the Chinese commentators on the same page), and pedantic to a degree that is useful: he states his sources and flags his own uncertainty. His English is Edwardian and occasionally arch ("baulk the enemy's plans"), which is a cost. Modern scholarly standards are Griffith (1963, Oxford; the catalogue record reads "translated and with an introd. by Samuel B. Griffith; with a foreword by B.H. Liddell Hart"), Ames (1993, which uses the Yinqueshan manuscript), Sawyer (1994, in the *Seven Military Classics*), and Mair (2007, Columbia). None of those were available to me in full text; I have used Giles for every quotation and have not quoted the moderns except by named report.

What survives. Sima Qian's *Shiji* (c. 100 BC) gives a biography of one Sun Wu of the state of Qi, who presented thirteen chapters to King Helü of Wu, demonstrated his method by drilling the palace women and beheading two royal concubines when they laughed, and was made general. The *Shiji* records no birth or death date. The earliest date it attaches to Sun Wu is 512 BC and the latest 506 BC. Giles quotes the whole biography; I read it there. Cao Cao (155–220 AD) wrote the first surviving commentary, and ten more commentators accumulated through the Tang and Song, of whom Du Mu, Mei Yaochen, Zhang Yu and Li Quan are the most substantial. The received text descends through a mid-Song compilation by Ji Tianbao ("Sun Tzŭ with the collected commentaries of ten writers"), whose only surviving copy Sun Xingyan (1752–1818) found in the library of the Hua-yin temple and then, under government commission and with one co-editor, Wu Renji, collated against the two older versions embedded in the *Tongdian* (Du Yu, mid-Tang) and the *Taiping Yulan* (983 AD); that collation is the "standard text" Giles translates. Giles gives no date for the edition itself; the copy he used was an 1877 reissue.

**Correcting the blog.**

*The blog says* "Modern discoveries believe the Art of War was written during the Han Dynasty (206 BC – 220 AD)." *The evidence says* this is wrong, and it is wrong in an instructive way, *because* the "modern discovery" being garbled here is the Yinqueshan find of April 1972, when construction workers at Linyi in Shandong opened two Western Han tombs on 10 April. Tomb no. 1 held 4,942 bamboo strips; Tomb no. 2 held 32 strips of a calendar for 134 BC. The tombs date to roughly 140/134 BC and 118 BC. What was found was a Han *copy* of a text already old — thirteen fragmentary chapters corresponding closely to the received *Sunzi*, plus five further chapters of uncertain status, alongside the long-lost *Sun Bin bingfa*. A burial date is a terminus ante quem, not a composition date: the slips prove the text existed and was being copied by the mid-second century BC, which pushes the floor earlier, not later. The find also cut the other way from the blog's reading. Because two different texts could be called "Master Sun's military methods," one strand of sceptical argument — Liang Qichao's early twentieth-century theory that the received text was really written by Sun Bin in the fourth century BC — lost its main support when both texts turned up in the same tomb as separate works.

*The blog says* Sun Tzu lived 544–496 BC, and that scholars debate "whether he lived during the Zhou Dynasty or Warring States Period." *The evidence says* two things. First, those dates are a late convention with no ancient warrant; the *Shiji*, the only substantial source, gives none. Second, the dichotomy is a category error: the Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC) falls inside the Eastern Zhou. The real question is Spring and Autumn (c. 770–476) versus Warring States, both of them Zhou.

*The blog says* "The English translation finally reached the western world in the early nineteenth century." *The evidence says* no, *because* the first translation into any European language was Amiot's French of 1772; the text appeared as "Les Treize Articles de Sun-tse" in the seventh volume of the *Mémoires concernant l'histoire, les sciences, les arts, les mœurs, les usages, &c., des Chinois*, which Giles describes at the head of his preface. The first English translation was E. F. Calthrop's, published in Tokyo in 1905 under the title *Sonshi* — twentieth century, not nineteenth. Giles states this outright, and his sentence is the flat refutation of the blog: "Throughout the nineteenth century, which saw a wonderful development in the study of Chinese literature, no translator ventured to tackle Sun Tzŭ." He is scathing about both versions: of Amiot he writes, "It contains a great deal that Sun Tzŭ did not write, and very little indeed of what he did."

My own position on the dating, stated as inference: the internal evidence Giles assembles is strong for a fifth- or fourth-century core. The text knows chariot warfare between feudal states, uses a land-tenure system already obsolete by Mencius's day, names Yue but never Chu as the enemy, and is quoted or plagiarised by authors of the Warring States and early Han. Against this, Mair (2007) argues the text coalesced over some seventy-five years, from the middle of the fourth century to the first quarter of the third century BC, and that Sun Wu — the strategist of the Spring and Autumn period to whom the text is traditionally attributed — never existed. (Mair's volume carries an appendix titled "The Pseudo-Biography of Sun Wu." This is from the Columbia University Press description of the book, not from the book, which I could not obtain.) The honest summary: the *text* is old, pre-Han, probably Warring States with possible earlier material; the *man* is not securely attested and may be a later construction around the book. Nothing in the book's usefulness depends on which is true.

## 2. The world the text assumes

The text assumes a world of a dozen or so competing states with fixed borders, hereditary rulers, and a permanent expectation of war. It assumes a sovereign who commissions a general, and a general who then operates at a distance with delegated authority — this separation is the structural premise of the whole book, and it produces both the doctrine of the general's independence (X.23) and the doctrine of the ruler's three ways of ruining an army (III.12–16).

It assumes an economy that can barely afford war. Chapter II costs an army of 100,000 at a thousand ounces of silver a day and states that campaigning will dissipate three-tenths of the people's income and four-tenths of state revenue. Transport over distance is so costly that a cartload taken from the enemy is reckoned worth twenty of one's own. This is a pre-industrial agrarian state with no credit system, no railways, and no capacity for a long war. Everything the text says about speed, about protraction, about foraging, follows from that single economic fact.

It assumes armies of conscripted peasants under professional officers, moved on foot with chariots, commanded by line of sight and sound — gongs, drums, banners, signal-fires (VII.23–26). Control degrades with distance and darkness. It assumes that the commander can physically see his own force and that the enemy can physically see his. This is why concealment is achievable and why "formlessness" is a coherent goal: there is no reconnaissance that cannot be defeated by moving at night.

It assumes a psychology of massed men, treated frankly as material. Soldiers are to be kept ignorant of the plan (XI.36–39), placed in danger to produce courage (XI.23–25), and managed by a cycle of daily mood (VII.28). The text is not cynical about this; it simply does not believe that a hundred thousand men can be led by persuasion. It also assumes the commander has the power of life and death over his own officers — the concubine anecdote is not decoration, it is the enabling condition.

It assumes that war has a fixed and knowable inventory of situations. The book is organised as a taxonomy: five factors, seven comparisons, six terrains, nine grounds, five spies, five fire-attacks, five faults. This taxonomic habit is the book's method, and it presupposes a domain stable enough to be enumerated.

Finally, it assumes the object of war is acquisition, not destruction. States are to be taken intact because a wrecked state is worth less than an intact one. The "win without fighting" doctrine is an economic argument before it is a moral one.

## 3. Core doctrine

Twenty principles. Loci are chapter and paragraph in Giles's numbering.

**1. War is a state matter to be studied, not improvised.** (I.1–2) *Reasoning:* it is "a matter of life and death, a road either to safety or to ruin," and therefore a subject of inquiry that cannot be neglected. The claim is that war is systematically knowable, which is the premise of the entire book. *Limits:* Sun Tzu never says study substitutes for experience; the commentators divide on this, and Sun Xingyan — quoted at length in Giles's Introduction under "Apologies for War" — has to defend the book against the stock example of Zhao Kua, who "pored over his father's books to no purpose" and is adduced by opponents "as a proof that all military theory is useless."

**2. Outcomes are forecastable by measurement taken before commitment.** (I.3–14; IV.17–19) *Reasoning:* compare the two sides on five constants — moral law, heaven, earth, commander, method and discipline — and seven specific questions (which sovereign has the people's allegiance, which general more ability, who holds the advantages of ground and season, whose discipline is stricter, which army stronger, whose men better trained, whose rewards and punishments more consistent). Chapter IV gives the same idea as a causal chain: measurement produces estimation of quantity, which produces calculation, which produces the balancing of chances, which produces victory. *Limits:* the factors are comparative, not absolute. The forecast tells you whether to fight, not how.

**3. All warfare is based on deception.** (I.18–25) *Reasoning:* the enemy acts on his picture of you, so the picture is a controllable weapon. Seem unable when able, inactive when moving, far when near. Irritate the choleric; pretend weakness so he grows arrogant (I.22); give the resting no rest; separate the united (I.23). *Limits:* Sun Tzu adds that these devices "must not be divulged beforehand" (I.25) — the doctrine depends on asymmetric knowledge and is self-cancelling once both sides read the book.

**4. Many calculations beat few; none beats nothing.** (I.26) *Reasoning:* the general who wins makes many calculations in his temple before the battle, the loser few. The temple reference is literal — the pre-campaign reckoning was a ritual accounting. *Limits:* Sun Tzu also says to modify plans as circumstances shift (I.16–17), so calculation is a starting position, not a script.

**5. Cost and duration govern everything; protraction ruins the victor.** (II.1–6, II.19) *Reasoning:* long campaigns dull weapons, damp ardour, exhaust strength and spend treasure, after which "other chieftains will spring up to take advantage of your extremity." There is no instance of a country benefiting from prolonged warfare. *Limits:* the argument is economic and holds for a state with no financial depth. Sun Tzu concedes stupid haste exists; he says cleverness has never been associated with long delays, which is an assertion about expense, not about tempo as such.

**6. Take your supply from the contested ground.** (II.8–9, II.15) *Reasoning:* transport cost across distance is the dominant expense and it impoverishes the home population — an army's proximity raises prices, which drains the people's substance. One cartload of the enemy's grain is worth twenty of your own. *Limits:* this is plunder, and Sun Tzu knows it; he pairs it with treating captured soldiers kindly and absorbing them (II.17–18), because the object is acquisition.

**7. Take whole rather than break.** (III.1–2) *Reasoning:* to take the enemy's country intact is better than to shatter it; to capture an army entire better than to destroy it. Supreme excellence is breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting. *Limits:* this presupposes you have the power to break him and he knows it. It is a doctrine of the strong, not a doctrine of avoidance.

**8. There is a hierarchy of targets, and the physical one is last.** (III.3–5) *Reasoning:* the highest form is to baulk the enemy's plans; next, to prevent the junction of his forces; next, to attack his army in the field; worst of all, to besiege walled cities. Sieges are costed explicitly — three months to build the equipment, three more to pile the mounds, a third of the men lost, the town still untaken. *Limits:* the ranking is by cost-efficiency, not by morality.

**9. Method follows the force ratio, and when hopelessly outmatched, withdraw.** (III.8–10) *Reasoning:* ten to one, surround; five to one, attack; two to one, divide; even, offer battle; slightly inferior, avoid; "if quite unequal in every way, we can flee from him." An obstinate fight by a small force ends in its capture. *Limits:* Zhang Yu's note (commentary, not text) says the ratios only hold when other factors are equal, and that a small numerical difference is often outweighed by energy and discipline.

**10. Know the other and know yourself.** (III.18; X.31) *Reasoning:* knowing both, a hundred battles hold no fear; knowing only yourself, you will lose as often as you win; knowing neither, you lose every time. Chapter X extends the pairing: know Heaven and Earth as well and victory becomes complete. *Limits:* Sun Tzu treats self-knowledge as a measurement problem (numbers, training, discipline), not an introspective one.

**11. Make yourself unbeatable first; the opening is the enemy's to give.** (IV.1–7) *Reasoning:* the good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat and then waited. Security against defeat is in your own hands; the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself. Defence indicates insufficient strength, attack a superabundance of it. *Limits:* this makes attack conditional on a surplus you may never acquire. Taken alone it counsels indefinite waiting.

**12. The best victory is invisible; win first, then fight.** (IV.8–15) *Reasoning:* to see victory only when the crowd sees it is not excellence; the clever fighter wins with ease, and so gains "neither reputation for wisdom nor credit for courage." He wins by making no mistakes, which means conquering an enemy already defeated. The victorious strategist seeks battle only after the victory has been won; the one destined to defeat fights first and looks for victory afterwards. *Limits:* Giles's note on IV.14 reads, in full, `A "counsel of perfection" as Tu Mu truly observes` — the phrase is Giles's, the observation Du Mu's, and between them the commentary treats this as an ideal rather than a procedure.

**13. Direct and indirect combine inexhaustibly; momentum is positional, not personal.** (V.5–11, V.21–23) *Reasoning:* the direct method joins battle, the indirect secures victory; the two lead on to each other in a circle. Five notes, five colours, five tastes generate more combinations than can be exhausted. Then the crucial move: the clever combatant "looks to the effect of combined energy, and does not require too much from individuals," because men in the right position behave like round stones on a slope. *Limits:* this requires that you control the slope — that is, the dispositions. It is an argument for arranging conditions rather than demanding effort.

**14. Impose the tempo; be first in the field and make him come to you.** (VI.1–6) *Reasoning:* whoever is first in the field awaits the enemy fresh; whoever is second arrives exhausted. The clever combatant imposes his will and does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him, drawing him by advantage or blocking him by damage. *Limits:* being first requires knowing where the field will be, which returns you to Chapter XIII.

**15. Concentration against dispersion; formlessness forces him to prepare everywhere.** (VI.13–18, VI.25–27) *Reasoning:* if your dispositions are hidden and his are known, you concentrate while he must divide, and a whole is pitted against fractions. Reinforce the van and the rear weakens; reinforce everywhere and "he will everywhere be weak." Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength from compelling him to make those preparations. *Limits:* this is the book's one genuine mechanism for the weaker side, and it is strictly local. It manufactures superiority at a point, not overall.

**16. There are no constant conditions; do not repeat a winning tactic, and refuse some orders.** (VI.28–33, VIII.2–3, X.23–24) *Reasoning:* water has no constant shape and takes its course from the ground; so tactics take their form from the opponent. Hence: do not repeat the tactics that gained one victory. Hence also the harder claim — there are roads not to be followed, armies not to be attacked, towns not to be besieged, positions not to be contested, and "commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed." If fighting means victory you must fight though the ruler forbids it; if not, you must not fight at his bidding. *Limits:* Sun Tzu couples this to a strict criterion — the general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace. Disobedience is licensed only for someone indifferent to reputation.

**17. Base plans on your own readiness, not on the enemy's intentions.** (VIII.11) *Reasoning:* rely not on the likelihood of his not coming but on your own readiness to receive him; not on the chance of his not attacking but on having made your position unassailable. *Limits:* none stated. This is the book's flattest assertion and the one least hedged.

**18. Five faults destroy commanders, and all five are character, not skill.** (VIII.12–14) *Reasoning:* recklessness leads to destruction; cowardice to capture; a hasty temper can be provoked by insults; a delicacy of honour is sensitive to shame; over-solicitude for the men exposes you to worry. When an army is overthrown and its leader slain, the cause will be found among these five. *Limits:* Sun Tzu does not say how to correct them; he says to meditate on them. Note that three of the five are virtues in excess.

**19. Move only on advantage; anger is not a reason, and some losses are irreversible.** (XII.17–22) *Reasoning:* move not unless you see an advantage, use not your troops unless there is something to be gained, fight not unless the position is critical. No ruler should put troops in the field to gratify his spleen. The reason given is asymmetry of recovery: "anger may in time change to gladness; vexation may be succeeded by content. But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life." *Limits:* this sits awkwardly beside II.16, where soldiers must be "roused to anger" in order to kill. Anger is fuel at the bottom and poison at the top.

**20. Foreknowledge comes from people, is bought, and is cheap against the cost of war.** (XIII.1–8, XIII.14, XIII.21–25) *Reasoning:* to grudge a hundred ounces of silver in honours and emoluments, when the war costs a thousand a day, is "the height of inhumanity." Foreknowledge cannot be got from spirits, from analogy with past events, or by calculation; only from men who know the enemy's situation. Five classes of spy; the converted spy is the hinge, because through him all the others are recruited and fed. *Limits:* it requires benevolence, straightforwardness and "subtle ingenuity of mind" to run agents, and a leak before the time is ripe is punished with death for both the spy and his confidant.

## 4. Weakness, scarcity, and the small actor

The text addresses numerical inferiority **directly** and its answer is mostly discouraging.

Directly stated: if slightly inferior in numbers, avoid the enemy; "if quite unequal in every way, we can flee from him" (III.9). An obstinate fight by a small force ends in that force being captured by the larger (III.10). If your numbers merely equal the enemy's, that is "amply sufficient," but it means no direct attack can be made — concentrate, watch closely, and obtain reinforcements (IX.40). Six ways of courting defeat are listed at X.14–19, and the first is *flight*, defined as what happens when a force is hurled against another ten times its size. He who exercises no forethought and makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured (IX.41).

So the text's plain counsel to the outmatched is: do not fight, withdraw, wait, and acquire more. It is not a handbook for underdogs. It is a handbook for a state that can field a hundred thousand men and wants to spend as little of them as possible.

Against that, three passages give the weaker side real, non-rhetorical mechanisms, and all three are direct.

First, **VI.13–18**, the concentration doctrine. If you know his dispositions and he does not know yours, he must prepare at several points and you need prepare at none; your whole meets a fraction of his whole. The final line is the operative one: "Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength, from compelling our adversary to make these preparations against us." Weakness is here redefined as a *distribution* problem rather than a *quantity* problem — which is the one place where the book makes the smaller party genuinely dangerous.

Second, **VI.21–24**, stated in the first person about a real strategic situation: the soldiers of Yue exceed ours in number, and that shall advantage them nothing; though the enemy be stronger, we may prevent him from fighting. The method given is not courage but reconnaissance by provocation — rouse him to learn his principle of activity, force him to reveal himself to find his vulnerable spots, then compare the two armies point by point to find where his strength is superabundant and where deficient.

Third, **III.3 and IV.1–2**. If the highest generalship is to baulk plans and prevent junctions, then the cheapest interventions come earliest, before force ratios matter. And security against defeat lies in your own hands even when victory does not — a weak party can always make itself hard to destroy, which is a lower bar than winning and entirely under its control.

**By clear implication** (my inference, flagged as such): the book's whole economic argument favours the poorer party in one specific circumstance. If protraction ruins states, and if the richer party is the one spending a thousand ounces a day, then time costs the large actor more than the small one — provided the small one is not maintaining an army of his own. Sun Tzu never draws this conclusion; his Chapter II warnings are addressed to the invader, and he assumes his reader is the one paying. The inference is available but it is not in the text.

**Not addressed at all:** the actor with no position, no reputation and no allies. Sun Tzu's weak party is a *state* — it has territory, a treasury, subjects, a ruler, and standing. Every mechanism above requires something to concentrate. There is no passage anywhere in the thirteen chapters about acquiring a force from nothing, establishing a reputation, or entering a contest as an unknown. The general is already commissioned on the first page of Chapter VII. Anything the book offers a man with nothing is extracted by analogy, and the analogy must be defended each time.

## 5. On the standing questions

**Preparation.** Central and repeated. IV.1: put yourself beyond the possibility of defeat first, then wait. VIII.11: rely on your own readiness, not on his forbearance. XII.16: the enlightened ruler lays his plans well ahead, the good general cultivates his resources. Preparation in Sun Tzu is not stockpiling but the removal of exploitable openings.

**Knowledge and intelligence.** The whole of Chapter XIII, plus III.18 and X.31. Foreknowledge is the single thing that distinguishes the sovereign and general who "achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men," it cannot be deduced or divined, it must be bought from human beings, and the outlay is trivial against the cost of the war it shortens. Intelligence in Sun Tzu is also *counter*-intelligence: VI.25 makes the concealment of your own dispositions the highest attainable pitch of tactical skill.

**Timing.** Present, and specific. V.13–15: the quality of decision is the well-timed swoop of a falcon; energy is the bent crossbow, decision the released trigger. VII.28–29: a soldier's spirit is keenest in the morning, flags by noon, and turns homeward in the evening — so avoid an army when its spirit is keen and attack when it is sluggish. XI.19: rapidity is the essence of war, taking advantage of the enemy's unreadiness. Timing is treated as a property of the opponent's state, observable from outside.

**Terrain and position.** The largest single block in the book: Chapter IX (mountains, rivers, marshes, flat country, camping), Chapter X (six terrains — accessible, entangling, temporising, narrow passes, precipitous heights, distant), Chapter XI (nine grounds, from dispersive to desperate, each with a prescribed action). X.21 states the governing idea: the natural formation of the country is the soldier's best ally, but the test of a great general is estimating the adversary and calculating distances and difficulties. Terrain is never good or bad in itself; it is good or bad relative to who occupies it first.

**Deception and information.** I.18–25 and VI.25–27 and XI.36–39. Note the scope: deception is directed at the enemy (I), at neutrals, and explicitly at one's own troops — the general "must be able to mystify his officers and men by false reports and appearances, and thus keep them in total ignorance" (XI.36). Sun Tzu treats information as something to be managed in all directions, not merely withheld from an opponent.

**Alliances.** Present but thin, and consistently instrumental. VII.12 and XI.52: we cannot enter into alliances until we are acquainted with our neighbours' designs. XI.12: on ground of intersecting highways, join hands with your allies. The hardest passage is XI.54–55: attacking a powerful state, the warlike prince prevents the concentration of enemy forces and overawes his opponents so that their allies are prevented from joining against him — and "he does not strive to ally himself with all and sundry, nor does he foster the power of other states." Alliance is a tool for isolating the enemy, not a network to be cultivated.

**Logistics and economy.** Chapter II is almost entirely this, and it is the most concrete chapter in the book: a daily cost figure, a two-trip limit on the supply train, the 20:1 ratio for captured provisions, three-tenths of popular income and four-tenths of state revenue consumed. VII.11: an army without its baggage-train is lost, without provisions lost, without bases of supply lost. The book's most famous doctrines — speed, avoiding sieges, taking whole — are all downstream of this arithmetic.

**Morale and discipline.** IX.42–45 gives the sequence: soldiers punished before they are attached to you will not be submissive; soldiers attached to you but not punished are equally useless; therefore treat them first with humanity and then hold them with iron discipline. X.25–26 gives the failure mode: regard them as sons and they will follow you into the deepest valleys, but be indulgent without authority and they are spoilt children, useless. VII.27 acknowledges that "a whole army may be robbed of its spirit." Morale is treated as a manipulable quantity with a known decay curve.

**The decisive point.** Present as a *method* rather than a concept. Sun Tzu has no Schwerpunkt; what he has is VI.5–8 — appear at points the enemy must hasten to defend, march to places where you are not expected, attack only what is undefended, hold only what cannot be attacked. Combined with VI.13–18, the decisive point is defined negatively: it is wherever he is not, at the moment he cannot get there.

**The culminating point and when to stop.** No formal doctrine — this is the clearest gap relative to Clausewitz. The nearest analogues are three. II.2–6: protraction exhausts the state and invites third parties. VII.7–10: a forced march of a hundred li to snatch an advantage delivers one-tenth of your army and loses the leaders of all three divisions — offensive reach degrades force in a quantified way. XII.22: the enlightened ruler is heedful, the good general full of caution, which is how a country is kept at peace and an army intact. The idea that an offensive contains its own reversal is implied by the first two but never stated.

**When not to fight.** Explicit and repeated. III.17: the first of five essentials for victory is knowing when to fight and when not to fight. VIII.3: there are armies not to be attacked, towns not to be besieged, positions not to be contested. X.23: if fighting will not result in victory, do not fight even at the ruler's bidding. XII.17–19: move only on advantage; if it is not to your advantage, stay where you are.

**Defence versus offence.** IV.5–7 is the whole doctrine and it is unusually blunt: security against defeat implies defensive tactics; ability to defeat the enemy means taking the offensive; standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength, attacking a superabundance of strength. The skilled defender hides in the secret recesses of the earth, the skilled attacker flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven. Defence is not a strategy in Sun Tzu; it is what you do while you do not yet have enough.

**The character required of the commander.** I.9 lists five virtues: wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage and strictness. VIII.12 lists the five faults, which are those same qualities running to excess or deficit. XI.35 adds the working temperament: quiet, and thus able to keep secrets; upright and just, and thus able to maintain order. X.24 gives the summary type — the general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace, whose only thought is to protect his country, is "the jewel of the kingdom." Indifference to reputation is the enabling virtue, because every other correct act in the book looks bad from outside.

## 6. Failure modes, misreadings, critiques

**The authenticity critique (classical Chinese).** Ye Shi (Yeh Shui-hsin, Song dynasty) made the strongest case against Sun Wu's historicity: the *Zuo Zhuan*, the great contemporary chronicle, does not mention him at all, though it records "vulgar plebeians and hireling ruffians" and gives full detail on his supposed contemporaries Wu Yuan and Bo Pi. Ye Shi concluded the text was "the production of some private scholar" and that the accounts of Sun Wu are "not authentic matter, but the reckless fabrication of theorizing pundits," the concubine story in particular being "utterly preposterous and incredible." Chen Zhensun made the same observation more mildly. Giles, who quotes both, accepts the force of the argument against the *biography* while defending the antiquity of the *text* — and adds his own chronological objection, which he claims nobody had made before him: the book names Yue as the enemy and never Chu, which does not fit the 512–506 BC window the *Shiji* gives for Sun Wu's career. Liang Qichao, early twentieth century, proposed Sun Bin as the real author; the Yinqueshan find of 1972 undercut that by recovering both texts as separate works.

**The moral critique (classical Chinese).** Zhu Xi admired the book's quality and distrusted its effect, saying that praise of this sort "encourages a ruler's bent towards unrelenting warfare and reckless militarism." This is the oldest version of the objection that the book teaches efficiency without teaching restraint. Du Mu's own summary of the doctrine — "Practice benevolence and justice, but on the other hand make full use of artifice and measures of expediency" — concedes the split rather than resolving it.

**The strategic-culture critique (modern).** Alastair Iain Johnston, *Cultural Realism* (Princeton, 1995), argues that the endlessly quoted "win without fighting" has been systematically misread as evidence that Chinese strategic tradition rates force low. Johnston's reading of the Seven Military Classics and of Ming practice finds a hard *parabellum* preference — offensive, violence-tolerant — underneath a symbolic Confucian-Mencian layer whose function is to justify decisions in culturally acceptable terms. On this account, taking Sun Tzu's rhetoric of bloodless victory at face value is a category mistake about what the rhetoric is for.

**The dating critique (modern).** Victor Mair (*The Art of War: Sun Zi's Military Methods*, Columbia, November 2007) holds that the text coalesced over about seventy-five years, from the mid-fourth to the first quarter of the third century BC, and that Sun Wu never existed. This is a minority-strong position, not a consensus; I have it from the publisher's description of the volume (cup.columbia.edu), not from the volume itself, which I could not obtain.

**The "Sun Tzu versus Clausewitz" misreading.** The popular framing — Sun Tzu the subtle Easterner who wins without fighting, Clausewitz the blunt Westerner who wins by battle — is an artefact of reception rather than of the texts. That B. H. Liddell Hart wrote the foreword to Griffith's 1963 Oxford translation is a catalogue fact — the MARC statement of responsibility reads "translated and with an introd. by Samuel B. Griffith; with a foreword by B.H. Liddell Hart." What that foreword *says* I have not read, so the further claim often made, that Liddell Hart read Sun Tzu as an ancient authority for his own "indirect approach," stays unverified here and should not be repeated as fact. The standing scholarly corrective is Michael Handel, *Masters of War: Classical Strategic Thought* (Routledge, 3rd revised and expanded edn, 2001, 510 pp.), which examines Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, Mao and, to a lesser extent, Jomini and Machiavelli, and whose stated central conclusion is "that the logic of waging war and of strategic thinking is as universal and timeless as human nature itself"; chapter 2 is "Comparing Sun Tzu and Clausewitz." I have this from the Routledge catalogue page, not from the book itself. Anyone building on Sun Tzu should in any case notice that IV.5–7 makes attack the mark of strength and defence the mark of insufficiency, which is not what the caricature predicts.

**Misuse in war.** The book's most quotable doctrines — deception, indirection, the primacy of morale — are the ones easiest to invoke as a substitute for the arithmetic of Chapter II. Mao Zedong, who read the text closely, quotes it by name in "Problems of Strategy in China's Revolutionary War" (December 1936), using "Know the enemy and know yourself" in Chapter I §4 and "Avoid the enemy when he is full of vigour, strike when he is fatigued and withdraws" in Chapter V §3. Mao's use is disciplined; the imitations are usually not. The recurring pattern is that "win without fighting" is cited by parties who lack the latent superiority that makes the doctrine work, in which case it names not a strategy but an absence of one.

**Misuse in business and self-help.** The text has been repackaged continuously since the 1980s as a management manual, and the failure is structural, not incidental. Three specific errors: (i) the two-party adversarial frame is imported wholesale into situations — a career, a craft, a market with thousands of participants — where there is no enemy with intentions, which converts a strategic text into a licence for paranoia; (ii) the doctrine usually circulated as "subdue the enemy without fighting" — Giles's wording is III.6, "the skilful leader subdues the enemy's troops without any fighting; he captures their cities without laying siege to them" — is read as avoid competition, when in Sun Tzu it presupposes overwhelming power that makes resistance pointless; (iii) the numbered lists in wide circulation under his name ("the 33 strategies," "the 21 principles") are not in the thirteen chapters — I read the whole translation and no such list occurs; whatever their origin, they are not Sun Tzu. The commodification has also produced an incentive to strip the cost accounting, which is the least quotable and most load-bearing part of the book.

**False and unprovable attributions.** All four of the following are widely printed over Sun Tzu's name and none occurs anywhere in Giles's bare translation or in his commentary edition; I checked both files for each string and got zero hits.
- "Keep your friends close and your enemies closer." The line comes most directly from *The Godfather Part II* (1974), written by Mario Puzo and Francis Ford Coppola, where Michael Corleone attributes it to his father and the wording is "keep your friends close but your enemies closer." The popular form circulated under Sun Tzu's name is not even the film's wording. (Source: Wikiquote's misattribution note on Sun Tzu, which also records the frequent misattributions to Petrarch and Machiavelli.)
- "If you sit by the river long enough, you will see the body of your enemy float by." Zero hits in either file. I could not source its actual origin and do not assert one; it is not Sun Tzu.
- "In the midst of chaos, there is also opportunity." Zero hits in either file. Origin unsourced.
- "Every battle is won before it is ever fought." Zero hits in either file. It is a loose paraphrase of IV.15, "the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won." It is widely said to be a film line; I could not verify that sourcing and so do not make the claim.
- "Appear weak when you are strong, and strong when you are weak" is a *legitimate modern paraphrase* of I.19 ("when able to attack, we must seem unable") together with I.22 ("Pretend to be weak, that he may grow arrogant"), not a fabrication, but it is not Giles's wording and should not be quoted as such.

## 8. Translation to the man who enters with nothing

Start with the unflattering structural fact. This book was written for a man who already has an army. Every operative doctrine in it presupposes a force to dispose, a treasury to spend, a commission to act on, and a named opponent who wants what he has. A man with no money, no network, no position, no credential and no reputation has none of those. He is not the general. He is not even the soldier. He is closest to the state's condition *before* it has a state — a condition Sun Tzu never discusses, because in his world such a person is not a strategic actor at all.

So the first honest transfer is a subtraction. Most of the thirteen chapters do not apply to him, and pretending they do is how the business-book industry produces nonsense. Chapters IX, X and XI — roughly a third of the book — are about moving physical bodies across ground, and their "translation" into life advice is metaphor with no testable content. Chapter XII is about arson. The signalling apparatus (V.1–2, and in full at VII.23–26) is about commanding men you cannot shout to. Chapter XIII assumes an opponent with a purchasable secret; most of what a beginner needs to know is public and simply unread, and calling that "intelligence" flatters idleness.

What actually transfers, with the reasoning:

**The cost accounting of Chapter II transfers, and it is the most important thing in the book for him.** Sun Tzu's central claim is that operations are limited by what they burn, that protraction ruins the operator whether or not he is winning, and that "there is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare." The man with nothing has one resource — unencumbered time and attention — and it depletes. The concrete application is unglamorous: know your runway in months, and refuse any undertaking whose payoff horizon exceeds it. Not because ambition is bad, but because Sun Tzu's arithmetic is right: when your weapons are dulled and your treasure spent, "other chieftains will spring up to take advantage of your extremity." What follows from the text, and no more: the campaign that outruns its supply is lost on the arithmetic regardless of how it is going tactically, and the operator is then worse placed than when he started — II.4, where the dulled weapons and spent treasure are what invite the third party in. Whether a long project yields a usable partial result is a question Sun Tzu does not address and I will not answer for him.

**IV.1–2 transfers, correctly read.** "To secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself." This is not a motivational line; it is a division of labour between what you control and what you wait for. Concretely, for a man with nothing: keep fixed costs low enough that no single reversal removes you; avoid debt that converts a bad month into a permanent condition; retain the ability to walk away from any arrangement. These are unimpressive acts. They are also the only half of the equation you own. The other half — the opening, the vacancy, the person who needs exactly what you have — is on the text's account provided by the other side, not produced by you (IV.2–3: the good fighter "cannot make certain of defeating the enemy"). That is not the same as saying effort is irrelevant to it: VI.23 has you rouse the opponent to learn his principle of activity and force him to reveal himself, which is effort spent on making an existing weakness legible rather than on manufacturing an opening where none exists. The discipline the text actually prescribes is that you must still be solvent when one appears.

**III.9–10 transfers, and it is the hardest thing in the book to accept.** "If quite unequal in every way, we can flee from him," and an obstinate fight by a small force ends in capture. The application is: do not enter contests decided by resources you do not have. A man with no capital should not compete on capital; a man with no credential should not enter a field gated by credentials and expect persistence to substitute. This is not defeatism, it is target selection, and it is the same move as III.3 — baulk plans rather than assault cities, because sieges cost six months and a third of the men and usually fail. Most of what a man with nothing needs is not more effort inside a losing contest but a different contest.

**VI.13–18 is the single mechanism the book gives the weaker party, and it transfers cleanly.** Weakness is redefined as a distribution problem: "Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength, from compelling our adversary to make these preparations against us." An established competitor must defend many positions; a man with nothing defends none, and can put everything on one narrow point. The application is narrowness. The text gives the principle, not a threshold; the threshold below is my own working rule and should be read as that — the domain should be small enough that within a year you can be the most knowledgeable person in it that anyone in your reach can find. Narrow means narrower than feels respectable. What is Sun Tzu's and not mine is the structural point: concentration is possible for whoever has nothing to hold, and VI.17 makes the reverse the established party's problem — reinforce everywhere and "he will everywhere be weak."

**VIII.12, the five faults, transfers exactly and without adjustment**, because they are properties of a person rather than of an army. Recklessness, cowardice, a hasty temper that can be provoked by insult, a delicacy of honour sensitive to shame, and over-solicitude for those in your care. For a man with no position the fourth is the killer. Having nothing means being condescended to, ignored, and occasionally humiliated, and a man who is sensitive to shame will start making decisions to avoid those sensations rather than to improve his position — taking the job that sounds better, refusing the work that looks beneath him, avoiding the room where he is the least impressive person. Sun Tzu's judgement, at VIII.14, is that when an army is overthrown and its leader slain the cause will be found among these five. He says nothing about beginners; the extension to them is mine, and rests only on the faults being personal rather than institutional.

**IV.8–13 transfers as a warning about selection.** The best victories bring "neither reputation for wisdom nor credit for courage," because they are won over circumstances that never became visible. A man with nothing is under permanent pressure to do legible things — things that can be described to others and that produce a reaction. The book says the correct move usually looks like nothing. What follows: applause is not evidence of a good move, since by IV.8–9 the best moves are the ones the crowd cannot see. What does *not* follow is that other people's reactions carry no information — Sun Tzu says nothing about that, and the doctrine of foreknowledge in Chapter XIII says the opposite about information from people generally. The usable rule is the narrow one: do not treat legibility as a proxy for value, and expect the highest-value use of a given year to be invisible and unrewarded while it is happening.

**XII.17–22 transfers as a rule about irreversibility.** Move only on advantage; never on spleen; and note the asymmetry Sun Tzu gives as the reason — moods reverse, destroyed kingdoms do not. Applied: separate decisions that can be unwound from decisions that cannot, and hold the second category to a completely different standard of evidence. Most catastrophic beginner errors are irreversible commitments made in a reversible mood.

**I.18 transfers, but only when demoted.** "All warfare is based on deception" does not translate into lying. (That judgement is mine, not the text's: Sun Tzu has no view on civil life, and I hold that lying destroys the only asset a man with nothing has, the willingness of strangers to extend him credit on his word.) What translates is the narrower claim behind it: control disclosure. Do not announce a plan before you can defend it, do not explain what you are building to people positioned to take it, and do not let your visible activity be a reliable index of your actual activity. That is a real and usable discipline. "Deception" as a life strategy is not.

**XIII transfers in a reduced form.** Sun Tzu's real claim in Chapter XIII is about relative price: a hundred ounces of silver spent on knowing the enemy, against a thousand a day spent not knowing him, and to grudge the first is "the height of inhumanity." For a man with nothing the currency is different — he pays in attention, proximity, and unpaid usefulness — and the specific ratio does not survive the change of currency — a hundred ounces against a thousand a day is an arithmetic about a campaign, not about a life. What does survive is XIII.5–6, which is a claim about the *kind* of knowledge that counts: foreknowledge "cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation," and "knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men." That is a real argument for spending a year adjacent to people who already do the thing rather than a year reading about it. What does *not* transfer is the apparatus: five classes of agent, converted spies, executions for leaks (XIII.19). He has no network to run.

What does not transfer, and why:

**"Forage on the enemy" (II.9, II.15) does not transfer.** In the text this is plunder, and its justification is an arithmetic that no longer exists: transport cost across distance made a captured cartload worth twenty of your own. In civil life the direct analogue is theft, and the sanitised analogue — use existing infrastructure instead of building your own — is a much weaker and more obvious claim than the one Sun Tzu is making. Take the weak version and drop the vocabulary.

**The adversarial frame does not transfer, and this is the most consequential exclusion.** Sun Tzu assumes a named opponent with intentions, resources and a plan. A man entering the modern world with nothing has almost no such opponents. His obstacles are indifference, illegibility, sorting mechanisms, and his own limited competence. Modelling these as an enemy produces a false picture — it suggests someone is opposing him who could be outmanoeuvred, when in fact nobody is paying enough attention to oppose him. The business literature's core failure is exactly this import, and it manufactures resentment in place of strategy.

**"Win without fighting" (III.2) does not transfer to him.** In the text it is a doctrine of overwhelming latent power: resistance is pointless, so it does not occur. Absent the power, "winning without fighting" is a euphemism for not competing. He does not have the power. He will have to do the unglamorous version — compete, in a narrow place, for a long time.

**Desperate ground (XI.23–25, XI.39, XI.58) does not transfer, and it contradicts the part that does.** Throwing troops where there is no escape (XI.23), burning the boats and breaking the cooking-pots (XI.39) — this is a general deliberately removing *other men's* options to extract performance from them. Self-applied it becomes the burn-the-ships motivational cliché, and it directly contradicts IV.1, which says to make yourself unbeatable first. The two doctrines are consistent in the book only because they address different parties: the general preserves his own freedom of action while destroying his soldiers'. A man with nothing who removes his own fallback is not following Sun Tzu; he is being the soldier instead of the general.

**The forecasting method (I.3–14) transfers only as a negative test.** Sun Tzu's five factors and seven comparisons require measurable quantities on both sides. A man entering with nothing usually cannot measure the relevant quantities — he does not know the field's real standards, who the actual competitors are, or what the work is worth. The honest use is diagnostic: ask what you would need to know to forecast this, notice you cannot answer, and treat that as information about how early you are rather than as a reason to proceed on feeling.

The net. The book will not make him formidable. Read honestly, it tells him: spend nothing he cannot lose, refuse contests decided by resources he lacks, narrow his front until concentration is possible, keep his fixed costs low enough to survive being wrong, expect the correct move to look like nothing and earn no credit, watch his temper and his pride because those are what will actually kill him, and wait for an opening that the other side will eventually provide. That is a small, slow, defensive doctrine. It is also, for a man with nothing, very close to correct — and the reason the self-help tradition rewrites it is that in its accurate form nobody wants to buy it.

**Method.** Both Sun Tzu files were flattened with `tr -d '\r' < FILE | tr '\n' ' ' | tr -s ' '` (the CRLF trap the extractor documents is real; without the `-d '\r'` step the joins fail silently). Loci were checked by parsing the bare translation into chapter-and-paragraph units and printing the cited paragraph, not by searching for the quoted words — which catches a right quotation under a wrong number.

**File identity, re-confirmed.** `suntzu_giles.txt` carries `Release date: May 1, 1994 [eBook #132]` and contains 103 occurrences of "Tu Mu": it is the commentary edition. `suntzu_giles_commentary.txt` carries `Release date: December 28, 2005 [eBook #17405]`, contains zero occurrences of "Tu Mu", and itself notes that the commentary edition "has been released as Project Gutenberg's eBook #132": it is the bare translation. The filenames are inverted relative to their contents, exactly as section 9 says.

**Quotations (section 7): 18 of 18 verified.** Nos. 1–15 grep-hit in eBook 17405; nos. 16–18 in eBook 132. Every locus was additionally checked against the numbered paragraph: I.18, I.26, II.6, II.19, III.2, III.9, III.18, IV.2, IV.12, VI.18, VI.32, VIII.11, XII.17, XII.21, XIII.5 — all correct as cited. **Principles (section 3): all twenty checked against the cited loci, not six.** Every locus in every principle was opened and read. All twenty are in the text at the paragraphs given. Specific confirmations worth recording: principle 2's five constants (I.4) and seven considerations (I.13) are as stated, and IV.17–18's causal chain runs measurement → estimation → calculation → balancing of chances → victory exactly as described; principle 9's Zhang Yu limit is verbatim in Giles's note to III.9 ("the saying only applies if the other factors are equal; a small difference in numbers is often more than counterbalanced by superior energy and discipline"); principle 20's three qualities are XIII.16–17 and the death penalty for a premature leak is XIII.19. Two paraphrase errors were found and fixed: principle 3's "flatter the arrogant" (I.22 says pretend weakness so that he grows arrogant), and principle 12's attribution of "counsel of perfection" to Du Mu.

**Section 6 critics: all real, none invented.** Ye Shi (Yeh Shui-hsin) — all four quoted fragments verified in Giles's Introduction, including the *Zuo Zhuan* silence, the "vulgar plebeians and hireling ruffians," the "production of some private scholar," the "reckless fabrication of theorizing pundits," and the concubine story as "utterly preposterous and incredible." Chen Zhensun (Ch'en Chen-sun) — present, and Giles's characterisation is right: he and Ye Shi reject the person while accepting the date. Zhu Xi (Chu Hsi) — verified, in "Appreciations of Sun Tzŭ." Du Mu's "Practice benevolence and justice, but on the other hand make full use of artifice and measures of expediency" — verified. Giles's own chronological objection — verified verbatim, including his claim of priority ("which, so far as I am aware, nobody has yet pointed out"), his two contemporary allusions (VI.21 and XI.30), and the load-bearing sentence "Now Ch'u is not mentioned in the 13 chapters at all." Johnston, Handel, Mair — all real books with the positions ascribed, verified against publisher pages; all three remain report, not text. The five false attributions were re-grepped against both files: zero hits in each, in both files.

**Unresolved.** Nothing in section 7 depends on any of these.

1. The origin of three of the popular misattributions ("body of your enemy float by," "in the midst of chaos," "every battle is won before it is ever fought") is unestablished. Each is verified *not* to be Sun Tzu, which is all section 6 now claims.
2. Griffith (1963), Ames (1993), Sawyer (1994) and Mair (2007) remain unobtained — in copyright, not in the cache. Every claim attributed to them is by report from a named source given at the point of use, and none is quoted.
3. The content of Liddell Hart's foreword to Griffith is unread. Its existence is catalogue-verified; the "indirect approach" reading is not.
4. Handel's and Johnston's theses are from publisher pages, not from the books.
5. The conventional figure of ~6,000 Chinese characters for the received text is repeated here from common report and is not independently checked; the English word count is.
6. Giles's Chinese romanisation is Wade-Giles; this file uses pinyin in its own prose and Wade-Giles inside quotations. That is deliberate, but it means a reader grepping the sources for "Zhang Yu" or "Zhu Xi" will find nothing — search for "Chang Yu" and "Chu Hsi."
